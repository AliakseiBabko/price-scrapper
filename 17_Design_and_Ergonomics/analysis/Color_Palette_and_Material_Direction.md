# Color Palette & Material Direction

## Scope

General color-theory and material-pairing technique gathered from processed sources: how to choose a coherent palette, warm-vs-cool balance, accent-vs-neutral pairing rules, how material choice (wood tone, stone/quartz type, metal finish) interacts with color choice. This is general knowledge/technique, not this project's own chosen palette — see `00_Master/Design_Concept.md` for that (currently also unfilled).

## Color and Surface Technique for a Low-Natural-Light Room

First real source for this page: ARCHWOOD/Marina Izmailova's direct
5-technique explainer for a room with genuine but weak/indirect daylight
(north exposure, a shadowing neighbor building, or a tree blocking the
window) — each technique grounded in a stated physical mechanism, not
just asserted preference.

- **A pale color reads duller and can visibly shift hue in low light** —
  shadow pulls any light tone toward gray, and a nearby colored object
  (a tree, a bright neighboring facade) can tint that gray toward its
  own hue. Compensate by choosing noticeably more saturated paint and
  upholstery colors than for an equivalent sunlit room.
- **Avoid pale intermediate tones (light gray, light beige, light blue)
  specifically in low-light rooms** — they read as dingy/dirty white
  rather than the intended pastel, since there isn't enough light for
  accurate color perception; a gentle pastel palette with subtle tone
  transitions reads worse under low natural light, not better.
- **Use the brightest white in a paint line (or one shade brighter than
  usual) for a dark room**, paired with saturated accents (turquoise,
  purple) for contrast, rather than the muted warm white preferred for a
  sunlit room. A muted palette, if still wanted, should come from white
  contrast *accents* (piped cushions, a white photo-frame wall
  arrangement) rather than from the wall paint itself.
- **Never use a mirrored or glossy ceiling in a dark room** — a poorly-
  lit gray ceiling just reflects more darkness, and the resulting
  visually-taller ceiling vs. actual floor-area mismatch creates a
  claustrophobic "well" effect.
- **Only place reflective/mirrored surfaces where real light already
  reaches** — a mirrored wardrobe door facing away from the window, in
  the darkest corner, only reflects darkness back into the room.
- **"Mirror window reveal" technique**: mirror insert panels on the left
  and right window reveals (not a full mirror frame including the sill)
  make the window appear to extend further by reflecting the opening
  itself; curtains should slightly overlap the window-opening edge for
  the illusion to read correctly.
- **Avoid fully covering a small window even with sheer tulle** — tulle
  still absorbs a meaningful fraction of already-scarce light; keep the
  window uncovered by day where psychologically comfortable, or combine
  multiple curtain types for different times of day instead of one
  fixed treatment.
- **Matte paint reads dull/flat in an already-dark room; a matte dark-
  toned volume in the room's far corner becomes one large uniform dark
  blob.** Glossy paint reflects maximum light but reads "vulgar" if
  overused — use it sparingly, in small details, regardless of the
  room's actual brightness. **Semi-matte/eggshell is the recommended
  middle ground for a dark room's walls** — absorbs less light than
  full matte without mirroring like gloss does in a dark corner.
- **Furniture with visible legs, glass-top tables, and generally
  "lighter"-reading pieces reduce visible cast shadow** in a dark room.
  Avoid velvet/velour upholstery (needs visible light-play to look
  good; better suited to bright rooms) and avoid large dark-colored
  furniture pieces (dark bedspreads, dark beds/sofas/wardrobes) — a
  dark-toned volume reads even darker once placed in a low-light space.

## Color Psychology — a Practitioner's Decision Framework (added 2026-08-25, Sergey Kodolov)

**⚠️ All claims below are `unverified` pop-psychology/practitioner
framing, not clinical or scientifically validated findings** — recorded
as one practitioner's stated design-decision heuristic, not adopted as
established fact.

- **⚠️ Three-tier priority order for choosing color, the most durable
  item here**: (1) general color-psychology association, then (2)
  cultural/regional association specific to the client's own
  country/culture (white = wedding/purity in Russia/CIS vs. mourning in
  India), then (3) the client's own personal history with a color (a
  personal trauma, or a disliked figure's signature color, can override
  any "objective" color-theory argument). **Itten's color wheel** is
  named as the standard tool for checking which specific hues pair
  harmoniously, rather than combining colors by unstructured taste.
- **Two color-appetite personality patterns, independent of any specific
  color**: some people deliberately choose muted/desaturated colors to
  signal seriousness — sometimes genuine preference, sometimes actually
  fear-of-color misread as preference, worth surfacing in a trust-based
  client conversation. Others (self-described "creative" personalities)
  actively dislike muted environments and prefer living "inside color."
- **Per-color associations (practitioner framing, `unverified`)**:
  white (enlarges small rooms, can feel cold/institutional in full-
  apartment use — mitigate with wood/textile/decor accents); pastels
  (non-irritating background tone, but show stains visibly); beige
  (calming/sleep-promoting, but stuffy if monochrome — cut with cooler
  accents; associated with comfort-zone avoidance and reduced future-
  orientation); acid/saturated colors (energizing, used in gyms/kids'
  rooms/food courts by convention); red (passion/urgency/appetite,
  short-term energy boost with a "comedown" — better for a task zone
  than a whole room); light blue (calming, but overuse linked to
  status-comparison/snobbery framing); yellow (stimulant, suited to
  living/kitchen not bedroom, softer mustard tones less overstimulating);
  brown (associated with hoarding/material-stability psychology,
  clutters a small room, suits someone wanting calm after chaos);
  orange (stimulates appetite — a real caution for a kitchen if weight
  management matters; warm tones read psychologically "warmer," useful
  for a cold-exposure room); green (calming, lowers perceived heart
  rate, dulls appetite, but dark green reads depressive; carries a
  CIS-cultural envy/poison association); dark/saturated blue (pulls
  inward, associated with empathy/family in moderation, easy to
  "drown in" if overused); violet (creativity/introspection, discouraged
  if the goal is expanding social reach); gray (formal/composed,
  suited to someone building self-discipline); pink (culturally read as
  frivolous by default, but a genuine boldness signal deliberately used;
  light pink anxiety-reducing but can read as infantilizing); black
  (psychologically heavy with extended exposure, but genuinely effective
  for deliberate intimacy/coziness in a large room — carries opposite
  cultural readings by context, elegance/status vs. mourning/fear).

## Color Proportion and Contrast Rules (Kruglov/Ontario, Round 14 triage, added 2026-08-28)

A distinct, complementary framework from the per-hue Color Psychology section above — not merged or reconciled with it, since this is a proportion/contrast methodology rather than a per-color-association one:

- **⚠️ 60-30-10 color-proportion rule**: 60% of a room's color should be the base fill (walls, floor), 30% mid-weight elements (furniture, curtains), and only 10% genuinely accent colors (decor, light fixtures, chairs). **The accent color must repeat at least 3 times** across distinct objects (e.g. a painting, a cushion, a vase all sharing one accent tone) — a single accent instance doesn't read as a deliberate choice.
- **⚠️ Three-criterion contrast rule for accents**: an accent color must differ from the base along at least one of three axes — **hue** (a different color outright), **temperature** (warmer or cooler), or **material/texture** (wood vs. metal vs. stone, etc.). If unsure about color contrast specifically, temperature or material contrast is the safer default.
- **Named warm/cool palette pairings**: a warm base (beige, sand, cream, terracotta, warm gray) pairs best with deep blue, olive or forest green, black, brass, or gold; a cool base (concrete gray, cold white, graphite) pairs best with terracotta, caramel, cognac tones, warm wood, bronze, or warm green.
