# Colour Psychology — a Decision Framework, and Whether It Is Usable at All

One practitioner's per-hue colour-psychology decision framework, immediately followed by the recorded disagreement over whether per-hue colour psychology is usable at all. **Split out on 2026-09-02 under the vault's 300-line page ceiling, verbatim.**

> [!WARNING]

## Color Psychology — a Practitioner's Decision Framework (added 2026-08-25, Sergey Kodolov)

**⚠️ All claims below are `unverified` pop-psychology/practitioner
framing, not clinical or scientifically validated findings** — recorded
as one practitioner's stated design-decision heuristic, not adopted as
established fact.

- **⚠️ Three-tier priority order for choosing color, the most durable
  item here**: (1) general color-psychology association, then (2)
  cultural/regional association specific to the client's own
  country/culture (white = wedding/purity in Russia/CIS vs. mourning in
  India), then (3) the client's own personal history with a color (a
  personal trauma, or a disliked figure's signature color, can override
  any "objective" color-theory argument). **Itten's color wheel** is
  named as the standard tool for checking which specific hues pair
  harmoniously, rather than combining colors by unstructured taste.
- **Two color-appetite personality patterns, independent of any specific
  color**: some people deliberately choose muted/desaturated colors to
  signal seriousness — sometimes genuine preference, sometimes actually
  fear-of-color misread as preference, worth surfacing in a trust-based
  client conversation. Others (self-described "creative" personalities)
  actively dislike muted environments and prefer living "inside color."
- **Per-color associations (practitioner framing, `unverified`)**:
  white (enlarges small rooms, can feel cold/institutional in full-
  apartment use — mitigate with wood/textile/decor accents); pastels
  (non-irritating background tone, but show stains visibly); beige
  (calming/sleep-promoting, but stuffy if monochrome — cut with cooler
  accents; associated with comfort-zone avoidance and reduced future-
  orientation); acid/saturated colors (energizing, used in gyms/kids'
  rooms/food courts by convention); red (passion/urgency/appetite,
  short-term energy boost with a "comedown" — better for a task zone
  than a whole room); light blue (calming, but overuse linked to
  status-comparison/snobbery framing); yellow (stimulant, suited to
  living/kitchen not bedroom, softer mustard tones less overstimulating);
  brown (associated with hoarding/material-stability psychology,
  clutters a small room, suits someone wanting calm after chaos);
  orange (stimulates appetite — a real caution for a kitchen if weight
  management matters; warm tones read psychologically "warmer," useful
  for a cold-exposure room); green (calming, lowers perceived heart
  rate, dulls appetite, but dark green reads depressive; carries a
  CIS-cultural envy/poison association); dark/saturated blue (pulls
  inward, associated with empathy/family in moderation, easy to
  "drown in" if overused); violet (creativity/introspection, discouraged
  if the goal is expanding social reach); gray (formal/composed,
  suited to someone building self-discipline); pink (culturally read as
  frivolous by default, but a genuine boldness signal deliberately used;
  light pink anxiety-reducing but can read as infantilizing); black
  (psychologically heavy with extended exposure, but genuinely effective
  for deliberate intimacy/coziness in a large room — carries opposite
  cultural readings by context, elegance/status vs. mourning/fear).

## Perspectives on Record — Is Per-Hue Colour Psychology Usable at All? (added 2026-08-30)

The section above records one practitioner's per-hue association table. A second practitioner, processed 2026-08-30, argues against exactly that genre — so the two are placed side by side rather than merged.

| Source | Position | Stated reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Sergey Kodolov** (the section above) | A per-hue association table is a usable decision aid, **applied in a three-tier priority order**: general colour-psychology association, then the client's own cultural/regional association, then their personal history with the colour | Personal history can override any "objective" argument, so the table is a starting point that gets overridden, not a lookup |
| **Надежда Кузина** | Treats universal per-hue advice **with explicit caution** — "оливковый успокаивает," "оранжевый бодрит," "бежевый делает интерьер дорогим" are the examples she names and declines to rely on | The same colour produces different emotions in different people for **biographical** rather than perceptual reasons. Her worked instance: the same olive is a trip through Tuscany for one person, a district clinic's walls for another, a grandfather's jacket for a third |

**Common ground, and it is closer than it first looks.** Kodolov's own third tier *is* Кузина's objection — he already states that a client's personal history with a colour can override any general argument. **The real disagreement is about which tier leads.** Kodolov starts from the general association and lets personal history override it; Кузина starts from the person and treats the general association as unreliable enough not to lead with. She reaches the same place from the historical side too: her Pastoureau argument (on [[17_Design_and_Ergonomics/analysis/Color_Selection_Process_and_Testing|Colour Selection Process & Testing]]) is that colour associations have already reversed once — medieval red+green read as near-monochrome, yellow+green as hellish kitsch — so they are cultural conventions rather than constants.

Both agree that **cultural/regional association is real and must be asked about**, and both treat the client conversation, not a table, as where the answer actually comes from.

**Your priority.** Not yet decided. For this project the practical consequence is small and concrete: **the per-hue table above is a conversation prompt, not a specification** — the deciding input either way is what the household actually associates with a colour.
