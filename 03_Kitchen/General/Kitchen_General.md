## Large Open Kitchen-Living Layout: Split Function Instead of One Continuous Run (added 2026-08-19)

> [!NOTE]
> First real content for this page. `single-account`, real 109m² project. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_BUfY5YcnN-U_large_kitchen_layout_island_vs_split_001|note]]]

On a sufficiently large open kitchen-living space, **don't default to one continuous kitchen run** (an L-shape with a separate island, or a U-shape with a bar-counter "tongue") — **splitting distinct functions into physically separate modules along different walls can solve circulation, furniture-placement, and corner-protection problems that a single continuous layout can't solve all at once.** A worked example, reasoning by elimination across three candidate layouts:

- **Island, rejected for four independent reasons**: a sink can't be added without an infeasible floor-level raise for gravity drainage (a sump/macerator pump was ruled out as too noisy for a living-room-adjacent space); a cooktop spreads odors through the whole open-plan room and forces a hood low enough to conflict with the island's own task lighting; the island creates an exposed corner that gets scuffed in normal use, while also leaving no good sofa or dining-table placement; and — undermining the original point of adding it — a cabinet-based island (unlike a furniture table on legs) has no legroom underneath, making it impractical for actual seated dining beyond a quick snack.
- **U-shape with a bar "tongue," rejected**: the tongue blocks convenient access to the cabinet module behind it, forces a ~4.5m round-trip detour to reach the far side of the kitchen for routine use, and — the decisive problem — leaves no viable sofa placement at all.
- **Chosen solution — split the L-shaped cooking/prep section and a separate bar-counter module onto different walls**: gives direct, unobstructed access to every module (no detour), enough dedicated floor space for a real dining table with legroom, a sofa that fits comfortably alongside the bar module facing the TV, and — as a bonus, not the original design goal — the bar module's own footprint happens to shield the room's one exposed exterior corner.

**Reusable takeaway**: a wide open-plan kitchen-living space doesn't need to force every function (cooking, casual bar seating, dining, living-room seating) into one shared kitchen run. Splitting cooking/prep from casual bar seating into two separate modules is worth considering before defaulting to an island or a bar-tongue extension, especially where corner-protection or circulation is also a concern.

## Why Not to Place a Kitchen at a Window (added 2026-08-19, chunk 4)

> [!NOTE]
> A placement/site-selection checklist — complements the layout-configuration content above (that's about *how* to lay a kitchen out once placed; this is about *where not* to place one at all). Built from 10 years of collected client feedback, several points illustrated with direct client quotes (`single-account` testimonial evidence). [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_qzfABiffDB8_never_kitchen_near_window_144|note]]]

Despite the intuitive appeal ("cooking with a view," natural light), a window-adjacent kitchen run carries eight independent, mechanism-explained downsides:

1. **Sink drainage**: stub-outs are always positioned as far from the window as practical (opposite wall) — placing the sink at the window forces either a long, slow-draining run or a sump/macerator pump, which brings its own problems (unpredictable audible hum, food-debris clogging, multi-day sewage odor during outages).
2. **Cooktop/hood incompatibility**: no wall/ceiling mounting position for a hood exists at a window wall without blocking light; worse, opening the window while cooking pulls outdoor air *inward* toward the cook, pushing kitchen odors away from the window and out through the front door instead — the opposite of the intended venting effect.
3. **Loss of upper-cabinet storage** on an already-narrow window-wall run — open shelving used as a substitute collects dust and can't hold bulkier equipment (food processors, meat grinders), forcing storage elsewhere and repeat retrieval trips.
4. **Fridge door-swing conflict**: a fridge forced into a window-adjacent side corner can have its door blocked from opening a full 90° by the adjacent wall, in turn blocking drawers behind that door — a real-world fix required rehanging the door to the opposite hinge (a new door panel) and still needing to walk around/press against the wall to open it.
5. **Eye pupil-adaptation/glare, a genuine physiological argument**: extended time at a bright window constricts the pupil; moving to a dimmer part of the kitchen needs ~1 minute of readaptation, during which cutting/handling hot items carries a real elevated injury risk (cuts, burns) — plus ongoing eye strain from repeated constriction/dilation cycling.
6. **Window/blind operation blocked** by a countertop run directly under the window, forcing the occupant to climb onto the counter to reach either — worked example: an east-facing window's morning glare forced blinds to stay permanently closed rather than repeatedly climbing up, and even then needed periodic climbing to clean grease/soot buildup.
7. **Task-lighting conflict at night**: a window-adjacent cook zone still needs mandatory evening task lighting (daylight only covers part of the day) — a later-retrofitted ceiling pendant made things worse, reflecting directly in the dark window glass and creating glare in the cook's eyes.
8. **Radiator/condensation mechanism**: kitchen humidity (cooking steam) makes its window more condensation-prone than elsewhere, but ~60cm of kitchen cabinetry blocks the radiator that would normally offset this — a real before/after case saw a window that never condensed during the (humid) renovation phase begin streaming condensation and warping the countertop once kitchen furniture went in front of the radiator; adding a drainage grille stopped the streaming but the room stayed noticeably cold, since even a powerful radiator can't project heat effectively through a wall of cabinetry.
