## ⚠️ Perspectives on Record

Three genuine disagreements between named sources. Each is set out in full on its detail page; they are surfaced here so a reader does not discover mid-page that a decision is still open.

- **⚠️ Worktop height — two Russian-market practitioners disagree on what "domestic standard" means.** Architect Виталий Злобин gives a domestic standard of **80–85 cm** against an international **90–93 cm**, and warns that built-in appliances and hardware are sized to one or the other. LA BURO, a turnkey Moscow bureau, states a flat **90 cm** — the top of Злобин's domestic range and close to his international figure. **Common ground: both agree the figure should scale up for a taller household** (LA BURO says 95–100 cm), and both treat it as something to fix *before* ordering. **Your priority: not yet decided — the deciding factor both point to is the household's own height, tested in a showroom rather than chosen from a table.** Full detail on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Layout_Sizing_and_Ergonomics|Layout, Cabinet Sizing & Ergonomics]].
- **⚠️ Corner kitchens — a workable default, or a configuration to avoid?** This vault's existing technique treats a corner layout as fine when the dead corner is handled with a 60×60 cm technical column. Иван Безруков / GRADIZ calls the L-shaped kitchen one of the least-favoured configurations — reading as "stacked boxes," with carousel corner mechanisms both inconvenient and expensive — and offers a linear run plus a separate dresser instead. **Common ground: the corner itself is the problem, and its mechanisms are where the cost sits.** They differ on whether to solve it or design around it.
- **⚠️ Upper cabinets — omit them, or treat that as a large-house luxury?** Existing content treats the no-upper-cabinets look as suitable only where a separate working kitchen absorbs the storage. Игорь Краснов reports his own kitchen has none at all, and separately notes upper cabinets are often never functionally used. **Recorded side by side rather than merged: his personal exception does not override the general guidance, and both are on the record.**

## Layout, cabinet sizing and ergonomics

**Default every module to a standard width (40/50/60 cm) and absorb any leftover in a filler strip rather than a custom-sized cabinet** — non-standard widths carry a real cost premium and integrate less reliably with built-in appliances sized to standard modules. The figures that follow from that: base run depth **60 cm**, upper cabinets **~55 cm above the worktop**, **~60 cm** tall and **35 cm** deep (deeper is possible and explicitly not recommended). Worktop height is the one genuinely contested figure — see the Perspectives block above.

**The ergonomic argument that most changes a specification is the toe-kick.** The fashionable no-toe-kick "floating" look costs a **~80 mm recess** that lets feet tuck under the cabinet, keeping the body's centre of gravity stable while standing at the counter; without it a cook stands further back and leans in, with fatigue reported within **5–10 minutes**. It also exposes the lower front edge to splashes and foot impact, and removes the service void that commonly hides outlets and a dishwasher drain. The recommended compromise is a **5–10 cm recess painted to match the floor or front**.

**On islands, the entry condition is area, not desire**: an island suits kitchens of **≥14 m²**, needs **≥80 cm clearance on every side** bar one, and a **minimum 1 m width** — and if it carries neither hob nor sink, it is an island for its own sake. For seated use leave a **35–40 cm knee recess**; splitting a 1 m island into back-to-back storage eliminates knee space entirely.

## Appliance integration

**Cluster the plumbing-dependent appliances near the riser and put the tall column at the far end** — sink, dishwasher and washing machine share a drain run, while a fridge and an oven column need neither. **Site the tall unit at the end nearest the window**, not mid-run: a tall unit standing where the prep zone would be casts a shadow across exactly the surface that most needs light, so it "sacrifices" the position that cares least.

**Two appliance decisions have non-obvious consequences.** Which side the dishwasher sits on follows from *which hand holds the plate* while scraping — for a right-handed user the dishwasher belongs to the sink's left, so the free hand loads without an awkward twist. And **avoid white built-in appliances**: model choice is far narrower than black across every brand and tier, and white shading genuinely differs between manufacturers — and even between two appliance types from the same brand made in different factories.

## Worktops and backsplash

**If the budget forces a choice, put the money in the worktop rather than the fronts.** This is the single most repeated finding in this folder, stated independently by a designer and by a fabricator who notes it costs him margin to say so: simpler ЛДСП or laminate fronts under a stone worktop read far better than expensive veneer or enamel fronts under a plastic one, which "kills the design instantly." Quartz agglomerate at **20–30 mm** is the working range, with a **front-edge chamfer** making a thick slab read thinner while staying structurally thick (that figure is a fabricator's, on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/General_Dos_and_Donts|General Do's and Don'ts]]).

**The overhang question is settled**: **25–30 mm, consistent along the run** — less exposes raw door edges, more blocks upper-drawer opening on handleless fronts. A documented experiment on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Worktops_and_Backsplash|Worktops & Backsplash]] tested this against the cabinetmakers' own argument and confirmed it twice over.

## Storage, hardware and mechanisms

**Default to drawers over hinged doors for lower storage**, and the reasoning is retrieval rather than capacity: a drawer presents everything at once, where a door-and-shelf cabinet requires reaching past or unloading the front items to reach the back. The configuration to avoid outright is **a drawer nested behind a hinged door** — it needs two motions to open and the drawer scuffs the door's inner face over time.

**Never pair a cheap substrate with cheap hardware.** Cheap hinges cannot be adjusted to set facade gaps in all planes, so the aesthetic result is unobtainable no matter what was spent elsewhere — and, worse, a cheap hinge on self-tappers gradually chews the carcass side into powder, at which point the repair is a side panel rather than a hinge. Against that: a fabricator's own kitchen, built with inexpensive fronts on premium hardware, ran **three years with no gap adjustment at all**. Both the failure mechanism and that field observation are on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Furniture_Facade_Materials|Facade Materials]], alongside the hardware cycle ratings. **Corner mechanisms are where this bites hardest — a cheap corner basket is worse than a plain shelf** ([[03_Kitchen/analysis/General_Dos_and_Donts|General Do's and Don'ts]]).

## Cabinet and countertop assembly

**Install in order and level before the worktop goes on.** Lower modules are assembled, levelled and joined — with the countertop fixing rails checked — *before* uppers or the worktop; working out of order forces rework at every later stage. Level with a laser in both height and depth, because modules out of the front-face plane leave fronts in visibly different planes and rails at mismatched heights, and both need a teardown to correct.

**The one irreversible step is the worktop joint.** A continuous sealant bead of **≥3 mm** along the full joining plate, pressed until squeeze-out is visible — no squeeze-out means too little sealant — and excess wiped only while wet. Skimp here and the worktop and the cabinet beneath it swell from water ingress.

## Kitchen lighting

**Task light over the prep zone must be diffuse, not a directional spot**, and the mechanism is the point: any directional source casts the cook's own head and hands onto the work surface as they lean in. A fabricator illustrates it first-hand — his own head shadowed the counter, so a second light line was added specifically to fill it. **Position the profile at the facade edge rather than the corner**: cornered, it merely washes the backsplash and lights the worktop unevenly — corroborated from two directions, the facade-edge mounting rule on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Kitchen_Lighting|Kitchen Lighting]] and a fabricator's mid-depth/100 mm-recess figures on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/General_Dos_and_Donts|General Do's and Don'ts]].

Whole-apartment lighting design, colour temperature and the same-scene matching rule live on [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Lighting_Design|Lighting Design]]; this page covers only what is specific to a kitchen.

## Cost, priorities and what to cut

**The durable principle across every source here: economise on what can be replaced later, never on what is buried or hard to change.** In kitchen terms that means fronts, decor and lighting fittings are legitimate savings, while hardware, the worktop and anything requiring a services change are not. The counter-intuitive corollary from a manufacturer: **the same visual result is often reachable at roughly half the price** by choosing the laminate decor that matches the veneer tone rather than the veneer itself — provided the colours are chosen deliberately and the hardware is untouched. He demonstrates it with samples in hand on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Furniture_Facade_Materials|Facade Materials]].

**Specify the kitchen from your own cooking habits, not from a standard appliance set** — how much you cook, what, and how — because a kitchen that has to be replaced is not a saving ([[03_Kitchen/analysis/General_Dos_and_Donts|General Do's and Don'ts]]). Priced add-ons and the full cut/never-cut list are on [[03_Kitchen/analysis/Kitchen_Cost_and_Priorities|Cost, Priorities & What to Cut]].
