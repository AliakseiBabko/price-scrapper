## Real Replanning Cases

ИНТЕРЬЕР НАЛИЦО's featured household (a real 52m² two-room block-building
apartment, region unresolved) demolished the wall separating a small,
isolated Soviet-era-style entryway from the living room, converting to an
open "European-style" layout. Stated reasons: the isolated entryway was
too cramped to turn around in (the household has two dogs) and received
very little natural light. `single-account`, real project. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_YarbTzTfq6I_interior_nalitso_old_fund_replan_case|YT_YarbTzTfq6I]]]

Olga Kachanova's featured household (real 53m² apartment, individual-
project building, **West Moscow, 2011** — level-1 region confirmed) lives
with a more radical version: a prior owner relocated the kitchen entirely
out of its original zone and into the corridor/hallway, splitting the
original studio into two rooms. The current owner bought the apartment
already in this configuration — at a price matching a standard one-room
unit despite the listing describing "two rooms" — and deliberately valued
it, being "used to every square meter being used productively, no dead
corridors."

**The household's own retrospective critique is the single most
load-bearing planning lesson from this case, stated directly**: "if I
designed this myself, I'd put a proper entry storage/closet zone *first*,
then the kitchen zone — walk in, hang up your things, *then* proceed
further in." As built, the kitchen sits immediately at the entry with no
room for more than one person to dress/undress at a time — a 3-person
household takes turns. **General principle**: when a kitchen (or any
prep/bending-space function) sits directly at the entry, verify a genuine
shoe-off/coat-off transition zone still exists ahead of it — don't let
that function crowd it out entirely.

The "dirty entry zone next to food prep" worry was tested against 5 real
years of living in this layout: never actually a problem for this
household — but explicitly attributed to their own lifestyle (little
tracked-in dirt/mud), not a general reassurance that this layout is
always fine for any household. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uvUmZuXgLtU_kachanova_kitchen_in_hallway_case|YT_uvUmZuXgLtU]]]

A partial wall segment directly abutting/splitting a window in half — a
defect pattern the ИНТЕРЬЕР НАЛИЦО household describes as common across
neighboring units in the same block-building type — was removed during
their replanning: it blocked roughly half the window's light and was
structurally degrading from thermal expansion/contraction cycling. Worth
checking any developer-era partition wall abutting a window for both
light-blocking and structural degradation before deciding to keep it.

A Spain-based practitioner's own hallway renovation (added 2026-08-25;
Alicante, Spain — level 1, this project's first Spain/EUR-region source)
demolished a non-load-bearing partition wall (plus its door frame)
between the hallway and living room to combine the spaces, on the stated
principle "if a wall serves no function, it's not needed" — the same
underlying logic as the ИНТЕРЬЕР НАЛИЦО case above, independently arrived
at. He also demolished a ~20 cm dropped/suspended ceiling — identified as
a typical 1960s Spanish construction workaround (a straw-and-gypsum
suspended ceiling over a concrete-joist-and-brick deck, built specifically
to conceal electrical wiring rather than route it properly) — a genuine
regional-construction-history data point. A ceiling section near shared-
building wiring (including a neighboring apartment's cabling through an
electrical shaft) was deliberately left as a small visible "step" rather
than fully rebuilt, since fixing it properly would have required much
more demolition and rerouting shared infrastructure — **a generalizable
principle**: weigh the "blast radius" of a fully proper fix against
accepting a smaller visible compromise, especially near shared-building
systems. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dsBkqzhX08I_ispanskiy_remont_hallway_full_reno|YT_dsBkqzhX08I]]]

## Hallway Proportion-Control Techniques (Anuta Vlady)

An individual practicing designer, new channel, low promotional content —
the batch's most reasoning-dense single source on hallway proportions
specifically:

- **Flooring board-layout direction** changes a corridor's apparent
  proportions: boards laid lengthwise (parallel to long walls) read as
  longer/narrower (a wide "edge border" runs along both walls); laid
  crosswise, the same corridor reads as wider. Neither is objectively
  correct — pick by the effect wanted. Large floor/tile patterns read as
  more cramped; small patterns read as more open.
- **Vertical lines/elements visually raise ceiling height; horizontal
  lines shorten wall height and widen the room** — horizontal (paint
  bands, wall panels, moldings) generally suits a narrow corridor better.
- **Color-blocking to control apparent length**: a dark accent wall at
  the far end shortens perceived length. A dark ceiling reads as lower,
  avoiding a "well/shaft" feeling — but if ceiling height exceeds ~3m, a
  dark ceiling alone isn't enough; extend the dark color partway down
  adjoining walls too. Splitting a long corridor into color-blocked zones
  (rather than one uniform run) works the same way — align a color
  division with a genuine architectural feature (e.g. a locally
  lower-ceiling section) where possible, or use an unavoidable structural
  obstruction (a load-bearing beam) as the natural boundary, as in one
  real applied case from a friend's in-progress budget renovation.
- **Light-fixture mounting height reinforces a dark-ceiling illusion**:
  recessed spots keep the light source high (weakest effect); surface-
  mounted/flush fixtures lower it (stronger); pendant/hanging fixtures
  lower it furthest and put the upper wall/ceiling into shadow, hiding
  real height almost entirely in one real example shown.
- **A forward-sightline focal point** (accent wall/artwork straight
  ahead) matters more than side-wall treatment — people pay far less
  visual attention to side walls while walking through a pass-through
  corridor than to whatever's directly ahead. Avoid large-format artwork
  in a narrow corridor (no room to stand back and view it); prefer
  small-format pieces.
- **When a corridor segment sits directly opposite a doorless living-room
  entrance**, treat it as effectively part of that room's interior —
  share its decorative-lighting switch rather than adding a separate one.
  General rule: don't give a pass-through corridor's accent lighting its
  own switch by default.
- **Mirror orientation must match the direction you actually want to
  expand** — a full-length mirror on a corridor's long wall expands its
  *length*, which can make an already-long corridor feel endless if
  that's not the goal; orient/place a mirror to expand width instead when
  length is already the problem.

[source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT__6E5wt-PfqQ_anuta_vlady_narrow_hallway_design|YT__6E5wt-PfqQ]]]

## Narrow-Corridor Segmentation (Alla Krol)

Since a corridor's walls usually can't be physically moved, enter through
the widest available zone and widen it visually (mirrors, light tones);
then divide a long corridor into a **minimum of three visual segments**
so each reads closer to a square proportion rather than one uninterrupted
tunnel — a mirrored "narrow point" segment can bridge two wider segments.
This is a coarser-grained companion technique to Anuta Vlady's own
proportion-control rules above (color-blocking, flooring direction), not
a contradiction of them. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_C-db6yom-IM_alla_krol_entryway_wardrobe_no_clutter|YT_C-db6yom-IM]]]

## Furniture-and-Lighting Composition Zoning (Квартиранты, added 2026-08-25)

A different mechanism from the color/line proportion-control techniques
above: dividing a long, poorly-lit corridor into two zones using a
**dimensioned furniture-and-lighting composition** rather than color or
line tricks alone. Zone 1 (adjacent to the living-room doorway) is
designed to read as an extension of that room; Zone 2 reads as the
corridor/transition zone proper.

**A specific, symmetric layout method, stated as a worked design rule**:
centered directly opposite the living-room doorway — a large mirror
(centered on the doorway's own midline), a console/chest of drawers below
it (**160-180 cm long, 100 cm tall**), two table lamps on the console
(needing two low-height wall outlets flanking the mirror), and wall
sconces either side of the mirror wired to a switch near the entry door.
**The whole composition is centered on the doorway and made symmetric by
measurement, not by eye**: the width available on one side (constrained
by an existing wall/opening near the entry) is measured, then the *same
distance* is marked off on the other side — this single measurement
simultaneously sets both the console-zone width and the width of a
wardrobe built into the space beyond it. A directly reusable method for
centering an entry-facing furniture composition symmetrically when the
two sides of a hallway aren't naturally equal.

**The zone boundary is reinforced with a color change on top of the
furniture break**: the wardrobe and ceiling in the "corridor" zone
deliberately differ in color from the "room-extension" zone, so the two
zones read as distinct even with no physical wall between them — the same
"double down on the zone break" logic as the color-blocking techniques
above, implemented through furniture/ceiling color instead. A wall-mounted
picture with dedicated LED backlighting, placed opposite a bathroom door,
does double duty as decor and as general corridor illumination for an
under-lit spot. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_sAXC1hn8u9A_kvartiranty_hallway_wall_prep_electrical|YT_sAXC1hn8u9A]]]

## Zone-Divider Alternative to a Hard Wall

An open bookshelf used as a soft zone divider between an open entry and a
living room (instead of rebuilding a wall) doubles as zoning element and
book storage — a concrete alternative to a hard partition for defining an
open-plan entry/living boundary, from the same ИНТЕРЬЕР НАЛИЦО case above.
See also `11_Budget_and_Planning`'s cross-cutting "Room-Zoning Partition
Methods" topic area (13DS/Olesya, Diskill/Nadia Karmin) for more zoning
methods not specific to entryways.
