## Faux-Brick Wall from Gypsum Plaster (Sasha_Life_Vlog, one real project across 4 episodes)

A full DIY method for a textured, exposed-brick-look wall finish, built
entirely from gypsum plaster over a hand-drawn grid — no stencil, roller
pattern, or premade brick-panel product involved.

**Grid layout and plastering** (episode 1):

1. Prime the wall with a deep-penetration primer; let dry fully.
2. Mark horizontal lines **7.5 cm apart** across the whole wall (a 6.5 cm
   brick width + ~1 cm allowance for the "mortar joint" line/tape).
3. Mark vertical divisions for a **25 cm brick length** (+1 cm joint =
   26 cm), offset **13 cm** (half the brick length) between rows for a
   running-bond (staggered) pattern.
4. **Prime the wall a second time**, over the drawn grid — this fixes/
   seals the pencil lines so they don't smear once plaster goes on.
5. Apply **12 mm stationery/office tape** along every drawn line (not
   hardware-store masking tape) — this masks the future recessed
   "mortar joint" lines.
6. Apply gypsum plaster (or ceramic-tile adhesive — both were found
   workable in the source's own research) over the taped grid.
7. **The core, non-obvious technique insight**: apply the plaster
   *deliberately unevenly*, not smoothly — "the less even you apply it,
   the more beautiful it looks." An uneven, textured surface reads as
   convincing 3D brick; a smooth, leveled application actively works
   against the effect. This directly inverts normal plastering training
   and is the single most important detail in the method.
8. Remove the tape **while the plaster is still wet** to reveal clean
   mortar-joint lines; smooth sharp corners/edges with a damp cloth.
9. Let dry a couple of days before priming again and painting.

**Real cost in time, not money**: grid-drawing took a full day on its own;
plastering was "very dirty work — more cleanup than actual work," roughly
2 working days total for one wall. A genuinely low-skill-friendly
technique precisely because unevenness is the goal rather than a flaw —
worth flagging for a first-time DIYer wary of hand-plastering skill.
[source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_rmYOxoub5y0_sasha_life_vlog_brick_wall_gypsum_ep1|YT_rmYOxoub5y0]]]

**Sequencing/tooling refinements from a later wall section** (episode 9):
confirms and extends the "prime over the drawn grid" step (draw first,
then prime, specifically to prevent smearing). A **short (~1.5 m) spirit
level was too short** for a full wall run, forcing repeated repositioning
and reducing line accuracy — use a longer level/straightedge if available.
**Drawing lines around a wall corner/turn is harder than a straight run**
(no jig keeps the angle consistent through a turn). **Masking tape applied
too far ahead of plastering doesn't hold reliably** — apply it the same
morning as plastering, not the day before. A named primer product,
"Геркулес" ("Hercules") deep-penetration reinforcing primer, was used.
**Leaving an un-erased reference line from a previous session** let the
next session's grid-marking continue without re-measuring from scratch —
a reusable technique for any multi-day task needing session continuity.
Real time cost: ~3 hours to mark one wall section; total renovation
output on a typical day was only ~1 hour once household duties
(cleaning, laundry, childcare) are accounted for — a realistic time-budget
baseline for a self-managed project fit around normal life, not a
dedicated full-time renovation.
[source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uzUqoUG0-Xs_sasha_life_vlog_faux_brick_wall_prep_ep9|YT_uzUqoUG0-Xs]]]

**Painting/finishing the brick texture** (a later episode): a thicker,
more expensive paint covered surface imperfections meaningfully better
than a cheaper paint used on the first coat — a real, checkable
price-vs-coverage tradeoff for this specific rough-texture application.
**Painting sequence**: brush the recessed mortar-joint lines first, then
go over the brick faces with a **dry roller (no additional paint loaded)**
— this removes visible brush strokes and leaves a soft, cloud-like
("meringue"/"zephyr") texture, the mechanism that actually makes the
*paint* read as brick-like rather than flat. **Real failure mode**:
losing track of which brick has already been painted is easy if
distracted mid-wall, since the technique requires brick-by-brick painting
rather than broad strokes. A smaller roller than the one already owned
was deliberately bought, since it's easier to control brick-by-brick.
[source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dvlxMc4X9_0_sasha_life_vlog_faux_brick_paint_technique|YT_dvlxMc4X9_0]]]

## Door-Jamb (Откос) Plastering, Including a Real Technique Failure

Before this same faux-brick wall's install, the source needed to correct a
door jamb left uneven by the installers who fit the door — jamb finishing
is not automatically included in door installation and needs separate
planning. **A documented negative result**: she tried mounting a rigid
corner guide-profile (meant to serve as a plastering straightedge) using
**double-sided tape instead of screws**, since she lacked the tools/
ability to drill into the concrete wall alone. **This failed outright** —
the taped profile kept coming loose under any contact, and she abandoned
the guide-profile approach entirely, finishing the corner free-hand
instead. Working free-hand (without a guide) produced a visibly less even
result, especially large gaps/holes at the top of the jamb, which she
planned to fill once dry. **A genuine mitigating factor worth
generalizing**: since this jamb would be covered by the faux-brick
texture afterward, the precision bar for this substrate-prep step was
lower than it would be under a smooth/painted finish — match a
substrate's required precision to the finish that will actually sit on
top of it, rather than defaulting to "as even as possible" everywhere.
Real time cost: ~2 hours on one corner, "a whole day" for the jamb
overall, for a first-time solo DIYer.

## Full Hallway Plaster/Paint Sequence (Испанский ремонт / "Spanish Renovation," Alicante, Spain)

A real tradesperson's own full-hallway renovation gives the most complete
plaster-through-paint sequence in this batch, independent of the
Sasha_Life_Vlog project above:

- **Ceiling**: light-sand the base plaster → prime → let fully dry →
  apply one coat of ready-mixed finish putty → let dry → sand → apply a
  second coat. Two coats were sufficient for a smooth, paint-ready
  ceiling.
- **Walls — a deliberate texture decision, with explicit reasoning**: for
  a narrow, light-painted corridor specifically, *not* leaving walls
  perfectly smooth was the right call — "in a narrow corridor, any small
  scratch will show immediately" against a light, bright finish.
  **Technique**: a large long-nap roller loaded with finish putty, rolled
  to an even, irregular "cobweb" texture, in two coats, then very lightly
  hand-sanded to knock down protruding high points. **Directly reusable
  rule**: match a wall's smoothness/texture decision to the corridor's own
  light level and paint darkness — a bright, light-painted narrow hallway
  is exactly the case where deliberate texture (not glass-smooth) hides
  everyday wear better, echoing the same underlying logic as the faux-
  brick project above from a different angle (rough texture as a feature,
  not a flaw, in a high-wear pass-through space).
- **Priming before paint**: a concentrated primer, diluted by substrate
  porosity — his highly-absorbent gypsum-plaster surface needed a
  **deep-penetration concentrate at roughly 60-70% water dilution** to
  reduce paint consumption and bind sanding dust.
- **Painting**: brush corners/edges first, then roll the field. A
  **narrower roller was deliberately chosen over the widest available**
  for control, accepting slower coverage — "the wider the roller, the
  faster it goes, but I wanted to do this carefully." Two coats each for
  ceiling (white) and walls (gray, deep-matte vinyl), full dry between
  coats.
- **A regional materials-availability finding, worth flagging explicitly
  as the source's own first-hand opinion, not independently verified**:
  plaster guide-rails/beacons ("маяки") were "practically impossible to
  find in Spain" at the time (2021) — many local plasterers didn't know
  the tool/method existed, and Spanish walls are commonly wavy/uneven as
  a locally accepted norm. A long aluminum straightedge/screed tool
  ("правило"), standard in CIS-region plastering practice, was hard to
  source in Alicante ~1.5 years before filming (special-ordered from
  Valencia) but had begun appearing at Leroy Merlin in Alicante by the
  time of filming — the source attributes this to CIS/former-USSR
  builders gradually changing local technique and tool availability.
  Relevant context for anyone renovating in Spain expecting Russian/CIS-
  market plastering tools and technique to be locally available by
  default.

## Gypsum Wall-Panel Casting — Pre-Filled Cavities at Fixture Locations (Konstantin Kruglov / Ontario, added 2026-08-28, Round 16)

**⚠️ A cast gypsum wall panel (poured/molded in place, then finish-painted) should have its cavities pre-filled solid at every point where a fixture will later be surface-mounted** — specifically the intercom/entry-phone handset location and any switch/outlet faceplate zone. On a real installed example, these specific spots were cast as solid fill rather than left with the panel's normal internal void structure, so the final surface reads as one continuous, uninterrupted plane once the handset and faceplates are screwed on — avoiding a visible seam, hollow-sounding spot, or fastening weakness at those points. A planning step to specify *before* the panel is cast, not something correctable afterward. `single-account`, `unverified`.
