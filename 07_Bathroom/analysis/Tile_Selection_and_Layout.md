# Bathroom — Tile Selection, Sequencing & Layout

Covers tile size taxonomy, laying sequence, grout selection, corner treatment, porcelain-tile buying guidance, and the specific technique for avoiding ugly corner slivers. Part of [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Guide]].

## Size Taxonomy

A useful reference vocabulary: classic 20–60 cm; "narrow"/plank tile (short:long ratio ≥1:3, wood-look); "medium large-format" 80–140 cm; "large-format" 150–240 cm; "very large format" >240 cm; small-format mosaic/"кабанчик" (subway-style), on mesh sheets or loose.

## Sequencing

Lay and grout the floor tile **first**, including under the tub's footprint — set the tub *after* floor tiling, not before (see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Bathtub_and_Shower|Bathtub & Shower Selection]] for how the tub-to-wall-tile junction itself sequences).

## Grout Comparison

| Type | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Standard cement | Cheapest, easiest to repair (can dig out/re-grout anytime) | Absorbs moisture, can mold/mildew, darkens, can crumble |
| Improved cement (polymer/latex-additive) | Denser, less water absorption, holds color, much less mold | Still not fully waterproof |
| **Epoxy (two-component)** | Essentially waterproof, doesn't absorb dirt/grease/dye, mold-resistant, most durable — **the right choice for a tiled shower podium or shower-cabin base** (see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Bathtub_and_Shower|Bathtub & Shower Selection]] and [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]]) | **3–5× the cost** of cement grout (material + labor); risk of permanently marking textured/embossed tile if misapplied |

## Corner Treatment

**External corners are always 45°-mitered and should always use epoxy grout even if the rest of the room uses cement grout** — a slight tile shift at an external corner will pop cement grout out (insufficient bonding area). A plastic/metal corner-trim profile is the deprecated older alternative — "haven't seen it used in ~5-6 years," per the source. Internal corners are never mitered (tile butts with overlap) and are sealed only with elastic silicone sealant, never grout — install a foam backer rod behind the sealant first, which improves adhesion and sealant lifespan.

**A premium, labor-intensive corner-rounding technique exists** — miter tiles at 45° as usual, then further grind/chamfer the joint and grout it with a specialized process to produce a smooth, fully rounded, injury-safe edge. Described as extremely slow, precision hand-work (compared to a manicure) — **real cost cited: ~120,000 RUB for this detail alone on one project.** A luxury-tier finishing option, not a default recommendation, but worth knowing as a real cost data point if injury-safe rounded corners are a priority.

## Shower Podium / Curb / Valve Height

- **Shower podium can sometimes be skipped entirely**: with a sufficiently thick screed (14 cm in one cited case), the drain/trap can be recessed without raising the floor at all — check site conditions before assuming a podium is required (see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] for the general slope-constraint reasoning this depends on). Where a podium/curb is still needed, keep it low (~5 cm) — tall enough to register visually and prevent stubbed toes, but a taller podium is a worse trip/injury risk. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Shower curb height should account for handheld-shower/bucket use, not just an overhead fixed shower** — where a household member dumps water over themselves rather than only using an overhead fixture, plan for the resulting splash volume (worked example: ~20 L) with a correspondingly higher curb/threshold (~6–7 cm in that case) instead of a standard low curb. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_business_class_five_attributes_19385e7a.txt`]
- **Shower valve/spout height should be set per-occupant, not a fixed standard height** — measure the household's actual heights and factor in the specific shower-wand hose length so the height works for every household member, rather than defaulting to a generic standard. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Shower podium riser tile-matching technique**: cut the podium's riser faces from the same porcelain tile used on the floor (small wedge-shaped cuts to form the slope/drain) rather than substituting mosaic — mosaic reliably fails to match the main tile's exact look. Alternative shown: cut the same porcelain tile into small squares and mesh-mount it as custom-matched "mosaic" for the podium specifically, when a curved/stepped riser genuinely needs mosaic-scale pieces. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]

## Porcelain Tile Selection — Type, Format, Quality Parameters, and Pricing

From a tile-industry wholesaler guest-source. **Flag pricing and country-of-origin claims below as commercial framing** — the guest is affiliated with the host channel's own discount partnership, an explicit conflict of interest on exactly those two points; the quality-parameter vocabulary (calibre, rectification, surface finish, coloring depth) is more straightforwardly technical and reusable regardless of that bias.

- **Porcelain tile (керамогранит) has largely displaced ceramic tile (плитка) in the market** — thicker, heavier, stronger, supports larger formats without cracking, and is generally considered to look better; porcelain reportedly makes up ~90% of current retail inventory.
- **Format**: rectangular, square, or "plank" (an elongated rectangle, usually wood-look). **Walls**: rectangular or square. **Floors**: square or plank. Plank *can* go on a wall, but only as a single accent wall.
- **Size selection is a genuine tradeoff, not "bigger is always better"**: larger tile means fewer, less visible grout lines, but must be checked against (a) the size of any revision hatch and (b) material-waste common sense for the specific room. A 60×60cm revision hatch is a common standard size, so 60×30 or 60×60cm tile aligns cleanly with it. **Don't use large-format tile in a small room just to minimize grout lines**: laying oversized slabs (e.g. 90×120cm) in a room only slightly larger than that slab wastes roughly half the material to trim scrap. **General default sizes**: walls 60×30 or 60×60cm; floor 60×60cm; scale down to 45×45 or 40×40cm if the room is small; scale up to 75×75 or 90×90cm if it's large.
- **A second source's own current fixed default: 60×20cm** (with 60×20/120×60 as size variants) for bathroom walls/floors — minimizes grout lines, ages well over 10-15 years, priced acceptably at this format. `single-account`, this channel's own current standard, not a market-wide default. A practical hiring check tied to this size: a tradesperson tiling at 60×20/120×60cm scale needs both a manual tile cutter capable of cutting up to ~120cm and an electric wet saw — lacking either disqualifies them from the job. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]
- **Even quality tile shows visible joint-width variation at 45°-cut corners on large-format/thin-joint tile — a manufacturing-tolerance limitation, not evidence of poor installation**: factory calibre tolerance causes the joint to appear to widen then narrow around a mitered corner even with correctly-laid, quality tile. `unverified`, speaker's own claim. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt`]
- **Quality parameters worth knowing when comparing tile, independent of price**:
  - **Calibre**: batch-to-batch dimensional precision. **Lower calibre is better** — a high-calibre batch is physically difficult to lay with thin, even grout lines. **Rectified tile** (trimmed to exact size after firing) achieves near-zero calibre and enables thin, precise grout lines — most modern porcelain tile is now rectified.
  - **Surface finish**: matte, glossy/polished, satin (semi-gloss), structured/textured, or "lapato" (mixed matte+glossy). **Never use glossy tile on a floor** — slippery, and gloss visibly dulls in high-traffic patches over time. **Lapato tile only works in large spaces where several tiles are visible together at once** — in a small area, a single lapato tile just reads as a smudge.
  - **Coloring depth**: surface-only printed coloring vs. full-body/through-body coloring. **Use full-body-colored tile for floors specifically** — surface-only coloring wears through with foot traffic over time.
  - **"Perfectly flat" tile doesn't exist, and minor waviness isn't a defect** — even premium tile lines have some natural bow/twist/warp within a few millimeters. Only reject a batch for genuinely severe warping.
- **Pricing framing (commercial — see caveat above)**: don't set a fixed tile budget and shop down to it — tile lasts 20+ years, so a modest per-m² price increase amortizes to very little. The source's own recommended target band: **~20–30 EUR/m²** (center ~25 EUR/m²) for solid design/geometry; a second tier around **~70 EUR/m²** for genuine premium Italian/Spanish tile, recommended only for a property valued above roughly 100,000 EUR. **Treat this pricing guidance, and the source's claim that Russian-made porcelain has closed the gap with Italian/Spanish tile, as one vendor-affiliated opinion, not verified market data.**
- **Three practical buying tips (general technique, not commercial)**: (1) avoid "discontinued"/clearance tile even at a good price — often assembled from mismatched warehouse batches with calibre/color inconsistencies across the lot; (2) avoid pre-ordering foreign tile that doesn't physically exist yet (made-to-order) — quoted lead times reportedly stretch to 6-12 months in practice fairly often; (3) buy tile 1-2 months ahead of installation, and if storage on-site isn't practical, pay the retailer to hold it rather than risk the specific batch selling out or being discontinued.
- **Level, flush bathroom flooring (no threshold) needs genuinely water-immune flooring — per Zemskov's caution**: he calls a flush-with-the-apartment-floor look often non-compliant with regulations he doesn't name or cite (`unverified` claim) and a real leak-exposure risk — even with a leak sensor at every fixture, some water reaches the floor before the sensor trips. Only pair this look with genuinely water-immune flooring (he names a material transcribed as "скворцинил," almost certainly a mis-transcription of "кварцвинил"/quartz-vinyl — `ASR-uncertain` on the exact name).

## Tile Layout Pattern — Avoiding Ugly Corner Slivers

A concrete, step-by-step method — the difference between a bathroom that "looks like the tile was chosen to fit the room" and one with awkward narrow cut strips in the corners comes down to this technique, not tile selection.

**Three layout approaches exist**:
1. **"Ugly" layout**: start laying whole tiles from one corner and keep going until you run out of room — whatever's left over becomes a single cut piece in the far corner. Only acceptable if that leftover cut piece is wider than half a full tile; if it's narrower, this produces a visibly awkward sliver. **Recommendation: avoid this approach entirely, regardless of the leftover width.**
2. **"Village/rustic" layout**: same as above, but split the leftover between *both* corners symmetrically — produces two narrower (but matching) cut pieces instead of one. **The one legitimate use case**: when you specifically need a plumbing-access hatch/niche to land entirely on whole, uncut tiles and centering the layout would otherwise force the hatch onto a cut piece — use this layout for the section containing the hatch, sized so the hatch location gets full tiles. If a plumbing box is too narrow to naturally produce a whole-tile hatch location this way, **widen the box slightly rather than accept a bad hatch layout**.
3. **"Classic" layout — the correct default for everything else**: (1) calculate how many whole tile rows/columns fit across the surface; (2) remove one whole row/column from that count; (3) lay what's left starting from the center, working outward toward both edges. This guarantees both corner cut pieces end up wider than half a tile, and they'll be equal or near-equal in width. Costs marginally more material (roughly an extra couple of square meters on a typical bathroom) — a worthwhile tradeoff against a lifetime of looking at bad corners.

**Application nuances**:
- **Floor tile follows the same classic-layout logic** as walls.
- **In a shower, apply classic layout across the full visible room length.** In a bathtub zone, apply it across the *visible* section only (between the entry wall and the tub apron) — the narrow cut strip that results directly under the tub itself is fine to leave as-is, since it's permanently hidden once the tub is installed.
- **If a room has two zones of different visible length**, apply the classic layout to whichever zone stays fully visible after fixtures are installed — optimizing a zone that ends up hidden behind a sink or cabinet wastes the technique on the wrong wall.
- **If shrinking a utility box slightly (e.g., by ~15 cm) yields meaningfully more room for a larger sink elsewhere**, that tradeoff can be worth accepting a correspondingly larger visible cut piece near the box, breaking strict symmetry.
- **For continuous flooring across multiple adjoining rooms (avoiding a transition strip/threshold at doorways)**: keep each wall as a separately movable object during planning specifically so a wall can be nudged later without redoing the whole layout. **When a narrow cut piece shows up along a wall, move the wall itself, not the tile grid — always in multiples of 10mm.** Moving a wall consumes exactly one grout joint's worth of material at that wall, so both tiles on either side remain whole — a cut piece removed in one room by shifting its wall does **not** cause a new cut piece to appear in the adjoining room. (Sliding the *tile grid* instead risks exactly that knock-on problem.)
- **Corroborating defect case for the "classic/centered layout" rule above**: a documented QC critique flagged a small wet room where the layout wasn't centered from the room's own centerline, producing narrow scrap-width cut pieces at *both* edges simultaneously — exactly the failure mode this page's classic-layout technique is designed to avoid. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_cWRgenv4B40_full_disaster_30m_274|YT_cWRgenv4B40]]]
- **A practical priority order for checking/fixing narrow cut pieces across a whole apartment's tile layout**: (1) the main/central circulation space first; (2) other main rooms' bare walls; (3) transition/passage spaces, especially right at an entry doorway; (4) window walls; (5) only then bring furniture placement into the model and re-check everything; (6) finally, auxiliary/utility rooms.
- **Vertical tile orientation is discouraged in most cases** — even with a correct classic layout, vertical tiles still show long narrow cut strips (rather than the compact corner pieces horizontal tiles produce). Vertical orientation is described as only working well at very cheap or very expensive/bespoke tile tiers; horizontal is the right default for the large middle of the market.

## Patterned/Printed Tile Print-Quality Risk (added 2026-08-19, remainder-pool batch)

**Printed/patterned large-format porcelain tile (imitation stone/marble-look) can show visible print-quality inconsistency across pieces once installed at scale** — the same printed pattern rendered inconsistently (uneven tinting, and on some cut pieces the print missing entirely, showing bare clear glaze underneath). **Practical guidance**: keep tile selection simple/plain rather than heavily patterned in a room that already has other visual-interest elements (e.g. a rain-shower fixture) — simplicity reduces exposure to this print-defect risk and still reads as intentional. `single-account`, `unverified`.
