# Bathroom — Lighting & Electrical

Covers light-source planning, mirror lighting, toe-kick/accent lighting, the exhaust-fan switching rules, and outlets to plan for. Part of [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Guide]]. General electrical infrastructure (cable sizing, panel formulas) lives on [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Electrical_and_Lighting|Electrical & Lighting]] — this page is the bathroom-specific application.

## Lighting

Konstantin Kruglov / Ontario supports the two linked extraction-note claims (`YT_9dfEdjOewng...` and `YT_dJMsXYUyh7A...`).

- **Prefer a diffused, large-area "glowing ceiling" light source over a single bright spot fixture for general illumination** — bright/even enough to catch every cleaning-relevant detail, but soft enough not to be uncomfortable to look up into while lying in the tub.
- **Always run dedicated electrical supply to the mirror location for its own lighting, even if no general ceiling fixture would technically be "too dim" without it** — no amount of general room/ceiling light is ever sufficient for grooming or makeup at the mirror on its own; a mirror light doesn't need its own switch (usually built into the fixture itself), but it does need its own wiring run, planned during rough-in — retrofitting power to a mirror after walls are finished means fishing wire through a completed wall.
- **Mirror lighting must be front-facing** (illuminating the face directly), not light that just washes onto the surrounding tile as decor — front light is what actually lets you see skin detail while grooming; ceiling light alone leaves the face in shadow. Tradeoff: a front-light fixture's frame/profile is visibly present around the mirror.
- **⚠️ Mirror-light color temperature: 4000K specifically** (not this page's general-lighting 2700-3000K default) — this is the temperature that renders skin/face color closest to how it actually looks, the whole point of mirror lighting being for grooming/makeup accuracy. A pendant fixture works but is fragile (easy to break); a wall sconce or a light built into the mirror's own frame both work well. **Mounting position matters as much as the fixture**: light must come from in front, not from behind the mirror (which mostly lights the wall, not the face) and not from a ceiling fixture positioned behind the user (which throws the head's own shadow across the face). `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]
- **⚠️ Avoid a decorative/frosted or textured-glass sconce at the sink/mirror position as a substitute for real mirror lighting** — a plain bulb behind textured glass looks attractive but the glass itself scatters the light into small decorative shadows around it, which works against the actual goal (evenly lighting the face for grooming). Use a plain diffuser/shade at this specific position, save decorative textured fixtures for elsewhere in the room. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]
- **Perimeter/accent lighting** (recessed in a shadow-gap ceiling profile, backlighting shelves or the mirror surround) is purely decorative — fine to run 24/7 as ambiance, but put it on a **dimmer** if manually toggled.
- **Never rely on a single light source for the whole room — corroborated by a second, independent source.** One harsh/bright "headlight"-like fixture as the only light is uncomfortable at night and unsuited to a relaxed bath; plan a second, dimmable/decorative source specifically for those situations (framed by this source as a cheap, easy-to-plan-ahead addition — a candle substitute — not a luxury upgrade). Reinforces this page's existing "plan every light source's type" guidance below. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_9dfEdjOewng_kruglov_bathroom_mistakes|YT_9dfEdjOewng]]]
- **Skip toe-kick/under-cabinet floor lighting, including under a bathtub apron's toe-kick niche** (see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Bathtub_and_Shower|Bathtub & Shower Selection]]), unless there's daily cleaning — it highlights dirt, hair, pet fur, and dust on the floor, making the room look perpetually dirty. A bathroom is called out specifically as a high-mess-accumulation zone.
- **A mirror embedded flush into shower-wall tile is a real tradeoff, not a clear win** — see [[08_WC/WC_Guide|WC Guide]] for the full case (maintenance burden vs. a specific grooming-routine benefit) and the recessed-fixture wall-depth caveat that applies to similar embedded fixtures.
- **Plan every light source's type before finalizing the electrical layout, not just the count**: main/general light, a dimmed or decorative secondary source, a dedicated mirror light, and any niche/accent lighting are each a separate planning decision (fixture type, switching, dimmer) — see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Planning_and_Layout|Planning & Layout]]'s "list first" method.
- **A dedicated makeup/vanity zone needs its own light, switch, and outlet, distinct from mirror lighting elsewhere in the room** — general room lighting (or even a separate full-length mirror's own light) isn't sufficient for close facial inspection during makeup application. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **A dedicated low-level "night light" circuit throughout the apartment is a genuinely useful electrical-planning pattern** — small, dim fixtures placed low (near the floor, in corridors) let you navigate to the WC at night without triggering the harsh main light. **The WC/bathroom itself still needs its own night-light fixture on the same low-level circuit — but positioned high in the room, not low**, since a small WC typically has no good low mounting location the way a corridor does. Skipping the in-room fixture defeats the purpose: you'd still end up switching on the main light once inside.
- **A second, independent source frames the bathroom night-light as a standard fourth lighting scenario for this room** (alongside main/task, decorative/evening, and mirror light — see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Lighting_Design|Lighting Design]]'s scenario-count rule), implementable via a lit niche behind the sink or near the tub, or a shadow-gap ceiling profile along one wall. Consistent with, not contradicting, this page's own "position it high, not low" guidance above. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]
- **⚠️ Never light a shower spot directly above the showerhead itself** — the fixture ends up behind/above the water stream from the user's perspective, and the showerhead's own body casts a shadow onto the user standing beneath it. Position a shower spotlight to the side instead, so light reaches the user from an angle unobstructed by the fixture. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]
- **A second, independent source corroborates this page's tub-glare caution above** (see also [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Lighting_Design|Lighting Design]]'s generalized reclining-glare rule): position any ceiling spot or LED-strip niche lighting near the tub so it doesn't shine directly into your eyes while reclining. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## ⚠️ Vertical-Sconce Mirror Lighting Geometry and Ceiling Illuminance Target (Игорь Краснов, added 2026-09-01)

[source: [[_Sources/YT_ThgEv7FWNeE_krasnov_bathroom_cheap_9things|ThgEv7FWNeE]]]

- **⚠️ Vertical wall sconces beside the mirror, at eye level roughly 160–170 cm from floor, spaced 90–100 cm apart** — gives even face-lighting without the harsh top-down shadow a ceiling-only spotlight produces (shadows under eyes, emphasised nasolabial folds). **Directly corroborates this page's existing front-facing-mirror-light guidance above** (Kruglov/Ontario) with a concrete mounting-height/spacing figure that page didn't previously have.
- **If using a wall-sconce fixture at this position: 7–10 W bulb wattage at 3000–4000 K colour temperature** gives natural skin tone — a slightly wider/lower range than this page's existing "mirror light: 4000K specifically" figure from Ontario; both agree 4000K sits inside the acceptable range, this source just widens the floor to 3000K as also workable.
- **General ceiling illuminance target: 150–200 lux/m²** for a 4–6 m² bathroom — the first illuminance figure recorded for this room on this page.

`single-account`, `unverified`.

## Exhaust Fan Switching

The legacy exhaust-fan archive citation is not itself an extraction note with `channel:` frontmatter.

**Extractor fan**: prefer a forced/powered, quieter model; always add a manual switch inside the bathroom (sometimes you need it on mid-use); consider disabling automatic timer/humidity-sensor triggers in favor of manual control; never block its makeup-air supply (see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Doors|Doors]]'s door-seal warning). See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/HVAC_and_Ventilation|HVAC & Ventilation]]. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b100_ventilation_d8c0351d.txt|zems_b100_ventilation_d8c0351d]]]

**Three recommended wiring patterns, and one hard rule**: the fan's switch must always be located *inside* the room, never in the hallway — a door-open sensor (auto-on, timed auto-off) sounds convenient but is impractical to wire (needs cabling routed under the tile), fails quickly in high humidity, and — more importantly — leaves no way to turn the fan back on from inside without physically opening and closing the door again, an awkward workaround exactly when you'd want it least. **A stronger rule: never let automation be the sole way to turn the fan ON — automation should only ever be used for turning it OFF.** An unexpected, automatically-triggered fan startup is jarring and can actively work against what you're doing — e.g. a humidity sensor blasting a fan on during a deliberately steamy bath meant to help a sick child, right when the household is trying to avoid a draft. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b100_ventilation_d8c0351d.txt|zems_b100_ventilation_d8c0351d]]]

**Three recommended patterns, and no others**: (1) a simple manual on/off switch inside the room; (2) the fan wired to come on with the light, but with an *additional* independent switch (wall-mounted or on the fan's own housing) so it can run without the light, or be silenced despite the light being on; (3) a push-button "on" paired with a fixed ~60-minute auto-off timer. `single-account`, but a clear, actionable set of options with reasoning, not just a bare preference.

**A second, independent source corroborates and sharpens the underlying rule**: never wire the fan to come on *only* together with the light on one single switch/button with no separate control — a dedicated fan switch (any position inside the room) is required regardless of which of the 3 patterns above is used. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_9dfEdjOewng_kruglov_bathroom_mistakes|YT_9dfEdjOewng]]]

## ⚠️ Layered Bathroom Lighting — a Multi-Technique Bundle, a Concrete Real-World Validation (architect Robert Gar / owner Tracy Underwood, Krasnov's synthesis, added 2026-09-01, Round 6)

[source: [[_Sources/YT_JgyOwNGlevc_krasnov_37sqm_small_apartment_tour|JgyOwNGlevc]]] — a real, documented pre-war NYC co-op bathroom, narrated/critiqued by Krasnov. Techniques attributed to Gar/Underwood; the synthesis point is Krasnov's own.

- Hidden lights placed under a floating stone vanity slab, creating a visual "levitation" effect.
- Light slits/gaps illuminating end walls specifically to bring out tile texture via light-and-shadow play.
- Niche lighting in the shower and behind the door illuminating small window openings, used together to create an illusion of expanded space.
- Cove/perimeter lighting along the ceiling edge creating a "floating ceiling" effect that visually raises perceived ceiling height.
- A backlit mirror reinforcing the sense of depth.
- A slatted/grate ceiling structure zoning the upper part of the room WITHOUT visually "eating" volume.

**⚠️ Krasnov's own explicit synthesis, the point worth recording distinctly from the list itself**: light distributed across MULTIPLE levels and zones (not just one source) is specifically what let a genuinely dark bathroom palette still read as spacious rather than cramped — a strong, well-illustrated real-world validation of this page's existing layered-lighting principle; cite as a concrete case example rather than re-logging the principle.

`single-account` (Krasnov's synthesis); the underlying techniques are a real, independently-documented project, not Krasnov's own claim.
