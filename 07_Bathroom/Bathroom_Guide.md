# 🚿 Bathroom Renovation Guide

Covers layout decisions, structural/framing choices, waterproofing-adjacent finishing, tile, doors, ceilings, lighting, fixture selection, and furniture specific to the bathroom room. Plumbing rough-in, the water-inlet node, and waterproofing *code* requirements live on the engineering-systems page — see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]], which this guide cross-references rather than repeats. Quick-reference rules with sources live in [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Dos_and_Donts|the companion Do's and Don'ts table]] — this page is the narrative companion to that table, same relationship as the shared engineering-systems template.

## 1. Before Planning Anything: Documents, Approval, and the Legal Direction You Can Expand

- **Order an ЕГРН extract with its graphic (floor-plan) section before starting any layout planning**, per the Russian process described (via Госуслуги/state services) — every organization you'd ask "can this be approved" will require this document first, alongside your own proposed plan (hand-drawn or software-made, either is fine). `single-account`, **Russia-specific process — not confirmed to apply to Belarus**; treat as a prompt to find the equivalent Belarus document/process, not as this project's own procedure. *(This project's stricter-bar regulations store is at `11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/intermediate/renovation_regulations_belarus_knowledge_store.md` — this source doesn't meet that store's Belarus-location bar and isn't added there.)*
- **If expanding the bathroom's footprint, you can only expand into non-residential space** (typically a hallway/corridor or a storage closet) — expanding into a living room or bedroom is described as essentially never approvable, for any apartment above the ground floor. An apartment on the ground floor above a basement or commercial unit reportedly follows different (looser) rules. `single-account`, same Russia-specific caveat.

## 2. Combined vs. Separate Bathroom/WC

Default to a **separate** bathroom and WC. Build/keep a **combined** layout only when:
- The apartment is small and ≤3 people use the one bathroom.
- The unit has multiple bathrooms, each serving ≤2 people.
- The apartment is intended for rental — a combined layout saves on tiling/waterproofing labor (one fewer wall needing two-sided finishing) and reads as more modern/photogenic in listing photos.

`single-account`, but a clear, actionable default with stated reasoning rather than a bare preference.

## 3. Prefab Plumbing Cabin — Demolish or Keep

See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §3 for the plumbing-installation side of this decision. On the room-planning side:

- **Cabin materials, historically — none worth keeping**: reinforced concrete (thick, strong, eats the most space); gypsum-concrete/gypsum panel (lighter, less durable); **asbestos-concrete sheet material** — asbestos is a recognized carcinogen, but only hazardous once disturbed into dust (cut, drilled, broken); solid/undisturbed, it's inert. Either way, the source sees no functional reason to keep any cabin type.
- **The gap between a prefab cabin and the real structural wall is commonly ~15–20 cm** — space that's simply wasted while the cabin stands, reclaimed entirely on demolition (in addition to the ceiling-height gain).
- **A code/inspection tradeoff worth planning for**: formally registering the cabin's removal may require either (a) full brush-on waterproofing membrane across the *entire* apartment floor, not just the bathroom, or (b) building a raised threshold/curb (порожек) at the bathroom doorway — which one applies depends on the inspecting authority. `single-account`, Russia-specific process — treat the *tradeoff itself* as the transferable insight, not the Russian registration process.

## 4. Correct Dimensions — Budget for Finish-Layer Thickness, Not Raw Measurements

**Don't plan a layout against the raw measured room dimensions** — budget for what plaster and tile actually consume off each wall before finalizing clearances:
- **Plaster**: ~1–1.5 cm per side (recommend budgeting the full 1.5 cm).
- **Tile + adhesive**: ~1.5 cm per side (roughly 0.5 cm adhesive + ~0.9–1 cm for the tile itself).

A wall measured at 3.5 m can easily finish out closer to 3.2 m once both layers are accounted for on both sides — plan fixture clearances against the *finished* dimension, not the bare-shell one. `single-account`, but a concrete, checkable number worth applying regardless of source.

- **Sink minimum clear width: 80 cm after finish layers** — a worked example from a critiqued layout: a nominal 81 cm stud-to-stud gap shrank to just 75 cm once plaster and tile were applied, judged too narrow. Directly reinforces the finish-layer-thickness caveat above with a concrete case where skipping it produced an unusable width. `single-account`, `unverified`. ## 4a. Minimum "Two-Fixture" Bathroom Sizing Formula — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

> [!NOTE]
> Everything in this subsection is **one practitioner's (Alexey Zemskov / Zemstandart-Zemsproekt, Moscow-based) own stated design convention**, `single-account` throughout, presented in his video as his company's standard formula — not a building code and not independently verified against another source. Treat it as a self-consistent, arithmetic-checkable starting point to weigh against your own preferences, not a settled rule. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_small_two_fixture_bathroom_sizing_aa1b3a59.txt`]

Covers the specific case of a "two-fixture" bathroom (tub + one of: toilet/sink/washing machine) where risers/plumbing stacks sit in an *adjacent* room:
- **Room width** (Zemskov's formula): from the start wall → +100mm wall thickness → +915mm door-and-trim clearance (15mm tile+adhesive + 100mm door-trim standoff + 700mm smallest door + 100mm standoff) → + the bathtub's own width. **Zemskov's rule**: don't add the 15mm tile allowance on the bathtub side — the tub sits flush, tile overhangs the rim instead.
- **Bathtub width, per Zemskov**: 700mm minimum (workable but uncomfortable), 800mm his practical default, 900mm for larger-bodied occupants.
- **Room length, per Zemskov**: = the bathtub's own length, no added tile allowance (tile overhangs the tub on the three exposed sides).
- **Bathtub length matched to occupant height, per Zemskov**: length ≈ occupant height, or up to 10cm less — his worked example: a 178cm-tall person fits a 180cm tub comfortably, but the same person in a 190cm tub could slide under and choke. His defaults: 1700mm typical / 1800mm for taller occupants. *(Note: this contradicts the Ontario/Kruglov-sourced §10 rule of "170cm even for a ~180cm-tall person" — two different practitioners, two different formulas; a genuine point-of-view disagreement worth weighing rather than resolving here.)*
- **Zemskov's resulting standard footprint**: 1715×1700mm or 1815×1800mm, depending on tub choice.
- **Zemskov's mixer-placement rule, flagged as the single most common mistake he sees**: always center the mixer on the tub's true physical center, never on the reduced width left after subtracting the tile overhang — an off-center mixer next to a centered drain/overflow reads as visibly wrong.
- **Zemskov's toilet cistern-box height rule**: build full floor-to-ceiling with a 600×900mm access hatch if a shutoff manifold or chemical cabinet sits above; otherwise 1250mm topped with a decorative countertop.

## 5. Framing / Cladding Box Systems and Partition Material

For non-structural boxing (concealing pipes, framing a niche) — see §14 for shelving-adjacent boxing:
- **Standard: Knauf profile system**, with cladding options ranked by suitability: **ГКЛВ** (moisture-resistant drywall — standard for boxes/ceilings) → **ГВЛ/gypsum fiber board** (better fastener-holding, better tile substrate) → **aqua-panel/cement board** (best for constant water-contact zones, the best tile substrate of the three).
- **A newer, premium alternative for boxing specifically: a dedicated plumbing-specific framing-profile system** (source names "ТЦ" as the originator, with cheaper market analogs — Wallbox, Homeet — offering incomplete component ranges but still costing more than base Knauf). Pre-formed corner pieces give exact 90° angles without a level, and the system has **much higher load capacity than Knauf** — can support a heavy soaking tub/frame or an in-wall recessed faucet for a freestanding tub, which Knauf-based framing typically can't support. Material cost is "several times" higher than Knauf; labor cost similar. `single-account`, brand mentions are commercial — treat the load-capacity distinction as the useful, vendor-agnostic takeaway.

**For full partition walls (not just boxing), three materials dominate, with concrete thickness figures:**
- **Foam/gas block or gas-silicate, 10 cm** — the most common choice on this practitioner's projects. Needs plastering on both faces (~1 cm each), finishing at ~12 cm total.
- **Drywall on a metal profile**, two layers of 12.5 mm board per side on a 5 cm profile — finishes at ~10 cm total, no plastering needed. **For ceiling heights above ~2.7–2.8 m, upsize to a 75 mm profile** (from the standard 50 mm) for adequate rigidity.
- **Silicate tongue-and-groove board (ПСП — distinct from the brittle gypsum tongue-and-groove board ПГП, which this source explicitly avoids using anywhere)**, ~8 cm — doesn't strictly need plastering, but the source plasters it anyway to guarantee a flat plane for tiling.
- All three, at these thicknesses, are described as meeting typical soundproofing needs adequately — see the source's own dedicated soundproofing comparison video (linked from the original, not independently verified here) for exact acoustic/load figures.
- **Choose partition thickness with the sewage pipe route in mind, not just soundproofing/cost**: chasing a wide pipe into a thin partition weakens it disproportionately. A 50mm branch pipe into a 10cm block wall is called borderline-acceptable; a 100/110mm main branch needs either a thicker partition or a dedicated false wall (фальшстена) built specifically to conceal it — plan this before committing to a partition thickness, not after. The general layout principle underlying this: **fixture placement in a bathroom effectively orbits the sewage stack**, since it's fixed in place and drains by gravity, not pressure — route the widest pipe (typically the toilet's) along the thickest available wall (often the apartment's own exterior/bearing wall) and use a false wall to bridge any remaining gap.

## 6. A Planning Methodology: List First, Then Walk Through the Scenario

Two lightweight but genuinely useful planning techniques from one source:
1. **Write an explicit list of everything the bathroom needs to fit** before laying anything out: every fixture (tub, sink, hygienic shower, toilet type — wall-hung/floor-standing), washer/dryer (separate or combo), storage for cleaning supplies, a laundry hamper, storage systems (open/closed shelving, wall/floor-mounted cabinetry), water heater type, and every planned light source (main, dimmed/decorative, mirror-specific, niche/accent). Handing a complete list to a designer (or using it yourself) catches "this doesn't fit" problems on paper instead of during construction.
2. **Mentally walk through the actual use scenario** once a draft layout exists: leaving the shower, where's the towel — floor, hook, rail? Where do you put worn clothes? Where does the laundry hamper live, and how do you load it? This kind of walkthrough surfaces placement mistakes a static floor plan doesn't.
3. **Verify fixture clearance against the door opening's actual position, not just gross wall dimensions.** A documented developer-layout defect: a toilet with only ~7 cm of passage clearance because the door opening was located without checking it against the space actually needed for toilet + sink + tub placement — a person entering repeatedly hit the fixture with their knee. A narrow wall stub next to a bathroom doorway (360 mm in the same example) was called out as too narrow to place fixtures without one protruding into the opening. `single-account`, `unverified`, but a genuinely checkable planning failure mode. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_worst_layout_toilet_clearance_kitchen_sill_91ec632e.txt`]

## 7. Fixture Placement Priority

**Start placing fixtures with the toilet, not last.** It has the widest drain pipe and should have the shortest, straightest run to the stack — everything else is placed around it. **The shower podium/drain is the second priority**, since it's the lowest drainage point in the room (where the trap sits) and also has real routing constraints. Everything else can be placed more flexibly around these two anchors.

## 8. Shower Cabins (Built, Not Prefab)

Excluding prefab plastic shower-cabin units (already discouraged in §11/Plumbing page §9), a built shower cabin is tile/glass walls over a pan/base — the base material choice is the main decision:
- **If the base is tiled, always grout it with epoxy, never cement** — epoxy is indifferent to sustained water contact; cement grout isn't, and a shower floor sees more water exposure than almost any other tiled surface in the home.
- **Base material options**: a ready-made pan in steel, cast/engineered stone, natural stone, or acrylic, or a fully tiled base. Steel is ranked least pleasant (cold, "less nice" underfoot); stone/marble is the most premium-feeling (cold initially, but reads as luxury); acrylic and tile are solid middle options. **The cost delta between a basic steel pan and a stone/marble one is cited as only ~20,000 RUB** — framed as a one-time cost for a fixture used for the life of the renovation, worth the upgrade. `single-account`, and the same cost-vs-longevity argument the source also makes for bathtub material (§10).

## 8a. Bathtub Splash Guard: Curtain vs. Glass

**For a bathtub specifically, default to a curtain, not a glass panel** — a conclusion drawn from a renovation company's own multi-year warranty/aftercare data (they track client contact for years after project completion, not just at handover), not a bare style preference. This is a different question from §8's shower-cabin base material — for a genuine shower cabin (not a tub), glass is the only real option and this section's reasoning does *not* apply there (see the note at the end).

- **Glass only looks better for the first few hours after installation.** After that, it needs wiping after every single use — fingerprints, soap-scum residue, water spots — and hard water makes this dramatically worse (one client's account: daily cleaning of white mineral spots became "a genuine chore," not an occasional touch-up).
- **Partial-height glass (about a third of the tub's length) is purely decorative** — it doesn't meaningfully block splashes, and the floor ends up with standing puddles regardless.
- **Glass to half the tub's length or more does block splashes reasonably**, but makes actually cleaning the tub itself awkward — you generally need to remove shoes, hike up clothing, and climb partway in to reach around the panel.
- **A sliding glass panel accumulates buildup in its bottom track over time** (calcium scale, soap residue, hair, skin particles — one client's memorable phrase: "a new form of life starts growing in there") and eventually stops sliding smoothly regardless of hardware quality — at which point it typically gets left in a fixed position, reverting to the same problems as a fixed partial-length panel above.
- **A hinged (swinging, non-sliding) panel is awkward regardless of which way it swings** — it's in the way of something no matter the direction, and can pop out of its mount or break under a careless bump or load.
- **A folding/"accordion" bi-fold panel is rated the worst option of all**, per repeated independent client feedback: its folded edge protrudes either outward into the room or inward into the tub. Outward usually isn't physically possible (a sink or washing machine commonly occupies that space), so it ends up protruding inward — producing repeated, genuinely painful knee/shin impacts.
- **Injury and damage data, from the same source's long-term warranty tracking**: zero clients have ever been injured by a curtain, including cases of slipping in the tub; several dozen clients have been injured by glass in comparable situations over the years. Zero clients have damaged their renovation by reflexively grabbing a curtain while falling (worst case: the curtain tears off its rings, buy a replacement); roughly 150 cases of glass causing real damage when grabbed during a fall or slip — torn-out mounting clips, cracked tile, shattered glass. Separately, roughly 50 clients who initially chose glass later asked to have it removed and replaced with a curtain (impractical, tiring to maintain, repeatedly bumped into, doesn't slide/open well) — against zero clients who chose a curtain and later asked to switch to glass for appearance reasons.
- **A reinforcing support brace is a real, cheap safety component frequently omitted by installers who claim it's "not included" with a shower glass kit** — universal braces that fit nearly any shower glass design are now widely available, so there's little excuse to skip one. Without it, a person leaning on the glass panel risks shattering it into fragments or tearing the mounting clips out of the wall — directly relevant given the injury/damage data above. Confirm a brace is included whenever installing shower glass.
- **A small custom-fabricated rubber gasket/strip at floor level behind a fixed shower glass panel, specifically where the floor lacks proper slope, prevents water running down the glass from pooling at that one untreated spot.** This is typically hand-made by the installer from available materials, not a stock part — it's reasonable for an installer to charge extra for it as separate work, but ask for it specifically rather than assuming it's included in a standard glass installation.
- **This entire section is about a bathtub — it does not apply to a genuine shower cabin/stall.** For a shower cabin, glass is the only good option; a curtain doesn't function well there. The cleaning-burden downside still applies to shower glass, but the *other* downsides above (splash-protection failure, track buildup, awkward tub-cleaning access, injury risk) largely don't, because a properly designed shower cabin creates a fully bounded wet zone (via the pan/podium) where the door's opening path doesn't conflict with anything else in the room and doesn't need to fully seal against splashes the way a bathtub screen does. See §8 for shower-cabin base construction.

## 8b. Recovering Leftover Space When a Tub Alcove Is Longer Than the Tub

A concrete problem worth planning for explicitly: fixed elements (a load-bearing wall, a utility box, a stack) sometimes force a tub alcove to be longer than the ideal tub length for the people using it (see §10 for tub length ergonomics) — leaving genuine leftover space at one end. **Two tempting but wrong solutions, and the correct one**:
- **Wrong: a flat/level surface extension beside the tub** to visually "lengthen" it — water pools there and creates a slip/mold hazard.
- **Wrong: ordinary furniture-style cabinet doors or open shelving in that leftover space, opening toward the tub/shower side.** Standard furniture door seals aren't waterproof — shower/bath splash reliably reaches anything stored there, soaking toilet paper and other contents and breeding mold and damp smell over time.
- **Correct: build a proper tiled utility column with its access opening facing the hallway/corridor side, never the tub side.** A tub-side opening gets water intrusion, makes the lower portion unusable, and leaves the upper portion unreachable (you can't safely stand a stool or ladder inside the tub itself to reach it, since the tub's own rim is sloped). Access from the corridor instead avoids all three problems and gives a large, genuinely useful, floor-to-ceiling storage column.
- **Construction detail**: tile the tub's own apron/screen with the same tile as the surrounding wall, so the junction between the apron and the new utility column reads as one continuous surface meeting at a clean 45°-mitered corner, rather than looking like two visually separate elements bolted together.

## 8c. A Load-Bearing-Wall Workaround for Washing Machine Hookups

**Never chase (штробить) a load-bearing wall horizontally for water supply or drain lines — this is a real structural prohibition, not a preference, and it applies at any depth, including shallow chases.** Vertical electrical chases are generally fine, since the plaster layer already applied to every bathroom wall is thick enough to hide most conduit without needing to cut into the structural wall itself — but water/drain routing needs a much deeper horizontal channel, which a load-bearing wall can't accommodate at all. **If a washing machine needs to sit against a load-bearing wall with no legal way to route its supply/drain/outlet to it directly, run those connections through the bathtub's own apron/screen structure instead** — the apron is already a non-structural, purpose-built cavity, and this avoids both the code violation and the alternative of building the wall out further (which eats into the room's usable width). A simple, easily-overlooked workaround worth defaulting to whenever this specific conflict comes up.

## 9. Bathtub vs. Shower

- **A bathtub is the more universal fixture**: someone who prefers showers can still shower in a tub, but someone who wants a bath can't get one from a shower stall — a relevant asymmetry for a rental unit, where you don't know the future occupant's preference. A tub is also generally easier for bathing small children, and for washing a large dog's paws (contains the mess; a shower area lets water/mud spread).
- **The source's statement on elderly suitability is internally inconsistent and not resolved here**: it states a tub "suits elderly users" in one breath, then immediately notes elderly users with knee problems often can't step into a tub at all — read as a caution that mobility should be checked case-by-case rather than assuming a tub is broadly elderly-friendly, not as a clean recommendation either way.
- For a case with no room for a full tub (the source's own home example), a shower zone sized for dog-paw-washing is offered as a compromise — case-specific, not a universal substitute for the above.

## 10. Bathtub Selection

- **Four material types, compared directly**:
  - **Steel** — rings/resonates when tapped, feels stiff and unpleasantly cold to the touch until the water warms it.
  - **Cast iron** — the most rigid construction of the four; heats up and cools down slowly, so even after filling it with hot water the tub's own body can still feel unpleasantly cold against your back; also cold to the touch when dry (e.g., brushing teeth leaning against it). Repairing chipped enamel is described as very difficult — re-glazing rarely matches the original factory finish.
  - **Acrylic** — huge price range depending on wall thickness/reinforcement; the cheapest versions flex/give underfoot. **Easiest of the four to repair** (a cracked or punctured acrylic tub can be patched relatively easily — the source's company has done this often after trades damaged tubs mid-project); an anti-slip coating, if damaged, generally can't be restored to its original grip once worn away.
  - **Cast/engineered marble (литьевой мрамор)** — the source's own recommendation: warm to the touch like acrylic, retains heat and feels as solid/durable as cast iron, and repairs are straightforward. Priced comparably to a *good* acrylic or *good* cast-iron tub in the same tier — the source explicitly notes a cheap acrylic tub (~15,000–20,000 RUB) is not a fair comparison, since a comparably solid acrylic or cast-iron tub runs similarly to marble (~40,000–50,000 RUB) once quality is held constant. `single-account`, explicitly the source's own opinion — but restated with more direct material-by-material reasoning across two of this channel's videos, not just asserted once.
- **Length ergonomics — don't default to the longest tub that fits**: a 170 cm tub is recommended even for a person ~180 cm tall (not just shorter users) — a longer tub means less to brace against, so you tend to slide down against the back wall; 170 cm gives enough length to keep your head above water while still bracing your feet against the far end comfortably.
- **Freestanding vs. built-in — a real plumbing constraint, not just an aesthetic choice**: see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §9 for the full drain/slope implication and the concrete mitigation options (raising the floor, a lift pump, routing via the floor below in a private house, or negotiating connection depth with the management company) — a freestanding tub generally needs its own floor drain and may force one of those choices, unless the sewage branch is already close and deep enough (roughly within 5-6 m of the stack, per one source's rule of thumb). Decide the tub type *before* finalizing floor-buildup plans, not after. **Also budget cleaning access around a freestanding tub** — if it's pushed flush against a wall on one side, that side becomes impossible to clean properly; leave a real gap if any side is against a wall.

## 11. Bathtub Apron / Screen (Экран)

- **Most popular current method**: a framed structure (block or drywall/profile) with a revision hatch, tiled over.
> [!WARNING]
> **Direct contradiction between two sources on toe-kick niches, not resolved here.** The Ontario channel (below) recommends a full-length toe-kick niche as the *only* valid foot-clearance option. A second, independent channel (Земстандарт, added 2026-07-31) argues **no toe-kick recess of any kind should be built at all** — calling it a legacy feature from the era of hand-washing laundry in a floor basin (kneeling at the tub, needing to tuck your feet under it), now obsolete since everyone has a washing machine, and purely a dirt trap with no remaining function. Both sources are experienced renovation-company practitioners; this page presents both positions rather than picking a winner — decide based on your own household's actual use pattern (e.g., do you ever kneel at the tub for anything — bathing a child, hand-washing something — or not).

- **Toe-kick niche (Ontario's position)**: build a **full-length tiled niche running the entire length of the tub** — the only currently "valid" option per this source, against three deprecated alternatives (an angled/beveled tile skirt, a small narrow niche, or a plain leg-clearance gap that collects dust/debris and cannot actually be reached by hand for servicing, contrary to what it might look like it allows). **A third source independently reinforces this specific warning from the lighting angle**: any light source placed to illuminate this gap (an increasingly popular "designer" touch) makes the accumulated dust/hair/lint underneath highly visible rather than hiding it — the exact same logic as the toe-kick floor-lighting warning in §17, applied to the same physical location.
- **No toe-kick recess at all (Земстандарт's position)**: skip the foot-clearance gap entirely, for the reasons above — pure dirt accumulation with no functional benefit for a household using a washing machine rather than hand-washing at the tub.
- **Never cut a full through-hole/vent opening in the apron** (distinct from a toe-kick niche — an actual round or rectangular opening straight through the panel) — one source warns this lets water seep in and causes a persistent damp smell that's very hard to fix afterward, and separately recounts two cases of small animals (a hedgehog, a cat) getting into such an opening and becoming trapped permanently. A clear, low-ambiguity "don't."
- **Always include emergency access to the drain/overflow assembly — but distinguish emergency access from routine/revision access, since they call for different solutions**: **revision access** (opened regularly — water meters, coarse/fine filters you service routinely) needs a proper hatch. **Emergency access** (essentially never opened — modern siphons, corrugated hoses, and plastic pipe are all rated for multi-decade service life) doesn't need a full hatch at all, per one source — even if using a self-cleaning siphon (which lets you clear its internal chamber without disassembly), you still need *some* way to reach the assembly if the overflow/drain itself ever fails; the two aren't substitutes for each other, but "some way to reach it eventually" doesn't require a permanent visible hatch.
- **A concrete technique for hidden emergency-only access, avoiding a visible hatch altogether**: plan the apron's tile layout so a small rectangle (roughly 2 tiles) near the drain zone can be removed without disturbing neighboring tiles — the surrounding whole tiles should overlap the access rectangle's edges by a couple of centimeters. Before installing just those 1-2 tiles, wrap their backs in stretch/cling film so the adhesive can't actually bond them to the substrate; grout the rest of the apron normally, but seal only the perimeter of these tiles with flexible sealant instead of grout. When emergency access is genuinely needed years later: cut the sealant line, pop the film-backed tiles out with a suction cup, do the repair, reinstall the same tiles, and reseal. `single-account`, but a clever, concrete, and genuinely reusable technique — a real alternative to a visible hatch for a component this unlikely to need attention.
- **Reinforce the apron's frame before tiling, so it doesn't flex underfoot** — especially important for acrylic tubs, which flex noticeably when stepped into if the surrounding structure isn't rigid.
- **Build the frame from masonry block, not a metal-stud/drywall frame** — faster and cheaper, and because a metal-stud frame is hard to anchor properly to the tub's own rounded top rim; anchoring it only on three sides (left/right/bottom, skipping the top) produces a worse structural result at similar-or-higher cost than block.
- **Apron tile layout must align exactly with the surrounding wall tile layout** — as if a section of the wall tile pattern were cut out and placed in front of the tub: vertical joints should line up with the grout lines on the wall above the tub, horizontal joints should line up with the side walls' horizontal joints.
- **A cheaper/faster newer trend**: XPS foam (extruded polystyrene) glued together as the apron's frame core, glued directly to the tub edges, then tiled over — functionally equivalent to a framed structure at lower cost/time.
- Off-the-shelf universal aprons with access doors (Hoff, Leroy Merlin, Lemana Pro-type retail products) are flagged as largely obsolete in current renovation practice — a second source adds a mechanistic reason beyond "obsolete": most tub manufacturers only sell the tub itself, not a matching factory screen, developer-installed tub height varies (~55-65 cm) against a fixed factory-panel height (forcing trimming or leaving a visible gap), and a factory panel doesn't seal the bottom joint against water ingress the way a tiled apron does.
- **Tub-to-tile junction sequencing**: ideally the tub is sized/positioned against already-plastered walls, with tile then coming down onto the tub's rim (not the reverse — don't tile first and then try to fit a tub against the finished tile). Occasionally a tub is chased slightly into the wall plaster, or even trimmed, to make this junction work — the goal is always tile landing on the rim cleanly, not an afterthought trim strip. **The rim should never sit proud of the tile surface — flush is acceptable, but slightly recessed is better**: most tub rims are rounded on the underside as well as the top, so a flush straight tile edge creates a wedge-shaped grout gap along the rim, and flexing under load (especially on acrylic, but even cast iron flexes slightly) can squeeze grout out of that gap over time.
- **Keep the apron strictly vertical/plumb — never build it raked or angled** — both for appearance and because any acute-angle corner traps dirt a standard cleaning brush can't reach.

## 11a. Bathtub Revision-Hatch Sealing & Spare-Materials Storage — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

> [!NOTE]
> This subsection is Zemskov/Zemstandart's own stated practice and reasoning, `single-account`, no cost figures given. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_bathtub_revision_hatch_spare_materials_c26c1a59.txt`]

- **Zemskov's rule: never make a bathtub siphon revision-access hatch quick-release/magnetic — always tile it in and seal the perimeter with silicone.** His stated reasoning: a magnetic hatch isn't watertight, splashed bathwater seeps into the unsealed gap and — with no airflow under the tub — rots, producing a persistent odor with no obvious source; separately, tile adhesive is hygroscopic and water reaching it via the gap causes it to swell and drop tiles.
- **Zemskov's correct-construction sequence**: position the tile precisely, then seal the full perimeter with silicone in one step (secures + waterproofs together).
- **Zemskov ties this to the leak-protection system**: a sensor at the potential-leak point under the tub means emergency access is needed rarely, which is why he considers a permanently sealed (non-quick-access) hatch acceptable rather than risky.
- **Zemskov's spare-materials habit**: store spare tile *inside* the same sealed hatch space, not in a separate storage area — his stated real-world observation is that tile stored elsewhere "consistently doesn't survive" until needed. His reasoning: tile lines get discontinued, and even same-collection replacement tile from a different batch visibly differs in color, so keeping the original leftover is the only reliable match. If both dark and light tile were used, he stores spares of both, plus spare grout (hard to match later, especially two-component) and a sealed, never-opened tube of silicone (an already-opened one dries out before it's needed).
- **Zemskov's own comparative framing**: cutting a silicone seal to retrieve stored spares is trivial next to the alternative it protects against — a cracked tile requiring jackhammer demolition and full retiling.

## 11b. Premium/Comfort-Class Bathroom Solutions — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

## 12. Access Hatches (Люки)

Three construction types, roughly in ascending cost/durability order:
1. **Off-the-shelf plastic/metal surface-mount** — fine for low-visibility/budget locations.
2. **Tiled hatch** (factory custom-order or an off-the-shelf pre-tile-ready frame) — hinge mechanisms vary (reinforced, hinged, pull-out).
3. **Tiled hatch built on a dedicated plumbing-framing-profile system** (see §5) — very strong, any shape (including L-/P-shaped, over a meter tall), near-invisible seams.

**A technique for a near-invisible, rarely-opened hatch**: seal the perimeter with elastic silicone sealant, let it cure, then score/cut along the outer contour — opening the hatch "peels" the grout line with it, hiding the seam when closed. **Caveat**: repeated open/close cycles can stop the sealant reseating fully — only suitable for a hatch meant for rare/emergency access, not frequent use.

**Furniture-panel hatches** (MDF/LDSP with acrylic/plastic/film finish): the failure point is edge-banding/coating damage — once compromised, moisture exposure quickly ruins the panel. **Use only laminated/enameled, moisture-resistant panel material for this — never plain particleboard (ЛДСП)**, which reportedly swells within about a week of moisture exposure.

**A separate, mechanism-level axis from the three construction types above: sagging vs. non-sagging hinge mechanisms (added 2026-08-04)**. Standard sagging-mechanism hatches are described by one practitioner as unreliable at any size, to the point of personally excluding any other type from his own projects — they need awkward multi-point force (suction cup, foot, elbow) to budge, especially at larger sizes. A non-sagging mechanism type opens/closes one-handed regardless of size/weight; demonstrated load-tested by the speaker's full body weight with negligible deflection. Recommended non-sagging over standard/sagging as a mechanism-quality upgrade independent of which of the three construction types above is chosen. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

**A dedicated low-level "night light" circuit throughout the apartment is a genuinely useful electrical-planning pattern** — small, dim fixtures placed low (near the floor, in corridors) let you navigate to the WC at night without triggering the harsh main light. **The WC/bathroom itself still needs its own night-light fixture on the same low-level circuit — but positioned high in the room, not low**, since a small WC typically has no good low mounting location the way a corridor does. Skipping the in-room fixture defeats the purpose: you'd still end up switching on the main light once inside.

## 13. Tile — Sizing, Sequencing, Grout, and Corners

**Size taxonomy** (a useful reference vocabulary): classic 20–60 cm; "narrow"/plank tile (short:long ratio ≥1:3, wood-look); "medium large-format" 80–140 cm; "large-format" 150–240 cm; "very large format" >240 cm; small-format mosaic/"кабанчик" (subway-style), on mesh sheets or loose.

**Sequencing**: lay and grout the floor tile **first**, including under the tub's footprint — set the tub *after* floor tiling, not before (see §11 for how the tub-to-wall-tile junction itself sequences).

**Grout comparison**:
| Type | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Standard cement | Cheapest, easiest to repair (can dig out/re-grout anytime) | Absorbs moisture, can mold/mildew, darkens, can crumble |
| Improved cement (polymer/latex-additive) | Denser, less water absorption, holds color, much less mold | Still not fully waterproof |
| **Epoxy (two-component)** | Essentially waterproof, doesn't absorb dirt/grease/dye, mold-resistant, most durable — **the right choice for a tiled shower podium or shower-cabin base** (see §8, and [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §9) | **3–5× the cost** of cement grout (material + labor); risk of permanently marking textured/embossed tile if misapplied |

**Corner rule**: external corners are always 45°-mitered and **should always use epoxy grout even if the rest of the room uses cement grout** — a slight tile shift at an external corner will pop cement grout out (insufficient bonding area). A plastic/metal corner-trim profile is the deprecated older alternative — "haven't seen it used in ~5-6 years," per the source. Internal corners are never mitered (tile butts with overlap) and are sealed only with elastic silicone sealant, never grout — install a foam backer rod behind the sealant first, which improves adhesion and sealant lifespan.

**A premium, labor-intensive corner-rounding technique exists** — miter tiles at 45° as usual, then further grind/chamfer the joint and grout it with a specialized process to produce a smooth, fully rounded, injury-safe edge (roughly matching the radius of typical bathroom fixture edges), rather than a sharp mitered corner. Described as extremely slow, precision hand-work (compared to a manicure — small tools, low speed, methodical) — **real cost cited: ~120,000 RUB for this detail alone on one project.** A luxury-tier finishing option, not a default recommendation, but worth knowing as a real cost data point if injury-safe rounded corners are a priority.

- **Shower podium can sometimes be skipped entirely (added 2026-08-04)**: with a sufficiently thick screed (14 cm in one cited case), the drain/trap can be recessed without raising the floor at all — check site conditions before assuming a podium is required (see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §9 for the general slope-constraint reasoning this depends on). Where a podium/curb is still needed, keep it low (~5 cm) — tall enough to register visually and prevent stubbed toes, but a taller podium is a worse trip/injury risk. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Shower curb height should account for handheld-shower/bucket use, not just an overhead fixed shower (added 2026-08-04)** — where a household member dumps water over themselves rather than only using an overhead fixture, plan for the resulting splash volume (worked example: ~20 L) with a correspondingly higher curb/threshold (~6–7 cm in that case) instead of a standard low curb, to contain the water inside the shower area. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_business_class_five_attributes_19385e7a.txt`]
- **Shower valve/spout height should be set per-occupant, not a fixed standard height (added 2026-08-04)** — measure the household's actual heights and factor in the specific shower-wand hose length (the customer picks the shower set in advance) so the height works for every household member, rather than defaulting to a generic standard. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Shower podium riser tile-matching technique (added 2026-08-04)**: cut the podium's riser faces from the same porcelain tile used on the floor (small wedge-shaped cuts to form the slope/drain) rather than substituting mosaic — mosaic reliably fails to match the main tile's exact look. Alternative shown: cut the same porcelain tile into small squares and mesh-mount it as custom-matched "mosaic" for the podium specifically, when a curved/stepped riser genuinely needs mosaic-scale pieces. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]

### 13.0 Porcelain Tile Selection — Type, Format, Quality Parameters, and Pricing

From a tile-industry wholesaler guest-source. **Flag pricing and country-of-origin claims below as commercial framing** — the guest is affiliated with the host channel's own discount partnership, an explicit conflict of interest on exactly those two points; the quality-parameter vocabulary (calibre, rectification, surface finish, coloring depth) is more straightforwardly technical and reusable regardless of that bias.

- **Porcelain tile (керамогранит) has largely displaced ceramic tile (плитка) in the market** — thicker, heavier, stronger, supports larger formats without cracking, and is generally considered to look better; porcelain reportedly makes up ~90% of current retail inventory. In current practice, "tile" for a bathroom/WC effectively means porcelain tile.
- **Format**: rectangular, square, or "plank" (an elongated rectangle, usually wood-look). **Walls**: rectangular or square. **Floors**: square or plank. Plank *can* go on a wall, but only as a single accent wall — using it broadly risks looking odd.
- **Size selection is a genuine tradeoff, not "bigger is always better"**: larger tile means fewer, less visible grout lines, but must be checked against (a) the size of any revision hatch and (b) plain material-waste common sense for the specific room. A 60×60cm revision hatch is a common standard size, so 60×30 or 60×60cm tile aligns cleanly with it (and with typical toilet-frame heights and tub lengths) — a reasonable general-purpose default. **Don't use large-format tile in a small room just to minimize grout lines**: a worked example given — laying oversized slabs (e.g. 90×120cm) in a room only slightly larger than that slab wastes roughly half the material to trim scrap, while a room sized close to a whole-number multiple of the tile can be covered almost without waste. **General default sizes**: walls 60×30 or 60×60cm; floor 60×60cm; scale down to 45×45 or 40×40cm if the room is small; scale up to 75×75 or 90×90cm if it's large. Sizing decisions should also account for revision-hatch position and electrical/plumbing stub-out locations, so a socket or pipe stub doesn't land right at a tile edge and force an ugly narrow cut fragment around it.
- **A second source's own current fixed default (added 2026-08-04): 60×20cm** (with 60×20/120×60 as size variants) for bathroom walls/floors — minimizes grout lines, ages well over 10-15 years, priced acceptably at this format. `single-account`, presented as this channel's own current standard rather than a market-wide default — treat as a second concrete option alongside the 60×30/60×60cm default above, not a replacement for it. A practical hiring check tied to this size: a tradesperson tiling at 60×20/120×60cm scale needs both a manual tile cutter capable of cutting up to ~120cm and an electric wet saw — lacking either disqualifies them from the job. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]
- **Even quality tile shows visible joint-width variation at 45°-cut corners on large-format/thin-joint tile — a manufacturing-tolerance limitation, not evidence of poor installation (added 2026-08-04)**: factory calibre tolerance causes the joint to appear to widen then narrow around a mitered corner even with correctly-laid, quality tile. Worth knowing before assuming a widening/narrowing corner joint means the installer did something wrong. `unverified`, speaker's own claim. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt`]
- **Quality parameters worth knowing when comparing tile, independent of price**:
  - **Calibre**: batch-to-batch dimensional precision. Press molds wear differently over a production run, so tiles even within one batch can vary slightly in size — calibre is the stated tolerance (e.g., a 60×60cm tile with a calibre rating might range 597–603mm). **Lower calibre is better** — a high-calibre batch is physically difficult to lay with thin, even grout lines, since some tiles will be oversized relative to neighbors. **Rectified tile** (trimmed to exact size after firing, rather than left at as-fired size) achieves near-zero calibre and enables thin, precise grout lines — most modern porcelain tile is now rectified; genuinely non-rectified tile is comparatively hard to find now.
  - **Surface finish**: matte, glossy/polished, satin (semi-gloss), structured/textured (mimicking a material like wood), or "lapato" (mixed matte+glossy regions within one tile). **Never use glossy tile on a floor** — slippery, and gloss visibly dulls in high-traffic patches over time; matte or structured only for floors. Glossy is fine on walls, and its reflectivity can visually enlarge a small room. **Lapato tile only works in large spaces where several tiles are visible together at once** — in a small area, a single lapato tile just reads as a smudge/dirty patch rather than an intentional effect.
  - **Coloring depth**: surface-only printed coloring vs. full-body/through-body coloring (colored throughout the tile's full thickness). **Use full-body-colored tile for floors specifically** — surface-only coloring wears through with foot traffic over time; on walls, where there's no foot traffic, the distinction matters much less.
  - **"Perfectly flat" tile doesn't exist, and minor waviness isn't a defect**: even premium tile lines have some natural bow/twist/warp within a few millimeters, driven by humidity variance during firing — normal and already screened for at the factory's own QC stage. Only reject a batch for genuinely severe warping, not for the kind of minor waviness present in essentially all tile including premium lines.
- **Pricing framing (commercial — see caveat above)**: don't set a fixed tile budget and shop down to it — tile lasts 20+ years, so a modest per-m² price increase amortizes to very little over that lifespan. The source's own recommended target band: **~20–30 EUR/m²** (center ~25 EUR/m²) for solid design/geometry; below ~20 EUR/m² options narrow noticeably; above ~30 EUR/m² there's little further improvement until a second tier around **~70 EUR/m²**, described as genuine premium Italian/Spanish tile, recommended only for a property valued above roughly 100,000 EUR (finish-material spend should scale with the property's own value). **Treat this pricing guidance, and the source's claim that Russian-made porcelain has closed the gap with Italian/Spanish tile, as one vendor-affiliated opinion, not verified market data.**
- **Three practical buying tips (general technique, not commercial)**: (1) avoid "discontinued"/clearance tile even at a good price — often assembled from mismatched warehouse batches with calibre/color inconsistencies across the lot; (2) avoid pre-ordering foreign tile that doesn't physically exist yet (made-to-order) — quoted lead times (e.g. "2 months") reportedly stretch to 6-12 months in practice fairly often; (3) buy tile 1-2 months ahead of installation, and if storage on-site isn't practical, pay the retailer to hold it rather than risk the specific batch selling out or being discontinued before you're ready to install.

### 13.1 Tile Layout Pattern — the Actual Technique for Avoiding Ugly Corner Slivers

A concrete, step-by-step method from a second source, genuinely new content not covered elsewhere on this page — the difference between a bathroom that "looks like the tile was chosen to fit the room" and one with awkward narrow cut strips in the corners comes down to this technique, not tile selection:

**Three layout approaches exist**:
1. **"Ugly" layout**: start laying whole tiles from one corner and keep going until you run out of room — whatever's left over becomes a single cut piece in the far corner. Only acceptable if that leftover cut piece is wider than half a full tile; if it's narrower, this produces a visibly awkward sliver. **Recommendation: avoid this approach entirely, regardless of the leftover width** — the other two methods below are essentially always better.
2. **"Village/rustic" layout**: same as above, but split the leftover between *both* corners symmetrically — produces two narrower (but matching) cut pieces instead of one. Better than the ugly layout, but the cut pieces are still visibly narrow. **The one legitimate use case for this layout**: when you specifically need a plumbing-access hatch/niche to land entirely on whole, uncut tiles (see §12) and centering the layout would otherwise force the hatch onto a cut piece — in that specific situation, use this layout for the section containing the hatch, sized so the hatch location gets full tiles. If a plumbing box is too narrow to naturally produce a whole-tile hatch location this way, **widen the box slightly rather than accept a bad hatch layout** — a little lost storage space is worth a correct tile layout (and it also nets a wider utility shelf as a side benefit).
3. **"Classic" layout — the correct default for everything else**: (1) calculate how many whole tile rows/columns fit across the surface; (2) remove one whole row/column from that count; (3) lay what's left starting from the center, working outward toward both edges. This guarantees both corner cut pieces end up wider than half a tile, and they'll be equal or near-equal in width — which reads as intentional and harmonious rather than like an afterthought. Costs marginally more material (roughly an extra couple of square meters on a typical bathroom) — a worthwhile tradeoff against a lifetime of looking at bad corners.

**Application nuances**:
- **Floor tile follows the same classic-layout logic** as walls.
- **In a shower, apply classic layout across the full visible room length.** In a bathtub zone, apply it across the *visible* section only (between the entry wall and the tub apron) — the narrow cut strip that results directly under the tub itself is fine to leave as-is, since it's permanently hidden once the tub is installed.
- **If a room has two zones of different visible length** (e.g., a longer run behind a vanity vs. a shorter run near the toilet), apply the classic layout to whichever zone stays fully visible after fixtures are installed — optimizing a zone that ends up hidden behind a sink or cabinet wastes the technique on the wrong wall.
- **If shrinking a utility box slightly (e.g., by ~15 cm) yields meaningfully more room for a larger sink elsewhere**, that tradeoff can be worth accepting a correspondingly larger visible cut piece near the box, breaking strict symmetry — the size difference is described as essentially invisible in normal use (only noticeable if someone deliberately stands in the doorway comparing the two corners).
- **For continuous flooring across multiple adjoining rooms (avoiding a transition strip/threshold at doorways), a second, non-obvious technique from the same source's own project extends the classic-layout method**: keep each wall as a separately movable object during planning (not fused into a single shape) specifically so a wall can be nudged later without redoing the whole layout. **When a narrow cut piece shows up along a wall, move the wall itself, not the tile grid — always in multiples of 10mm.** This matters because of a real, non-obvious mechanism: moving a wall consumes exactly one grout joint's worth of material at that wall, so both tiles on either side of that joint remain whole — a cut piece removed in one room by shifting its wall does **not** cause a new cut piece to appear in the adjoining room. (Sliding the *tile grid* instead of the wall does risk exactly that knock-on problem — which is the common mistake this technique avoids.)
- **A practical priority order for checking/fixing narrow cut pieces across a whole apartment's tile layout**: (1) the main/central circulation space first — confirm left-right cut pieces are equal and wider than half a tile; (2) other main rooms' bare walls (no furniture against them); (3) transition/passage spaces, especially right at an entry doorway — a bad cut piece is most noticeable exactly where the eye lands first on walking in, so it's worth prioritizing over strict symmetry elsewhere; (4) window walls (also reliably bare); (5) only then bring furniture placement into the model and re-check everything once more, since furniture can hide an otherwise-bad cut piece and change what's worth optimizing for; (6) finally, auxiliary/utility rooms. **Regulatory exception worth knowing about**: some room types (the source's own example: a licensed medical facility's sterilization room and WC, with legally fixed minimum dimensions) can't be adjusted even slightly, forcing an unavoidable cut piece — not a concern in an ordinary apartment renovation, where there's essentially always room to shift a wall a few centimeters instead of living with a bad cut piece permanently.
- **Vertical tile orientation is discouraged in most cases** — even with a correct classic layout, vertical tiles still show long narrow cut strips (rather than the compact corner pieces horizontal tiles produce), which looks worse. Vertical orientation is described as only working well at very cheap or very expensive/bespoke tile tiers; horizontal is the right default for the large middle of the market.

## 14. Shelving and Storage

- **Skip mirror cabinets over the sink** — described as inefficient use of space; a narrow (~35–40 cm wide, ~2–2.5 m tall) pantry-style cabinet elsewhere in the room, plus a plain lit mirror, is recommended instead of trying to combine storage and mirror in one shallow unit.
- **Tiled niches** are the most durable/stylish shelving option but the most expensive; **glass shelving in a single niche is offered as a functionally equivalent, "tens of times cheaper" alternative** — cheaper than fully tiling multiple individual shelf niches, per the fixture-selection source. **Use tempered/toughened glass only** — it shatters into small, safer pieces rather than shards if broken.
- **Hide open shelving/product-clutter from the direct sightline of someone entering the room** — place it on the wall nearest the door (out of the immediate view) rather than opposite the entrance, or use concealed/recessed shelving instead of open metal corner caddies, which one source considers visually messy regardless of how organized the contents are.
- **Flush-mount metal shelving embedded in a tile joint** (no visible mounting hardware) — durability depends entirely on powder-coat finish quality (properly cured in a paint booth = durable).
- **Small-bathroom storage via a raised water-inlet node (added 2026-08-04)**: raise the water-inlet node ("узел приёма воды") on a platform, addressed at project stage, to create usable storage space underneath for toiletries/paper goods — a way to reclaim floor-adjacent storage in a tight bathroom without a dedicated cabinet. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Floor-standing vs. wall-hung vanity cabinet — reconsider the default "wall-hung is always better" assumption**: wall-hung is easier to clean under, but if the sink sits close to the tub and you'd need to reach its own overflow/drain siphon for repair, a wall-hung cabinet can be very difficult to remove for access. A floor-standing cabinet with a countertop-mounted sink and a wall-mounted faucet lets you simply slide the cabinet aside for the same access — worth choosing case-by-case based on proximity to the tub's own serviceable plumbing, not just on ease of floor cleaning.

## 15. Doors

| Type | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hinged/swing (concealed-frame or standard) | Cheapest, most selection, no custom sizing, good soundproofing | Less trendy look, but works fine with good design planning |
| Flush/concealed-mount | Most modern/popular now; any finish material possible (panel, veneer, mirror, metal); only type supporting door closers and improved leaf soundproofing | ~90% custom-order (pricier material+labor); needs extra finishing — tiling/grouting/mitering the door reveal, painting both sides — cited at **~20,000-25,000 RUB extra per door** (5 doors ≈ 100,000-130,000 RUB, easy to underestimate in a budget); only pairs with concealed/shadow-gap skirting, not classic baseboards |
| Sliding | — | **Explicitly the worst option** — worst soundproofing of the three; recommended against for bathrooms except a private master-suite bathroom used only by a couple |

- **If a full-size washer/dryer (~60×60 cm) is planned, the door opening needs to be 80 cm (70 cm leaf), not the more typical 60 cm leaf** — a standard door leaf is too narrow for the appliance to pass through after the door is finished, forcing a costly disassemble-appliance-in / reassemble-door-around-it workaround (paying for the door installation labor twice, and risking the reassembled door looking different from a first-time install). Plan this before finishing, not after.
- **Account for tile buildup when setting the door frame's reveal/upstand at a wet-room opening, or the door trim will end up crammed against the tile.** A general rule already used for corner clearances is a ≥100mm reveal from a door frame to an adjacent wall/corner — but at a bathroom/WC doorway specifically, that reveal is measured *before* tile and adhesive are added to the wall, and tile+adhesive has a real, non-trivial thickness. If the raw (pre-tile) reveal is built to exactly 100mm, the finished reveal after tiling will be visibly less, and the door casing will sit too close to the tile. **Build the raw reveal to ~115mm or more at a wet-room door specifically**, so it still nets out to ≥100mm once tile and adhesive are accounted for. `single-account`, but a concrete, checkable number worth applying regardless of source.
- **The flooring transition strip at a bathroom/WC doorway should sit centered under the door in its closed position**, so neither room's flooring is visible past the threshold from the other side — and **the strip itself should be no wider than ~40mm**, matching a standard door leaf's own thickness; a wider strip visibly protrudes and looks like an afterthought.
- **Never add a drop-down/lift threshold draft seal to a bathroom door**, even though it's marketed as a soundproofing upgrade. A bathroom's exhaust fan (wall-mounted or in-duct) depends on makeup air flowing in under/around the door to actually pull air out; sealing that gap starves the fan of the airflow it needs to work, causes it to run against resistance, and shortens its service life — the same "no extraction without makeup air" principle already documented for kitchen hoods in [[03_Kitchen/Appliances/analysis/Hood_Analysis|Kitchen Hood Analysis]] §2, now confirmed to apply to bathroom exhaust fans too.

## 16. Ceilings

**Stretch ceiling is used in ~95% of bathrooms and is recommended as the default choice** even by designers who'd otherwise prefer drywall — reasons given: fast install, cost, reliability, repairability, and full immunity to temperature/humidity swings. A drywall ceiling's failure mode is severe by comparison: even a minor leak from the upstairs neighbor requires **replacing the entire ceiling structure**, since drywall has nowhere for absorbed moisture to go. PVC panels, Armstrong grid ceilings, and metal/plastic slat ceilings are all flagged as essentially obsolete in current renovation practice.

## 17. Lighting

- **Prefer a diffused, large-area "glowing ceiling" light source over a single bright spot fixture for general illumination** — bright/even enough to catch every cleaning-relevant detail, but soft enough not to be uncomfortable to look up into while lying in the tub.
- **Always run dedicated electrical supply to the mirror location for its own lighting, even if no general ceiling fixture would technically be "too dim" without it** — no amount of general room/ceiling light is ever sufficient for grooming or makeup at the mirror on its own; a mirror light doesn't need its own switch (usually built into the fixture itself), but it does need its own wiring run, planned during rough-in — retrofitting power to a mirror after walls are finished means fishing wire through a completed wall.
- **Mirror lighting must be front-facing** (illuminating the face directly), not light that just washes onto the surrounding tile as decor — front light is what actually lets you see skin detail while grooming; ceiling light alone leaves the face in shadow. Tradeoff: a front-light fixture's frame/profile is visibly present around the mirror.
- **Perimeter/accent lighting** (recessed in a shadow-gap ceiling profile, backlighting shelves or the mirror surround) is purely decorative — fine to run 24/7 as ambiance, but put it on a **dimmer** if manually toggled.
- **Skip toe-kick/under-cabinet floor lighting, including under a bathtub apron's toe-kick niche** (see §11), unless there's daily cleaning — it highlights dirt, hair, pet fur, and dust on the floor, making the room look perpetually dirty. A bathroom is called out specifically as a high-mess-accumulation zone (hair, lint, dried water spots), making this warning apply more strongly here than in most rooms.
- **A mirror embedded flush into shower-wall tile is a real tradeoff, not a clear win** — see [[08_WC/WC_Guide|WC Guide]] §4 for the full case (maintenance burden vs. a specific grooming-routine benefit) and the recessed-fixture wall-depth caveat that applies to similar embedded fixtures.
- **Plan every light source's type before finalizing the electrical layout, not just the count**: main/general light, a dimmed or decorative secondary source, a dedicated mirror light, and any niche/accent lighting are each a separate planning decision (fixture type, switching, dimmer) — see §6's "list first" method.
- **A dedicated makeup/vanity zone needs its own light, switch, and outlet, distinct from mirror lighting elsewhere in the room (added 2026-08-04)** — general room lighting (or even a separate full-length mirror's own light) isn't sufficient for close facial inspection during makeup application; a makeup zone is its own planning item, not assumed to be covered by the mirror-lighting rule above. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

## 18. Shower and Sink Mixers

- **Mount types, shared across shower and sink mixers**: **external/surface-mounted** (cheapest, most reliable, any brand — just two supply pipes exposed and a mixer bolted on; the largest share of the market); **built-in/embedded** (hot/cold mixing happens inside the wall, with separate temperature and flow-control handles on the tile face — modern and clean-looking, but meaningfully more expensive and much less forgiving of installation error; enables routing a ceiling- or wall-recessed rain shower head with no visible arm); **floor-standing** (used specifically for freestanding tubs — either a true floor-mounted mixer, or a mixer mounted on a box built from the same plumbing-framing-profile system described in §5); and **rim-mounted** (installed directly on a tub's own rim — rare, used only when a tub is specifically designed for it and no other mixer type fits the layout).
- **A second, independent source states the black-fixture maintenance downside even more emphatically — recommending against black plumbing fixtures outright**, not just flagging it as a tradeoff to weigh. Worth treating black as a harder "no" by default unless you're prepared for the visible-residue maintenance burden described below.
- **Color options and their real-world maintenance tradeoff**: **chrome** shows the fewest water-spot marks regardless of local water hardness/iron content — the most forgiving finish to maintain visually. **Black** shows every water spot and mark, and a cheap black coating wears visibly within a short time — only worth choosing from a reputable manufacturer with genuine reviews, not a budget unknown brand. **White** hides water spots reasonably well but shows rust staining from iron-heavy water clearly. **Gold/brass tones** (real brass, or PVD coating) are a premium option, priced accordingly.
- **Add-on functions, common to both shower and sink mixers**:
  - **Aerator**: mixes air into the water stream, cutting water usage noticeably versus a plain mixer — a genuine, low-cost water-saving feature.
  - **Thermostatic control**: pre-set a target temperature once; the mixer holds it on every subsequent use, rather than requiring you to re-dial it in each time. Also a real child-safety feature (a child turning on a pre-set thermostatic tap can't accidentally get scalding water) and a comfort/water-saving feature (no need to run water while adjusting temperature). More expensive than a standard mixer — the source specifically recommends it for a shower mixer, less consistently pushed for sink mixers.
  - **Infrared/motion sensor** (touchless activation): saves water but is described as personally irritating in practice by the source (short auto-cutoff duration, hard to get a longer wash in) — installed relatively rarely on this source's own projects; consider carefully rather than defaulting to it for "eco" appeal alone.
  - **Ceiling- or wall-recessed rain shower head**, fed from a built-in mixer: a striking look, and not complex to execute technically, but adds significant cost — cited around **~50,000-60,000 RUB** for a quality unit's full mixer/rain-head assembly. Look for a model with **quick-release, cleanable nozzle plates** (silicone nozzle inserts that unclip for rinsing and reattach) — a genuine longevity/maintenance feature worth prioritizing over the brand name. **A genuine rain shower needs real surface area (ceiling- or wall-mounted, not a small fixture that just "hangs and drips") and its own dedicated water-supply stub-out** — a second source stresses pricing this line item out explicitly before committing, since a genuinely good rain shower head alone can cost more than an entire basic shower cabin.
- **A recessed/built-in mixer with a thermostat needs the wall cavity to be more than ~100-120mm deep.** If a load-bearing wall sits directly behind the planned mixer location, that wall must be furred out to accommodate this — which can shrink the room by as much as ~12cm, since chasing a load-bearing wall to recess the mixer instead isn't permitted. Confirm what's behind the wall before specifying a recessed thermostatic mixer in a tight room.

## 19. Toilet Selection

- **Two mainstream configurations**: **wall-hung (подвесной)** on an in-wall frame/cistern (see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §12 for the frame's own installation requirements) and **floor-standing (напольный)**. A hybrid — a floor-standing bowl plumbed to an in-wall frame — exists but is described as rarely worth choosing over the two mainstream options.
- **Size must be tried in person, not chosen from an online listing alone** — a concrete, actionable warning: a toilet that looks fine on a marketplace listing (right price, right description) can turn out uncomfortably small or large once actually sat on; a toilet is one of the few fixtures worth a physical showroom visit specifically to sit on it before buying, not just to view.
- **An emerging, inconsistent code-enforcement risk worth watching (not yet a settled rule)**: hiding a wall-hung toilet's in-wall frame/cistern inside a "santekhkorob" that also houses water distribution, meters, and a tankless heater has been standard practice for years, but the source reports isolated recent cases of BTI (technical inventory bureau) inspectors objecting to this at final acceptance. `single-account`, Russia-specific (BTI is a Russian institution) — flagged for awareness, not applicable as written to a Belarus project.
- **Toilet clearance**: plan for the average adult's body width (~80 cm), not just the fixture's own footprint, when setting how close a toilet sits to an adjacent wall or fixture.
- **Color**: white is used in ~95% of installs with no real downside; colored options (black most common besides white) work fine but should be chosen deliberately to match the room's overall design concept, not added as an isolated accent.
- **Two add-on functions worth defaulting to**: a **rimless flush design** (no internal rim ledge to trap water/limescale/bacteria — easier to clean and typically more water-efficient) and an **anti-splash bowl geometry** — both are commonly filterable options at point of purchase and add comfort/hygiene with no real downside.
- **"Smart" toilet tiers, cheapest to most expensive**: a **quick-release seat/lid** (two-button detach for cleaning, reattaches with a click) is a low-cost, broadly-recommendable upgrade regardless of anything else. A **bidet-function seat/lid retrofit** (cold water + a dedicated 220V circuit) adds wash/dry functions to an existing toilet at a fraction of a full smart-toilet's cost. A **toilet with bidet function built in** from the factory is pricier still. A **full Japanese-style smart toilet** (heated seat, integrated dryer, remote control, auto open/close) sits at the top of the range with a very wide price spread. **If you already use (or plan to use) a hygienic shower, a built-in bidet function is redundant** — the source explicitly recommends picking one or the other, not both, rather than defaulting to "more features is better."

## 20. Hygienic Shower / Bidet Spray

See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §10 for the core safety mechanism and fix (the "vent-type"/anti-forgetfulness valve design). Two points worth adding here:
- **Always pair a hygienic shower with both a check valve on the manifold and a full leak-protection system** — stated as a near-mandatory pairing, not an optional upgrade, given how routinely hygienic-shower hoses are left pressurized by accident.
- **The anti-forgetfulness valve design exists specifically because manufacturers heard this exact complaint repeatedly** — it's a direct response to a widespread, previously-unsolved failure mode, not a marginal feature; if buying a hygienic shower today, there's little reason to buy a version without it.
- **Built-in (recessed) hygienic-shower valves are more common now than external units** — tell your contractor/plumber explicitly which type you're planning during rough-in pricing, since a quote based on the wrong type (external, cheaper) can otherwise turn into an expensive change order once the correct built-in type is actually installed.

## 20a. Utility Furniture: Pull-Out Columns and Clearance Rules

- **A pull-out (slide-out drawer-style) furniture column is a better solution than a fixed shelf unit for a deep, narrow utility space** (e.g. the leftover space from §8b, or a gap created by the mandatory 3-sided tile-over-tub-rim rule in §16) — it gives comfortable access to the entire depth rather than forcing you to unload items just to reach the back.
- **Build a solid back panel into this kind of column — don't leave it open-through.** A kitchen pass-through cabinet is deliberately accessible from both ends (cooking zone and refrigerator zone), but a bathroom utility column only ever needs access from the room side — the far end sits permanently against a wall behind a door nobody uses from the other side. An open-through design without a back panel just means items get pushed through to the inaccessible far side while reaching in with wet or soapy hands.
- **Leave at least 30mm of clearance between a pull-out unit's carcass and the wall/tile plane it sits flush against**, even when a fully flush look is requested. Door and window casings/trim (commonly ~1cm thick) protrude past the wall plane; a cabinetmaker who doesn't account for this produces a drawer or door that physically stops opening partway once a nearby door is installed. Confirm this clearance is specified before cabinetry is built, not after.

## 21. Sinks

- **A concrete length-by-length sizing table exists — see [[08_WC/WC_Guide|WC Guide]] §2 for the full comparison** (50/60/70/80/90/100cm+, including why 80cm is the best all-around size and why it lines up with a common ~170cm opposite-wall layout). The same table applies to a bathroom vanity sink, not just a WC. **Depth (front-to-back): 40–45cm is the comfortable range** — enough to rest your elbows fully on the counter while washing your face without leaning in far enough to press your forehead against the mirror (a narrow-depth, wide sink is the specific combination that causes this — repeated forehead contact leaves a visible oily mark on the mirror over time).
- **Four mounting types**: **freestanding/pedestal** (floor-mounted on its own leg) — considered obsolete for a full bathroom; occasionally still used purely to save space in a very small WC, but the pedestal itself is functionally useless (no storage) and the source doesn't recommend it as a default anywhere. **Vanity/countertop-mounted on a cabinet** — the most popular, reliable, predictable option, with the widest selection; the one real maintenance downside is that it's typically silicone-sealed to the wall(s) it touches, and if the tub's own overflow/drain siphon needs replacing and the sink sits in the way, the sealed sink may need to come off first (see §14's floor-vs-wall-hung-cabinet note for a way to mitigate this). **Vessel/top-mounted (накладная)** — a popular current trend (round, square, bowl-shaped), but harder to keep clean: the tighter the angle between the sink's outer wall and the countertop surface, the more dust/grime collects in that junction and the harder it is to wipe out. **Undermount (glued underneath the countertop)** — the easiest of the four to keep clean, since there's no rim junction at all; often fabricated as one piece with a custom cast-marble/engineered-stone countertop by the same supplier, a fairly common pairing.
- **A colored or textured (e.g. marble-look) sink finish is a practical choice for a household that's hard on cleanliness, not just an aesthetic one** — it hides water spots, soap residue, toothpaste splatter, and small hair clippings far better than a pure white sink; recommended specifically for a household that won't realistically wipe the sink down after every use.
- **When forced to trade sink width against toilet clearance in a tight room, one experienced practitioner's default is to favor the wider sink** — citing a 70cm toilet clearance as workable in practice against a spacious 100cm+ sink as a clearly worthwhile upgrade. `single-account`, framed by the source itself as a design-taste tradeoff for their own price tier, not a universal rule.

## 22. Towel Warmer

See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §11 for the full case for electric over hydronic (water-based) towel warmers — safety mechanism, a real incident from this same source's own installed work, and cost figures. Placement-relevant summary: **an electric towel warmer has no placement constraint** (any wall, even the ceiling) and costs roughly **~2,500 RUB to install**, versus a hydronic unit's **~10,000-15,000 RUB** (requires the management company to weld shutoff valves into the riser) plus, if you want it covered by leak protection, a dedicated second leak-protection branch on top of that.

## 22a. Above-Washer Storage: Leave a Reachable Gap, Not Just a Deep Cabinet

The space above a washing machine (see §21/§23) is easy to under-use (often just a couple of towel hooks) or over-build as one uniform deep cabinet. **Split it deliberately**: the deep upper section (commonly ~60cm deep) is really only practical for long-term storage, since reaching its back requires a step stool — but **always leave a separate, easily-reachable open shelf or small gap directly above the machine itself**, sized for items used routinely (detergent, etc.) that would be awkward to fetch from deep inside an enclosed cabinet. Skipping this dedicated reachable gap in favor of one uniform deep cabinet is a common, easily-avoided planning miss.

## 23. Washer/Dryer and Water Heater — Placement Clearances

- **Washer/dryer niche width**: for a 60 cm-wide machine, build a 64 cm niche (3–4 cm clearance) — a niche built to the machine's exact nominal width won't actually fit it.
- **Washer/dryer door swing**: the appliance door should be able to open past 90°, ideally to ~120°, or loading/unloading becomes awkward — check this against any adjacent wall/tile before finalizing the niche position.
- **Washer + dryer as separate units**: stack them vertically (a column) where possible — described as the most convenient arrangement when both are separate machines rather than a combo unit.
- **Tankless water heaters must sit at/within the collector node itself** — they're plumbed directly into it. **Storage/tank heaters can be relocated anywhere in the bathroom** (above the toilet, above the washer inside a cabinet, etc.), a real placement-flexibility difference worth factoring into the tank-vs-tankless decision covered in [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]] §7.

## 24. Heated Floor

**Electric underfloor heating, cost and control**: a full setup (heating mat, thermostat, installation) is cited around **~30,000 RUB** — described as easily worth it if the budget allows, with no real downsides beyond cost. Because tile heats and cools slowly, it can't be toggled on-demand like a light switch — the source runs theirs on a schedule (starting ~6am to be comfortably warm by ~9am). **Plan the thermostat's mounting height by how often you'll actually adjust it**: a simple on/off thermostat can go anywhere; a programmable one (set-and-forget) can be low/out of sight; one you expect to adjust frequently should be mounted higher, at a convenient reachable height.

- **Heating-mat area cap and sensor redundancy (added 2026-08-04)**: one practitioner caps underfloor heating mats at **~12 m²** per mat/zone. Each mat should have **3 temperature sensors, not 1** — the sensor itself is described as more likely to fail than the mat, and a failed single sensor leaves no way to control (or safely confirm the state of) an otherwise-working mat. `single-account`, `unverified`. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_business_class_five_attributes_19385e7a.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pro_secrets_lifehacks_f0be401f.txt`]
- **Multi-mat rooms need a multi-loop plan, plus one aggregating controller so occupants don't have to walk between thermostats (added 2026-08-04)**: a room larger than the ~12 m² cap needs either a center-only or perimeter-only mat, or two separate mat loops each with its own thermostat. Worked example: living room = two 12.6 m² mats, kitchen = two 12–14 m² mats, each room with two thermostats. To avoid forcing occupants to operate multiple room thermostats separately, install one aggregating app/controller that lets every room's thermostats be operated as a group from one interface. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_business_class_five_attributes_19385e7a.txt`]

### Thermostat Placement — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10, apartment-wide, not bathroom-specific)

> [!NOTE]
> **A direct tension with the height rule above, not resolved here.** The bullet above (a different, unnamed source) says thermostat mounting height should scale with how often you'll adjust it (low is fine for a set-and-forget unit). Zemskov's rule below is a flat eye-level recommendation for every thermostat, on ergonomic/legibility grounds, and he explicitly rejects the common "mount high so kids can't reach it" child-safety framing as his actual reason. Both are `single-account`; decide based on which reasoning applies to your own household. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_underfloor_heating_thermostat_placement_e9333bb7.txt`]

- **Zemskov's core rule: a thermostat must always be visibly mounted, never hidden** — unlike a light switch (the light itself signals state), a hidden thermostat gives no way to check on/off/temperature without walking to it.
- **Zemskov's WC/bathroom-specific rule: mount the thermostat outside the room it controls, never inside** — lets you check at a glance (before entering, or on the way out of the apartment) whether it was left on. **General heuristic he gives**: place the thermostat on the same side (inside or outside the room) as that room's own light switch.
- **Zemskov's mounting height: 160-170cm (eye level), not waist height and not floor/baseboard level** — his stated reason is that an LCD display (unlike a smartphone screen) is only legible head-on, not at an angle, so a low unit forces crouching to read/operate; he explicitly argues the widely-repeated "mount high so children can't reach it" framing is not his actual reasoning, and separately notes he finds it ineffective in practice (children lose interest in switches quickly).
- **Zemskov's thermostat-type recommendation**: an "electro-mechanical" type (LCD display for information only, physical tactile buttons for control) over a fully mechanical dial (no way to confirm the current setting from a distance) or a fully touchscreen unit (sluggish feel versus a smartphone; his estimate ~98% of clients with a fully-loaded unit use only one function despite paying for the extra complexity).
- **Zemskov's single most emphasized rule: always extend the thermostat's temperature-sensor wire by soldering, never with twist-splices or standard connectors** — a stock sensor wire is too short for eye-level mounting; soldering is, per Zemskov, the one place in a modern renovation he still recommends it, since the joint must repeatedly pass through a dedicated conduit as sensors (unlike heating elements, which he says rarely fail now) do eventually need replacing. **Install that conduit from the thermostat down to the sensor pocket at rough-in stage** — without it, a failed sensor can't be practically replaced later without disturbing the finished floor.

## 25. Toilet Visibility From the Entrance

A pure design/personal-preference point, included for completeness: some people strongly dislike seeing the toilet immediately upon entering and design the layout (sink or shower up front, toilet tucked away) around hiding it; others (including this source, personally) don't consider it worth trading entry convenience for. If this matters to you, it's worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting — the source notes a real case where a client had the door position shifted specifically to avoid a toilet sightline, accepting a less convenient entry angle in exchange.

## 26. Furniture

- Cabinetry material/finish, ranked cheapest to priciest: **LDSP (chipboard) → film-wrapped MDF → plastic-faced MDF → enamel-finished MDF**.
- Avoid natural wood veneer/solid wood in a wet bathroom environment.
- **Edge-banding quality is the real failure point** — damage to the banding or its protective coating lets moisture in and causes rapid furniture failure, echoing the general "don't skimp on the part that actually determines longevity" theme found elsewhere in this project's sources.

## 27. Electrical & Ventilation Cross-References

Bathroom-specific electrical and ventilation content lives on the systems pages, not duplicated here:
- **КУП (potential-equalization/bonding box)** — a bathroom-mandatory grounding circuit for metal fixtures (towel warmer, metal tub/frame, metal shower drain, etc.), distinct from ordinary outlet grounding. See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Electrical_and_Lighting|Electrical & Lighting]] for the general electrical planning content this extends; a full write-up of the КУП box's own bonding list is worth adding there if more electrical-specific sources are processed.
- **Extractor fan**: prefer a forced/powered, quieter model; always add a manual switch inside the bathroom (sometimes you need it on mid-use); consider disabling automatic timer/humidity-sensor triggers in favor of manual control; never block its makeup-air supply (see §15's door-seal warning). See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/HVAC_and_Ventilation|HVAC & Ventilation]].
- **Exhaust fan switching — three recommended wiring patterns, and one hard rule**: the fan's switch must always be located *inside* the room, never in the hallway — a door-open sensor (auto-on, timed auto-off) sounds convenient but is impractical to wire (needs cabling routed under the tile), fails quickly in high humidity, and — more importantly — leaves no way to turn the fan back on from inside without physically opening and closing the door again, an awkward workaround exactly when you'd want it least. **A stronger rule: never let automation be the sole way to turn the fan ON — automation should only ever be used for turning it OFF.** An unexpected, automatically-triggered fan startup is jarring (the same category of problem as a motion-activated light suddenly switching on) and can actively work against what you're doing — e.g. a humidity sensor blasting a fan on during a deliberately steamy bath meant to help a sick child, right when the household is trying to avoid a draft. **Three recommended patterns, and no others**: (1) a simple manual on/off switch inside the room; (2) the fan wired to come on with the light, but with an *additional* independent switch (wall-mounted or on the fan's own housing) so it can run without the light, or be silenced despite the light being on; (3) a push-button "on" paired with a fixed ~60-minute auto-off timer. `single-account`, but a clear, actionable set of options with reasoning, not just a bare preference.
- **Outlets to plan for**: washer, dryer, hair dryer, electric toothbrush/razor charger, robot vacuum (if under a wall-hung vanity), water heater, UPS, leak-protection system, mirror backlight/electric towel warmer, heated floor thermostat (see §24), ceiling lighting, separately-switched extractor fan.
