# Wardrobes & Storage

Covers built-in and walk-in clothing storage design: vocabulary, sizing formulas by configuration (single-sided through four-sided walk-ins, and built-in sliding-door wardrobes), real worked cases (niche creation, opening reconfiguration, access-point placement), common mistakes with real client corroboration, and legal recourse if a furniture contractor's work turns out defective. Format follows [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/_supporting/wiki_page_format|the shared page template]] — narrative sections first, a Quick Reference table at the end.

## 1. Key Concepts — Wardrobe vs. Walk-In Vocabulary

- **Built-in wardrobe** (встроенный шкаф) — storage bounded by the room's own walls; only door panels (typically sliding/"купе") are purchased. Cheaper and more space-efficient than a free-standing unit: floor-to-ceiling use, simpler junction detail (baseboard + bottom rail only), and no side/back/top/bottom panels to buy. `confirmed`, independently stated across two sources in this batch.
- **Free-standing ("corpus") wardrobe** (корпусный шкаф) — a fully enclosed cabinet with its own sides/back/top/bottom, dimensions fixed by the manufacturer, movable as a unit. Not this page's focus, but the baseline the built-in option is compared against throughout.
- **Walk-in closet** (гардеробная) — a dedicated room: a storage-zone run (or several) plus a walk aisle, entered through its own door. Distinct from a wardrobe in that a walk-in's aisle is dead space a plain wardrobe doesn't need — see §2's core sizing tradeoff.
- **Storage zone** (зона хранения) — a run of shelving/hanging rod along one wall inside a walk-in, described by its vertical tiering (see §2.1) and depth (see §2.2).

## 2. Sizing Fundamentals (Apply to Both Walk-Ins and Built-In Wardrobes)

### 2.1 Vertical Tiering

The most practical configuration is **3-tier**: bottom 50cm (shoes), a hanging rod at 2m (clothes), a shelf at 210cm (bulky/seasonal items, luggage). Alternatives are worse:
- **1-tier** (a single rod ~170-200cm): wastes space badly — only appropriate as a temporary measure before real wardrobe furniture is installed.
- **2-tier-both-clothing**: forces same-length garments (shirts/jackets only), the bottom row nearly touches the floor, and the top rod ends up at 220-230cm — hard to reach for shorter people.
- **2-tier shoe+clothes** (rod at ~2m, nothing above 210cm): specifically suits rooms with a ducted-ventilation-unit bulkhead eating ceiling height above 210cm — common in larger/premium apartments, and the reason some rooms have an unusually low usable ceiling in that specific zone.
- **4-tier** (small shoe tier + two clothing rows + a shelf): looks impressive in showroom displays, but is bad in practice — awkward shoe access (buried under hanging clothes), a same-length-garment constraint, and a top rod at 240-250cm.

`confirmed`, `single-account`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_RyHzFDGgqKA_wardrobe_closet_layout_zones|extraction note]]]

### 2.2 Zone Depth

- **Clothing zone: 60cm.** **Shoe zone: 35-40cm, max 45cm — never 60cm.** A 60cm-deep shoe zone invites two-row storage that blocks front-row access, forcing a search through the full depth every time; shoes should sit perpendicular to the wall in a single row instead. `confirmed`, corroborated across two sources in this batch. [sources: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_RyHzFDGgqKA_wardrobe_closet_layout_zones|note]], [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_kj503mBQXq8_small_closet_layout|note]]]
- **Built-in sliding-door wardrobe depth — the single most commonly miscalculated figure on this page.** The naive assumption is that a wardrobe only needs to match the hanging-garment depth itself: ~50-53cm for everyday clothing, ~60-63cm for outerwear. **That's wrong.** Clothing needs a 2-3cm air gap on each side (it shouldn't be pressed against the back wall or brushing the sliding doors), and the sliding-door track/rail system itself consumes roughly 10cm (each panel ~35-40mm thick + ~10mm clearance between panels). **Correct minimum depths: 65-70cm for everyday clothing, 75cm minimum (80cm preferred) for outerwear.** `confirmed`, explicitly flagged by the source as commonly miscalculated. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_VgsPDOcPV7c_sliding_door_wardrobe_sizing|extraction note]]]

## 3. Walk-In Closet Width by Configuration

- **Single-sided minimum: 135cm** = 10cm door-trim standoff + 70cm rough opening (for a 60cm door) + 55cm zone depth (finishes to 60cm). Reducible to **125cm** (drop the standoff, if no adjacent wall-trim conflict) or **120cm** (standoff dropped + zone shortened 45-50cm using 30-40cm shelving instead) with a still-usable 60cm aisle. **Maximum before double-sided applies: 145cm** (150cm allows an opposite 30cm shelf; 175cm+ upgrades to a proper double-sided layout — 55cm aisle + two 60cm zones). `confirmed`, arithmetic-checked. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_RyHzFDGgqKA_wardrobe_closet_layout_zones|extraction note]]]
- **Don't shrink a bedroom to carve out a single-sided walk-in** — a plain wardrobe in the same footprint holds the same clothing volume without losing the ~70cm an aisle costs. **Minimum room length to justify one without sacrificing other function: 5m** (at 3m room width) — shorter rooms should default to a plain wardrobe instead. `confirmed`, worked example. [source: same]
- **Double-sided doors typically swing outward**, except into a narrow corridor, where inward-swing is chosen for safety over comfort.
- **Three-sided minimum leg length: 80cm.** Shorter legs force overlapping zones that become impractically hard to reach. **Overlap counter-clockwise** (right zone placed first, front overlaps it, left overlaps the front) — most people are right-handed, so compressing hung clothes with the stronger hand while retrieving with the free hand is more comfortable; the same logic extends to reaching into far shoe-storage corners. `confirmed`, ergonomic handedness rule. [source: same]
- **Four-sided minimum width: 260cm** (auxiliary zone left) or **280cm** (auxiliary zone right); 300cm+ opens further configurations, more so above 360cm. [source: same]
- **Worked layout for the common ~1.90×1.05m developer-supplied storage room**: only one configuration is arithmetically workable — 60cm zones on both left and right walls with a shallow (~33cm-deep) shoe rack centered at the far end. L-shaped and back-wall-only layouts both fail (leave only 25-45cm for storage after door-opening/aisle allowances are subtracted). `confirmed`, arithmetic-checked. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_kj503mBQXq8_small_closet_layout|extraction note]]]
- **A mirrored walk-in needs a minimum width of 1.7m**: 1m mirror-standing clearance + 60cm clothing zone + 10cm sliding-track allowance. Below that, don't build a walk-in with an internal mirror at all — see §5 for why the mirror can't just be skipped or relocated. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_8odGOTVAuIk_bedroom_mistakes_client_testimonial|extraction note]]]

## 4. Wardrobe vs. Walk-In: The Core Tradeoff

### 4.1 Real Case: Two Wardrobes Beat One Walk-In

A single-sided walk-in's aisle is dead space a plain wardrobe doesn't need. **Real, client-confirmed project**: the client originally wanted a small single-sided walk-in entered from the living room, which would have forced the bed and a door opening to each shift ~700mm. Instead, the practitioner installed **two separate wardrobes sharing one ~100mm-thick dividing wall** — one for outerwear near the entry, one for shirts/blouses/dresses in the bedroom — losing only the 100mm wall thickness total while gaining two full-depth storage runs **and improved inter-room soundproofing** (a wardrobe run acts as an acoustic buffer). The client confirms the outcome positively on camera. `confirmed`, real case + client testimonial — the strongest evidence tier on this page. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_Xh7uwbKVmfA_two_wardrobes_beat_walk_in|extraction note]]]

**Rule of thumb**: before defaulting to a walk-in, check whether two adjoining rooms could each get their own wardrobe against a shared wall instead — especially valuable when the two rooms need acoustic separation anyway (e.g. an entry corridor and a bedroom).

### 4.2 Walk-In Access-Point Placement

**A walk-in attached to a child's bedroom should be entered from a shared hallway, not from inside the child's own room** — entering from the child's room consumes wall space that room could otherwise use for a proper wardrobe (with a full-length mirror door). A closed door already grants the privacy a child might want, regardless of which room the doorway opens from. This is a missed-opportunity case (the client chose the less-optimal option against the practitioner's recommendation), not a rebuilt one — treat as the practitioner's stated general preference. `single-account`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_u28Gqg_V7eE_walkin_access_from_hallway_not_bedroom|extraction note]]]

### 4.3 A Dividing Wall Converting an L-Shaped Corner Into Two Built-In Niches — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

> [!NOTE]
> Real jobsite case, 88.5m² apartment (a recurring project referenced across other Zemstandart videos already in this store), Alexey Zemskov's own account, `single-account`, real cited cost figures. Also folded into `13_Surfaces_and_Finishes/Ceilings_Guide.md`, `13_Surfaces_and_Finishes/Flooring_Guide.md`, and `13_Surfaces_and_Finishes/Doors_and_Trim.md` — this was a single, unusually cross-cutting case. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_entry_hallway_dividing_wall_case_8963951b.txt`]

- **The problem, per Zemskov**: a developer's L-shaped entry hallway, main-wall width 1.56m (finishing to ~1.5m), too narrow for a wardrobe deeper than 40cm without protruding into the door opening — a 40cm-deep wardrobe only fits shoes/light clothing, not coats. Deep storage needs the L-shape's 60cm-deep leg, but per Zemskov that geometry only accommodates a **free-standing ("corpus") wardrobe**, not a built-in — pricier (pays for panels a built-in wouldn't need) and, even with a rounding module to soften the exposed corner, wastes roughly **30% of the wardrobe's usable length** on the purely transitional section, by his figures.
- **Zemskov's fix**: a short (2.16m) dividing wall, cost under 2,000 RUB, converting the L into two separate rectangular niches — letting a built-in sliding-door wardrobe replace the free-standing one, using full ceiling height and losing zero length to a transitional section. He frames this as solving five separate problems (wardrobe cost, ceiling, flooring, baseboard-termination — see the other three pages above) with one cheap structural change.
- **This corroborates, with a specific quantified figure (~30% length loss), this store's existing built-in-vs-corpus wardrobe cost theme** — same underlying tradeoff, now with Zemskov's own real-project waste percentage attached.

## 5. Common Mistakes (Real Client-Corroborated)

From a real client's own finished bedroom, walked through on camera — the strongest evidence tier on this page for the mistakes below:

- **Never install mirrored wardrobe panels where they'd reflect peripheral-vision motion visible from the bed.** Real case: mirrors were installed facing the room instead of inside the closet as designed, causing genuine nighttime discomfort (any movement produced an unsettling "someone moving beside me" effect); corrected to match the original design. **~65% of this practitioner's clients reportedly request matte/frosted panels over mirrored ones** specifically for this reason — self-reported, `unverified` percentage, but consistent with the underlying mechanism being real.
- **A walk-in closet needs its own mirror inside it — never rely on a mirror in another room.** See §3 for the resulting 1.7m minimum-width rule.
- **Scratch-test glass wardrobe-door panels with a fingernail before ordering.** Cheap glass shows permanent scratch marks from ordinary door-opening contact — real client-reported defect on installed glass, from normal fingernail contact with short nails.
- **Never skip soft-close dampers on heavy glass sliding doors.** Without them, a forceful slide can shatter the panel against the end stop. A furniture installer pushed back against including dampers on one project; the practitioner overrode that and kept them.
- **Never use recessed spotlights inside an enclosed mirrored closet.** Even in large numbers, spotlights produce a harsh directional light cone that emphasizes every skin/clothing-fold shadow — use a glowing/luminous ceiling panel instead. Real installed mistake (installer defaulted to spotlights despite the plan) with real client-reported discomfort using the space afterward.
- **TV height is an inherent compromise, not a mistake with a clean fix.** Low enough for comfortable lying-flat viewing looks visually awkward when off (breaks symmetry with flanking millwork); high enough to look intentional forces semi-upright/angled viewing when lying down. No dimension resolves both — a planning caveat to set expectations around.
- **Never place a regularly-used work desk/computer setup inside a bedroom.** Even quiet keyboard use is reported disruptive to a sleeping partner over repeated regular use; acceptable only as a rarely-used decorative surface (e.g. opening a laptop once a month), never a daily workstation. Real client corroboration on camera, plus a stated recurring-complaint pattern across other clients (`single-account` for the recurrence claim).

`confirmed` throughout, client-corroborated on camera. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_8odGOTVAuIk_bedroom_mistakes_client_testimonial|extraction note]]]

## 6. Real Worked Cases: Making Room Where None Exists

### 6.1 Recessing a Wall to Create a Built-In Niche

Real project, 91m² apartment: recessed an entry-zone wall inward (72-78cm) to create a niche exactly sized for a built-in wardrobe with sliding doors — turning what would otherwise require a bulky free-standing unit into a flush built-in. **Side benefit**: the recess also converts an exposed exterior corner into a protected one once boxed with the wardrobe door front. When this interacts with an adjacent balcony/loggia opening, the opening may need repositioning to keep the wardrobe's sliding-door track plane clear — this project shifted the balcony opening -18cm on one side / +34cm on the other, preserving an 85cm clear opening. **Planning-discipline principle stated explicitly**: precompute the sliding-door rail width and wall-junction detail independent of which side the door panel will ultimately occupy — described as what separates a fully-engineered project from a designer's rendering. `confirmed`, real dimensioned case. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_n0O47DdhATw_builtin_wardrobe_niche_technique|extraction note]]]

### 6.2 Reconfiguring a Window/Balcony Opening

Real project, 79.5m² apartment: **bed and TV placement should be locked in before wardrobe placement, not after** — once the two highest-priority fixed elements (bed against the only workable wall, TV opposite it) are set, remaining wall space for storage may require reconfiguring the room's own window or balcony opening. Worked dimensioned sequence: offset 700mm from the right wall, +800mm to set the new opening width, offset 500mm from the left wall for window width, raise the balcony threshold to exactly 100mm above the screed, form a radiator niche 850mm down from the ceiling sized 5mm larger than the radiator grille's actual **back-mount** dimensions (not its visible decorative face — e.g. a 1000×600mm grille needs a 1005×605mm niche). Result: a 700mm-wide space fits a 600mm-deep wardrobe with clearance to spare against the window reveal. `confirmed`, precise dimensioned sequence. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_CHCB4KPupyc_bedroom_window_reconfig_for_wardrobe|extraction note]]]

**Bonus — bedroom electrical layout formula from the same source**: center the bed on the room's horizontal midline; symmetric independently-switched bedside lights on each side of the headboard; at each nightstand, group a light switch, a through-switch for the main room light, and 1-2 sockets; project the bed's centerline onto the opposite wall to center the TV on the same axis. Always add at least two outlets inside the balcony/window reveal at the final design step.

## 7. If a Contractor's Work Turns Out Defective

This is Russian consumer-protection law as explained by a named guest lawyer in one source — **not an independently verified citation of the actual statute text**. Treat the mechanism/process as plausible and worth knowing, but verify the specific percentages against the current law before relying on them in a real dispute.

- **Two legal-expertise categories determine what can be claimed**: **товароведческая экспертиза** (product/goods expertise) applies to a free-standing furniture purchase; **строительно-техническая экспертиза** (construction-technical expertise) applies when the item is built-in and its removal affects the apartment's own interior finishing — the latter additionally allows claiming finish-restoration costs a goods-only claim wouldn't cover.
- **The other party must be formally notified of a scheduled expert inspection's date/time in advance** (e.g. via registered telegram) to preserve their documented right to attend — skipping this lets them later contest the report's validity in court.
- **Recommended sequence**: engage a lawyer first (to identify contract type — work vs. goods, since the law treats these differently) → determine built-in vs. free-standing → formulate specific questions for the formal expert examination → notify the other party → conduct the examination → calculate the claim.
- **An individual entrepreneur (IP) in Russia is personally liable with their own assets** — ceasing IP status doesn't extinguish an existing debt, which can be pursued against the person privately instead. Relevant when evaluating recourse against a small IP-structured contractor specifically.
- If unresolved via a pre-court settlement demand, Russian consumer-protection law reportedly adds a **50% penalty on the combined goods/damages/moral-harm total**, plus a **3%/day contractual penalty (неустойка)** on the contract value, capped at a stated maximum — `unverified` against the actual statute text, single secondhand account.

[source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_pVMvsuhrwWs_furniture_dispute_legal_process|extraction note]]]
