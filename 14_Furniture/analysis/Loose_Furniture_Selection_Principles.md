# Loose Furniture — Selection Principles

## Scope

The cross-cutting rules for judging any freestanding piece — sofa, bed, table or chair — **before** getting to its category-specific dimensions. Legs, junctions, visual weight, proportion, and the compositional reasons a piece reads "designed" or doesn't.

Category detail lives on the companion pages: [[14_Furniture/analysis/Seating_Selection|Seating Selection]] and [[14_Furniture/analysis/Table_Types_and_Dimensions|Table Types & Dimensions]].

Created 2026-08-30 from three sources in the Shevrina/SMBUREAU Round 2 batch — the first loose-furniture content in this vault, which previously held only wardrobe and storage pages.

> [!NOTE]
> **All content on this page is `single-account`, from one studio (Мария Шеврина / SMBUREAU).** Three separate videos, so the rules are consistently held rather than offhand — but this is one practitioner's doctrine, not a corroborated body of practice. The three source videos are largely brand-by-brand surveys of the **Russian** furniture market; **none of the per-model verdicts were extracted**, only the reasoning underneath them, which is market-independent.

## Legs

The single most repeated topic across all three sources, and the rule got sharper each time.

- **⚠️ Splayed legs ("ножки в растопырку") — reject.** Stated in all three videos, most forcefully as "самый ужасный элемент любых предметов мебели." The practical objection is consistent and physical: **you strike your little toe on them.** On tables it is worse when the legs extend past the tabletop edge — "как будто стол вам пытается подставить подножку" — because you cannot see them as you approach.
- **⚠️ The precise version, from the chairs video: the objection is to legs raked in *two* directions at once.** "Не только под углом назад, но ещё и вбок — **назад было бы достаточно**." A backward rake alone is fine and normal on a chair; adding a sideways splay is what breaks it. **This is the usable form of the rule** — the blanket "no angled legs" version she uses elsewhere is an overstatement of her own position.
- **⚠️ Tapered legs (narrowing toward the floor) — reject.** "Совершенно бесполезный элемент, лишняя работа, которая ведёт к дисгармонии" when a cylindrical leg would do the same job. Their studio does not specify them. **Prefer cylindrical.**
- **⚠️ Legs must be visually anchored, not "growing from nowhere."** Her named failure is legs meeting the seat at arbitrary points — "растущими непонятно откуда, просто рандомно… ни к чему не привязаны и растут вникуда." **The leg rake should coincide with the angle of the back.** Without that relationship the piece reads as "винегрет из деталей, которые не связаны друг с другом."

## Why a Piece Reads "Designed"

**⚠️ Her strongest general claim: an attractive piece is never accidental** — "он не может быть красивым ни с того ни с сего." She names the causes, and they are checkable against anything in a showroom:

- **Соосность — coaxial alignment** of elements to each other.
- **Repetition of one form at several scales.** Her worked positive example is a modular sofa whose seat, arms and back are visibly the *same* element at different sizes, with identical radii and identical forms throughout.
- **Deliberate scale relationships between details** — or a deliberate *contrast* instead. Either is a decision; neither being present is the failure.
- **⚠️ Equal corner radii at every junction of the piece.** Unequal radii within a single item is her named marker of unconsidered design.
- **Junction quality (узлы сопряжения деталей)** as the practical tell: visible glue lines, an unresolved corner where an armrest turns into a leg, an unshaped seat.

## Visual Weight — the Lever That Actually Controls Room Size

**⚠️ Contrast of forms is the specific mechanism.** A **thick leg or base under a thin top reads light**; when both are the same thickness the piece reads massive and clutters the room. She works this in both directions across named examples, and notes one table stays visually light *despite* being large purely because of this contrast.

**⚠️ The counter-intuitive corollary, and the most useful single idea here: a short but bulky piece is visually heavier than a longer slim one.** People buy a compact sofa *because* the room is small, and it makes the room read smaller. Her stated mechanism is **high seat height plus disharmonious side-proportions**, and her fix is specific: **add legs at the same overall sitting height** — this lowers the cushion mass relative to the floor and lightens the piece without costing seat height. She notes the same model can look elegant in its larger configuration and wrong in the compact one.

Massive chairs are disproportionate in a small room by the same logic, and she notes viewers independently reported the same impression.

## Off-Vertical Elements Clash With the Room

**⚠️ A compositional mechanism rather than a taste verdict, and the best-argued item in the sofa source.** Slightly angled headboards and bed sides look harmless in a catalogue photo. But a real room is full of true verticals — **door openings, wall panels, decorative reveals, track lighting, a floor lamp, a pendant.** Set a slightly-leaning element beside those and the mismatch reads as an error rather than a style.

Her prescription follows from it: **prefer 90° geometry on large pieces**, and treat any deliberate lean as something that has to survive comparison with everything vertical already in the room.

Related form rule: **don't mix rectangular and round geometry within one piece** — semicircular variants of otherwise-rectangular models "выглядят, знаете, как Котопёс."

## Finish and Fabric

- **The same frame can be cheapened or lifted by its fabric.** Prefer a textured weave with a visible stitch (рогожка) or a velour with slight tonal variation over a flat monotone.
- **⚠️ But for a piece meant to stay current indefinitely, avoid decorative topstitching along the seams** — it adds period specificity to an otherwise neutral form.
- **A cheap light-wood piece betrays itself through construction**: to hit the price the seat is glued up from many small offcuts, which reads untidy in light timber. **The same model in a dark finish hides it** and becomes specifiable.
- **A top surface finished in a different shade from its base** reads "наигранно, искусственно и не архитектурно — как будто просто какой-то пластик наклеили."
- **⚠️ Saturated colours lower the perceived status of an interior.** Consistent with the same practitioner's position on bright accents in [[17_Design_and_Ergonomics/analysis/Decor_and_Finish_Selection_Technique|Decor & Finish Selection Technique]].

## "Детскость" — the Disqualifier She Names Most Often

**A childish note in a piece disqualifies it from an adult living or dining zone** — fine in a kids' room, wrong in a restrained interior. She raises this as a characteristic weakness of the Russian furniture market specifically, in both the sofa and chair videos: a shortage of "обычных брендов для взрослых серьёзных людей."

Useful as a naming of something buyers often feel but can't articulate about a piece that is otherwise fine on paper.

## Mixing In Something Older

Her stated position on adding one non-minimalist element to a restrained interior: **make it mid-century / Bauhaus.** Argument — those pieces have not dated across decades of her own observation (~15 years personally). Named archetypes rather than SKUs: the Wassily chair, Pierre Jeanneret's chair and its interpretations, the CH series. *(Names came through poor ASR and should be verified before sourcing anything.)*

**⚠️ Attached caution: a period chair alone does not deliver the look.** It needs supporting elements from the same period — period lighting, matching low tables, and **more glass with chromed metal**. A single vintage piece in an otherwise plain room reads as an orphan.
