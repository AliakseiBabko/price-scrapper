## What a Pantry Is For, and a Named Common Mistake

A pantry stores cleaning supplies/chemicals, luggage/bags, professional
tools, small appliances (vacuum, iron, steamer), and even a stepladder.
Zlobin flags a common real mistake he sees repeatedly, including in
professional architects' own floor plans: **private-house designs
frequently omit a pantry entirely**, because post-Soviet clients and
architects both grew up in apartments — where pantry space genuinely
doesn't exist as a category — and unconsciously carry that
apartment-scale planning habit into a house that actually has the room
for one. (This project's own plan is itself apartment-based, so the
mistake pattern doesn't apply directly, but the sizing/planning content
below still generalizes to apartment kitchen-adjacent storage.)

## Minimum Pantry Area — a Cited Reference Figure

**A real cited external source, not just this architect's own
convention**: Ernst Neufert's *Architects' Data* recommends allocating
**at least 2% of a house's useful/livable area** (excluding corridors,
halls, and stairs) to pantry space — e.g. a 100m² useful-area house
should have at least 2m² of total pantry space, which can be distributed
across several smaller pantries rather than concentrated in one room.

## Don't Substitute an Attic, Mezzanine, or Basement

A pantry's defining function is **frequent, low-friction access** (an
item like an iron needs to be reachable "almost every day"), which an
attic/mezzanine/basement doesn't provide — storage location should match
access frequency, not just wherever there's spare volume.

## The "Leftover-Space" Design Method

A repeatable technique for fitting a pantry into an already-drawn floor
plan: slightly shrink and/or shift an adjacent room (a real worked
example: an oversized shared bathroom) to carve out a pantry-sized
sliver, entered from a hallway or an adjacent room — finds pantry space
without adding new square footage.

## Ventilation

Desirable but not mandatory for a general pantry (the same rule applies
to walk-in closets/dressing rooms) — a door-undercut gap usually suffices
for supply air; if too narrow, add a bottom door vent cutout or
decorative grille. Exhaust: either a dedicated vent duct to the roof, or
a compromise grille opening into the hallway. **A concrete caution
against defaulting to a dedicated duct**: up to roughly one-third of a
house's total heat loss can occur through natural ventilation pathways,
especially in central/northern Russia's climate — often not worth that
cost for "such a modest, leftover-space room"; southern climates are more
forgiving of a dedicated duct.

## Kitchen-Adjacent Pantry — Two Functions, Three Planning Types

Now considered standard (was a "novelty" as recently as the mid-2010s per
this source). **Two named functions**: (1) it **unloads the kitchen** —
functioning as a second kitchen for storage purposes at a fraction of the
footprint (a worked comparison: folding a 5m kitchen-cabinetry run down
to 1/2-1/3 its length in a dedicated pantry yields comparable storage
capacity for far less area); (2) it **improves kitchen-zone layout
flexibility**, especially as a **pass-through pantry** creating an
alternate entry-to-kitchen route (useful for unloading groceries without
a full walk-through of the house in shoes/outerwear).

**Three planning types**:

1. **Pantry as part of the kitchen furniture run** — adjacent to (not
   necessarily flush with) the cabinetry, visually screens the kitchen
   corner, can include a freezer for longer-term frozen storage. Keep
   this type's microclimate somewhat cooler than the rest of the house;
   ventilation is explicitly recommended (not just "desirable") here,
   scaling up with room size and the specific foods stored.
2. **Pantry as a planning/zoning tool** — its own wall/volume positioned
   between the kitchen and an adjoining living/dining space specifically
   to create a physical/visual division, entry accessible from either
   side.
3. **Pass-through pantry** — the fullest expression of the layout-
   flexibility function (see above), positioned off the entry so
   groceries route straight to the kitchen or bypass toward a wardrobe/
   guest WC.

## Storage Combinatorics — Corroborates the Existing Walk-In-Closet Taxonomy

**⚠️ Directly corroborates, from an independent topic angle,** this
page's own [[14_Furniture/analysis/Walkin_Closet_Configurations|Walk-In
Closet Configurations]] four-way taxonomy (there sourced from clothing-
closet content) — this source independently derives the **same four-way
geometric taxonomy** for general pantry shelving, framed as "pure
geometry" giving only four possible configurations regardless of what's
stored: **one-sided, two-sided, L-shaped, and U-shaped** (the last
reserved for larger pantries needing at least 90cm clearance between
opposing shelf runs, 100cm recommended, for an adult to turn around).
**Shelf-depth guidance**: 25cm minimum, 30-40cm the better working
range, 40cm+ crosses into heavy-duty warehouse-style shelving more
suited to a garage/workshop than a household pantry.

## Pantry Entry-Point Placement (Cross-Referenced, Same Batch)

A companion source from the same batch (a bedroom/guest/kids-room
practice video, not a pantry-focused one) independently flags a related
rule worth recording here: **the entry point into a pantry matters as
much as its gross floor area** — entering a small under-stair pantry
from its narrow/cornered end can leave as little as ~1-1.2m of genuinely
usable depth, while a centered entry point splits the same footprint
into two independently usable storage zones. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uJ6l8t5TpwY_zlobin_guest_kids_practice_11x9house|YT_uJ6l8t5TpwY]]]
