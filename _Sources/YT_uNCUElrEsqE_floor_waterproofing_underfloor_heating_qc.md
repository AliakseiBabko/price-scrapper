## Durable Facts

- **Waterproofing membrane sheet orientation matters, not just seam overlap**: rolled bitumen/felt-backed waterproofing sheets have one edge with the felt backing removed (a bare strip meant for overlapping the next sheet). This bare-edge strip must face **toward the wall**, not run down the middle of a floor span — placing the first sheet the wrong way around forces the crew to overlap two bare (non-felted) edges against each other mid-floor, creating a long unsealed seam ("прогон") that defeats sound-insulation across the whole run. **Checkable defect**: walk each seam looking for a visible "dip" — the unsealed section is thinner and visibly lower than the felted areas around it.
- **Waterproofing integrity check method**: the membrane is treated as a "basin" that should hold standing water indefinitely. QC method: manually walk every seam and every overlap pocket and gently try to lift/peel the edge with a tool (not fingernail). If a pocket **doesn't** tear/lift further than a small existing flap, it's properly bonded — leave it. If it tears open further (revealing an unglued dry patch), it's a real defect requiring a patch.
- **Patch technique for a torn/unbonded waterproofing seam**: heat the substrate spot and the patch piece **separately** first, then press them together once both are hot, then go back and heat-seal the patch's perimeter edge with a torch. Anti-pattern the speaker calls out: crews often just heat the perimeter and smear it for looks without properly melting the base contact — do not accept that shortcut. Patches should stay **small**, because a large patch cannot be reheated evenly before it cools, causing poor adhesion.
- **Longitudinal sheet-to-sheet seams (mid-run splices) require the same bare/felt-stripped edge treatment as sheet ends**: where a full sheet doesn't reach corner-to-corner and installers splice along the sheet's length, the felt must be manually cut away from that edge before bonding (not just overlapped with felt-to-felt), and the resulting seam should be visually indistinguishable (monolithic) from a factory seam. These mid-length splices are called out as the most error-prone spot to inspect.
- **Waterproofing must NOT be applied around drain stacks/risers ("стояки") outside wet rooms**: a deliberate exception — the area immediately around plumbing stacks is left unsealed on purpose, so that if a neighbor upstairs floods, water flows straight down through that gap to the floor below rather than pooling and eventually breaching sideways into the unit's own living space. Framed as a specific, non-obvious rule: "never waterproof the area around stacks — leave it open for water to pass through."
- **Waterproofing membrane height vs. finished screed level**: waterproofing must extend **above** the planned final screed level (not flush with it), so it can be trimmed down afterward and sealed with an acoustic sealant at the finished-floor line. The speaker sets the target screed level using a laser level referenced from the building entry-door threshold (goal: interior finished floor matches the building corridor floor level, avoiding a step at the apartment's front door) — a specific, reusable technique for determining target screed height in this speaker's practice.
- **Wet-room (bathroom) waterproofing must be fully heat-bonded to walls**, unlike other areas of the same floor where wall-bonding is treated as more optional — in wet rooms the membrane should be melted enough to become "part of the wall," fully sealed around the entire perimeter including where it meets load-bearing structure.
- **Underfloor heating manifold ("коллектор") placement**: must be permanently fixed at its final designed location before pipe runs are laid — never placed "temporarily," because any later repositioning disturbs every connected pipe run. If there's no natural mounting point, build a dedicated frame to fix it securely.
- **Heating pipe routing rules**: pipes should run parallel to main walls, bend gently without kinks, with a bend radius of roughly **0.5 m**, and pipes should be positioned/documented (photographed) precisely so their location can be found later without a thermal camera (useful since pipes are only thermally visible when hot water is running, e.g. not in summer) — protects against accidentally drilling into a pipe later (e.g. when installing a door stop) and, if a pipe is ever punctured, lets the crew know exactly where to open the screed to fix it locally.
- **Pipe sleeving**: heating pipes should be wrapped in a protective flex sleeve along their entire length (with a bit of extra allowance), with correctly executed/pressed fittings, and using name-brand fittings (the transcript names REHAU) rather than cheaper alternatives — framed as a "no analogs" category, echoing pipe-material-selection caution already present in this project's Plumbing_and_Waterproofing page.
- **Pipe anchoring to the sub-floor — a specific product recommendation**: speaker states a strong preference for older-style **bitumen adhesive strips** (heated with a heat gun to bond) over newer **peel-and-stick adhesive strips**, describing the newer strips as difficult to apply properly and costing roughly **5x more** for no real benefit. `unverified`, single-account product opinion, but stated as a strong, specific preference from someone claiming years of hands-on installation experience with both.
