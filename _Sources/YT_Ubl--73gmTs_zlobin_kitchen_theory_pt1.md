## Rooms / Zones — Kitchen

- **Kitchen needs the shortest practical path from the entry (front
  hallway, or a distribution hall in a larger house) to its own storage** —
  the stated reason is carrying grocery bags from the car/entrance to
  fridge/pantry/cabinets as quickly as possible; this drives both the
  kitchen's placement in a floor plan and the internal placement of
  storage elements within the kitchen itself.
- **Historical framing, useful context for the open-plan trend below**:
  the kitchen evolved from a hidden, service-only room (19th century,
  deliberately isolated from a home's formal circulation) to today's
  three-stage evolution — kitchen+dining+living as three separate rooms
  with a "see-through" connection → dining room absorbed into the kitchen
  (dining table moves into the kitchen itself, formal dining time
  shrinking) → living room also absorbed, producing the now-familiar
  studio/open-plan kitchen-living-dining structure. Source attributes the
  shrinking formal-dining-room stage specifically to less time spent on
  table service/eating rituals in modern daily life.
- **Kitchen size floor and range (Neufert-sourced, house context)**:
  minimum kitchen area **8m²**; for a 150-200m² house, target **12-20m²**
  (20m² if the kitchen includes the dining table, 12m² if dining is
  elsewhere). **Minimum comfortable single-row cabinet run length: 7m** —
  below that, function starts getting compressed.
- **⚠️ Functional-use time breakdown for a kitchen (cited as originally
  Neufert-era literature, source explicitly flags it as dated and revises
  it live)**: storage ~20%, food-prep/cutting (needs open countertop —
  "рабочая поверхность") ~25%, dishwashing ~20%, heat-treatment
  (cooking/baking) ~20%, eating ~15%. **Reasoning point drawn from
  these numbers: prep-surface time (25%) exceeds eating time (15%), yet
  most non-professionals design a kitchen by prioritizing the dining
  table's size/appearance first and treat prep-surface planning as an
  afterthought — the source argues this is backwards.**
- **⚠️ Nuance/tension flagged explicitly by the source, not fully
  reconciled**: the ~20% dishwashing figure above is called dated —
  modern lifestyle (less home cooking, more delivered/takeout food) is
  argued to have shifted the balance so that **dishwashing now
  outweighs cooking time** in practice, since a dishwasher lowers
  dishwashing time somewhat but delivered food raises its *share* of
  total kitchen activity even more. Recorded as the source's own stated
  reasoning, not an independently verified study — `single-account`,
  `unverified`.
- **Work triangle (fridge-sink-stove), side length rule**: each side of
  the triangle should be **no more than 1.5m** for maximally efficient
  food-prep movement. Food/task flow through the triangle, explicit
  sequence: fridge (raw storage) → prep surface → sink (wash/prep) → prep
  surface → stove (cook) → table (serve) → back to fridge (leftovers) —
  every side of the triangle doubles as a prep-surface run, not just a
  connector.
- **Single-row layout, access-order rule from the entry point**: fridge
  first (closest to entry, for the grocery-carry reason above), then sink
  (per the triangle), then stove.
- **Two-row (galley) layout, corner-avoidance rule**: don't put the sink
  in the corner nearest the kitchen's entry door — it concentrates traffic
  (someone washing dishes blocks people entering/exiting) — free that
  corner for the fridge instead, and place the sink further down the run.
- **U-shaped kitchen note**: functionally the same triangle logic as the
  two-row layout; source flags that swapping sink and stove position
  (relative to a default arrangement) can sometimes leave more usable
  countertop, calling it a minor optimization rather than a hard rule.
- **⚠️ Window-placement debate for the work zone, generalizable design
  tension**: traditional guidance (Neufert) puts the **prep surface**
  under the window (max natural light for fine hand-work/visual tasks);
  the **current trend** instead puts the **sink** under the window — the
  source frames this as a real, unresolved stylistic shift rather than
  endorsing one side, and separately notes (see Regulations/Planning Rules
  below) that this trend has a real infrastructure cost (radiator
  relocation).
- **Kitchen island, sizing and function**: an island can increase total
  usable prep surface by **up to 25%**. **Minimum clearance from island to
  the main cabinet run: 1m, ideally ≥1.2m** for a genuinely comfortable
  island. Best-performing shapes per the source's own experience: an
  elongated island, or — the source's favorite — a **semi-circular
  island**, which fits the most functions plus informal seating in one
  footprint. An island doesn't need to be single-function (e.g. sink-only)
  — adding 1-2 informal seats lets a household with staggered morning
  schedules (e.g. early-leaving parents/kids vs. later-rising
  grandparents) grab a fast breakfast without using the full formal dining
  table, which the source frames as poorly suited to fast/casual eating
  because of the longer serve-and-clear round trip it requires.
- **Peninsula as an island alternative**: an L-shaped run's open end can
  form a peninsula (полуостров) carrying the sink or another triangle
  element, without a fully freestanding island.
- **Bar-counter trend note, dated observation**: elevated bar counters
  with bar stools and a small dedicated bar fridge were popular with
  younger clients roughly 10-15 years before this video (i.e. around the
  2000s-2010s); the source states clients have essentially stopped asking
  for this in the ~5 years before this video's 2020 upload — framed as an
  observed client-request trend, not a technical recommendation either
  way. Low reuse value as a design *rule*, but useful as a dated market
  signal.

## Materials / Furniture / Built-ins — Cabinet Dimensioning

- **Base cabinet run depth: 60cm is described as an unspoken universal
  standard** ("негласный стандарт") that the industry defaults to.
- **⚠️ Base cabinet/countertop height, domestic vs. international
  standard, a genuinely new figure for this store**: domestic (source's
  own, ex-USSR/Russian-market) standard ≈ **80-85cm**; international/
  Western standard ≈ **90-93cm**. Source recommends knowing which
  standard a given manufacturer/order follows before finalizing a
  kitchen order, since built-in appliances and accessory hardware are
  often sized to one standard or the other.
- **Upper (wall-hung) cabinets**: mounted **~55cm above the base
  cabinet/countertop** on average; own height **~60cm**; depth **35cm**
  (deeper is possible up to ~60cm but explicitly not recommended by the
  source).
- **⚠️ Window-sill-height coordination rule**: a window's sill is
  conventionally set at 90cm from finished floor, which conflicts with an
  80-93cm-high countertop — recommend raising the sill to **100cm** to
  clear the countertop cleanly and avoid needing to trim the interior
  windowsill board (подоконная доска) where it would otherwise collide
  with the countertop's depth.
- **Kneading/dough-work surface height, flagged as a fading requirement**:
  a standard 80-93cm countertop is too high for comfortable kneading
  (needs top-down body-weight pressure); where this is a real priority,
  lower a dedicated section to **~70-75cm**, or use a pull-out lowered
  work surface instead of lowering the whole run. Source explicitly frames
  this as a legacy requirement few modern households actually need.
- **Design posture rationale for all the height figures above**: the
  stated goal behind every cabinet-height number is letting the cook stand
  with a straight back at all times — too-low a counter forces stooping,
  too-high forces standing on toes; both are framed as the real ergonomic
  failure the height standards exist to prevent.

## Plumbing / Regulations / Planning Rules — Infrastructure

- **Two independent vent ducts required for a kitchen (natural/passive
  ventilation context)**: one for general room ventilation (since the
  kitchen counts as a "wet" room type due to the sink) and a **separate**
  one for the range-hood exhaust — **never route the hood exhaust into
  the general ventilation duct**, called a common, "crude" mistake that
  strips the room of its natural ventilation. **This corroborates, from an
  independent theory-focused source, the same never-combine-hood-and-
  general-exhaust rule already well-documented on
  [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Fresh_Air_Ventilation_and_Ducting|Fresh-Air Ventilation & Ducting]]**
  (ADS-vent, house-ventilation-explainer, and Zemstandart sources) — not
  genuinely new content, recorded here as a fifth/sixth independent
  confirmation.
- **⚠️ Radiator-relocation consequence of the sink-at-window trend, new
  content not yet on this store's heating pages**: if the sink (rather
  than the prep surface) sits under the window per the modern layout
  trend above, the radiator — which code/norms conventionally place under
  the window — can no longer go there and must move to a side wall
  instead. Consequences: (1) a side-wall radiator needs a **higher rated
  output** than a standard under-window unit, since it's no longer
  directly countering the window's own cold-air draft the same way; (2)
  the source states a radiator should never be tucked inside the kitchen
  cabinet run itself — its efficiency drops sharply and it "won't work"
  properly, in addition to risking the cabinetry. Underfloor heating and
  convectors are named as alternatives, with the same underfloor-heating
  whole-floor-consistency caveat this store already records elsewhere
  (mixing underfloor heat in the kitchen with radiators in adjoining
  rooms on the same floor is called impractical).
- **Drain/stack adjacency rule, plumbing**: kitchen sink drainage uses a
  **50mm pipe**, with a steeper minimum slope-per-length requirement than
  a 100mm pipe — in practice this limits how far a sink can sit from the
  vertical stack regardless of screed thickness. **Grouping the kitchen
  adjacent to a bathroom/WC (sharing the same stack) is called a
  functionally correct choice for drainage even though it's aesthetically
  awkward** (exiting a WC directly into a kitchen-dining view); boiler
  rooms and shower rooms are named as similarly good functional
  neighbors for the same water/drain-proximity reason.
- **Orientation note, low-confidence/no reasoning given**: kitchen
  daylight orientation "should be" northeast or northwest, per a single
  passing remark at the very end of the video with no supporting
  mechanism stated — recorded as `single-account`, `unverified`, not
  elevated to a rule.
