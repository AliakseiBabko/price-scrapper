## Planning Rules / Budget — Designing for Resale/Rental "Liquidity" (Flip and Rental-Investment Segment)

- **⚠️ Core reframe: for a flip or rental-investment property, the design objective is deliberately *not* a personally expressive or "cozy" interior — it's the widest possible buyer/tenant pool.** The guest states this explicitly as the central rule distinguishing investment-property design from a client's own home: prioritize a universal, flexible layout with maximum storage over any personal aesthetic statement, because the property must appeal to as broad a demographic as possible (a young family, a single tenant, or an older couple should all be able to picture themselves living there).
- **⚠️ Named list of choices that concretely narrow the buyer pool and reduce liquidity, stated as near-certain value destroyers**: a non-functional layout (odd leftover nooks, too few sleeping spaces, too little storage — flagged as *worse* for liquidity than finish quality, because a bad layout only becomes obvious once a prospective buyer/tenant is physically on-site, not from listing photos); strongly saturated/characterful color choices named directly (**red bathtub, orange kitchen, exposed ceiling beams**); a dark overall interior (reduces buyer appeal both because dark spaces photograph poorly for online listings and because retrofitting more/better lighting into an already-finished interior is described as "practically impossible" — electrical routing can't be meaningfully redone after the fact); complex/expensive finish joinery and premium-branded materials chosen for their own sake rather than because the segment calls for them.
- **⚠️ Named technique for making a resale interior "read as expensive" without actually spending on expensive materials**: (1) warm-toned, natural-look textures (wood-look, not necessarily real wood); (2) if color is used at all, a complex/muted/desaturated tone that resists being named as a single pure hue (the guest's own example: a color you can't decide is "blue or green" reads as more expensive than a clean saturated color); (3) minimal, deliberately restrained glossy accents — a small amount in light fixtures, hardware, or décor only, not broad glossy surfaces; (4) an explicit "less is more" restraint principle, phrased as an analogy: an interior wearing "every piece of jewelry at once" (multiple loud finishes/patterns/accents layered together) reads as cheap/showy regardless of the individual materials' real cost, the same way a person over-accessorized with every piece of jewelry looks worse than someone wearing one well-chosen piece.
- **⚠️ Layered lighting is presented as the highest-leverage low-cost lever for perceived value** — explicitly offered as a substitute for spending on expensive materials: multiple light levels/zones read as more "put-together" and expensive than a single central fixture, and (per the point above) is disproportionately important for resale specifically because a dark listing photographs and shows badly and can't be fixed after the renovation is finished.
- **⚠️ Named concrete listing-photo mistake, stated as a common real-world failure the guest sees repeatedly on rental/resale listings (Avito)**: hanging a mismatched leftover decor item (her own example — an old picture in an ill-fitting frame, wrong style, too small) on an otherwise large, undecorated wall — described as actively worse than leaving the wall bare, and named as one of the worst things she regularly sees on resale/rental listings.
- **A softer, non-absolute companion rule**: avoid dark interior doors specifically for a liquidity-oriented project (keep doors light); baseboards/casings should still be present and deliberately finished (visible trim, not concealed) even on a cost-constrained project — a budget project can skip a concealed-door treatment to save cost, but the visible trim choice must still look intentional, not skipped.

## Planning Rules / Budget — Design-Fee-vs-Real-Cost Budget Mismatch, a Second Independent Case

- **⚠️ Real named case, directly analogous to Round 2's large-format-tile budget-mismatch finding (`-XuTH1e-Fc0`), from an unrelated project/material**: a Moscow-based designer told the guest her own quoted turnkey renovation rate for a 70 m² Moscow apartment was **≈3,000 RUB/m² (≈$40/m², trailing-6-month USD/RUB conversion), inclusive of demolition and rough (черновые) materials, full apartment, per the design project** — a rate the guest immediately flags as arithmetically incompatible with real 2026 St. Petersburg/Moscow-market renovation costs for that scope. The stated root cause: the quoting designer changed only the *finish specification* in the project (e.g. substituting cheaper polymer trim for natural gypsum molding) while never re-checking whether the underlying labor/preparation scope (wall leveling, ceiling type, demolition volume) had changed at all — it hadn't, so the quoted price silently assumed the same minimal labor scope regardless of what was actually specified.
- **General mechanism, stated as the interview's own explicit generalization of this failure**: a design project's price commonly moves in the client's head only when the *visible* material choice changes (e.g. "natural plaster molding" become "cheaper polymer molding"), while the much larger real cost driver — the underlying labor/preparation work required by the overall design (uneven walls, ceiling type, plumbing/electrical relocations) — stays fixed in the estimator's assumptions regardless of what's actually drawn. The guest's own explicit fix: designers should consult builders during the concept/floor-plan stage, not only once a finished project is handed over for pricing, specifically so labor-scope assumptions get checked against the real drawing before a client ever sees a number.

## Contractor Acceptance / Vetting — Author-Supervision Scope, a Third Independent Corroboration

- **⚠️ Author-supervision ("авторский надзор") scope defined precisely, corroborating this store's existing distinction from ARCHWOOD and this channel's own prior source (`33b61qeO_XY`), with added mechanical detail not previously recorded**: author-supervision explicitly does **not** cover workmanship/technique quality (the guest's own example: she does not check whether plaster has cured properly or was applied correctly — that is technical-supervision/inspector territory). What it *does* cover: (1) re-measuring the space after demolition, since as-built dimensions reliably shift once old finishes are stripped (stated as a near-universal surprise to newer designers, "100% will change" is not a sign anyone measured wrong originally); (2) verifying installed electrical outlet/switch positions match the drawings; (3) tracking the finish-material delivery schedule against the actual construction timeline, since finish materials (tile, flooring, fixtures) are commonly ordered on long lead times that must be coordinated with when the site is actually ready for them; (4) documenting agreed changes and site visits in a running log ("журнал выездов авторского надзора") rather than relying on memory.
- **⚠️ Real self-reported early-career mistake, offered as the concrete cost of skipping this check**: the guest's own account of an early project where she failed to verify an outlet/switch position during author-supervision — a control-panel output ("вывод для брашки" — an appliance/fixture connection point) ended up positioned incorrectly, forcing a visible surface-mounted cable channel (color-matched to the wall, but still a visible defect) as the only fix once discovered post-installation. Cited as a formative example of why the electrical-position check specifically matters, not a hypothetical.
