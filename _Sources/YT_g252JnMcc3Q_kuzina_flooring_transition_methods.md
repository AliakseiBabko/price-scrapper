## Flooring — the screed-planning consequence (the part that isn't about the joint at all)

- **⚠️ Her opening point is a sequencing one, and it is the highest-value item in the video**: the two coverings have different total build-up thicknesses, so **the transition decision has to be made before the screed is poured, not when the joint is being finished.**
- **Concrete build-up figures she gives**:
  - **Tile / porcelain + adhesive ≈ 15 mm total.**
  - **Parquet or engineered board + plywood beneath + adhesive between ≈ 30–32 mm.**
- **The consequence, stated directly**: "вы не можете начать укладывать плитку, если вы точно не определились с тем, как у вас будет паркет" — whether the parquet is glued to plywood, glued straight to the screed, or is laminate instead, each demands a different screed height. **The difference in thicknesses is compensated in the screed**, so the screed cannot be set until the finish build-ups on both sides are chosen.

## Flooring — the four joint methods, with the condition attached to each

She frames the usual set as three (metal threshold, cork compensator, sealant) and adds grout as a fourth that she wants to discuss first.

**1. Grout («затирка») — the only option for a non-straight joint.**
- **Its unique use case**: a joint that is deliberately irregular — she names the currently fashionable hexagonal/octagonal tile interlocking into parquet with a ragged edge. **A threshold strip cannot follow that line at all, and a cork compensator is very difficult to.**
- Method: use the same two-component polymer grout used on the tile itself, run neatly along the tile-to-parquet joint.
- **⚠️ Hard condition: the parquet must be rigidly bonded to the substrate, not laid floating.** If it is floating for other reasons, **bond just the single row that meets the tile** — the rest of the floor then moves as designed while the joint row stays dimensionally stable against humidity and temperature.
- Verdict: neat, and reasonably durable.

**2. Cork compensator («пробковый компенсатор») — the most common, and the one she is moving away from.**
- Two grades: plain pressed cork sold in sheets and tinted on site to match one material, versus a **preformed profile** ("типа батончик") with a smooth rounded top edge, factory-tinted — neater and longer-lived.
- **Why it is chosen: it breathes and works well in expansion**, absorbing the joint's movement.
- **Why she is moving away from it**: "выглядит это не очень эстетично," and specifically poor where the tile side is a cut edge rather than a factory edge meeting the parquet. She notes **parquet fitters recommend it**, and that many designers keep specifying it for that reason and because it does not stand out badly — but "всё больше склоняюсь к тому, что предпочтительнее ставить металлические порожки."

**3. Silicone sealant, and "liquid cork" («жидкая пробка») as a middle option.**
- **Liquid cork = sealant with cork crumb mixed in.** It behaves more like cork and is **harder to pick out of the joint** than plain sealant. She is openly unsure whether it looks meaningfully better.
- **Silicone is the most elastic of the four and handles building settlement best.** Visually it is the least obtrusive — she rates it **less visible than cork**.
- **⚠️ Width constraint that limits the liquid-cork option specifically**: the joint should be **up to 5 mm**, and at that width the cork crumb in the compound sometimes simply will not fit through.
- **Two failure modes she reports rather than asserts**: many designers say it **hardens over time and loses its elasticity**; and it is **much easier to pick out of the joint** than the alternatives — her concrete example is a pet acquiring the habit of digging sealant out of floor joints. Consequence: **the joint needs periodic renewal**, which she judges tolerable but real.

**4. Metal threshold («металлический порожек») — her recommendation.**
- Aesthetics: **brass thresholds can be genuinely attractive**, or the strip can be **painted to the floor colour**, which reads far less noticeably than leaving it as a bare metal strip.
- **⚠️ The decisive advantage: it is the only one of the four that works with a floating floor** — provided the top of the "T" is **wider than ~5 mm**, so it covers the movement gap and no gap opens as the floor breathes.
- **Nothing needs renewing** over time, unlike the sealant option.
- She considers good thresholds a recent market development — "один из последних трендов… на рынке сейчас много качественных таких порожков."
- **⚠️ The real obstacle is procurement, not performance, and it must be handled early**: a threshold **cannot be bought with the tile and cannot be bought with the parquet.** It has to be sourced separately, painted if required, and physically delivered to site. "В этом заключается основная сейчас проблема."

## Planning Rules

- **⚠️ Where the transition falls in a doorway, the joint must sit under the door leaf** so it is hidden when the door is closed. **This means the door's swing direction must be settled before the floor joint is made** — she flags it as obvious but says people do get it wrong.
- Corollary of the above plus the screed point: the flooring-transition decision is bound to **three** other decisions that are all made earlier — screed level, finish build-up on both sides, and door swing.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- **Specifying grout at a joint where the parquet is floating** — the mechanism is the same one that makes cork and sealant fail there.
- **Making the floor joint before the door swing is settled**, so it does not land under the leaf.
- **Leaving the threshold to be bought "with the flooring"** — it is a separate procurement item and is the thing most likely to be missing on site.
