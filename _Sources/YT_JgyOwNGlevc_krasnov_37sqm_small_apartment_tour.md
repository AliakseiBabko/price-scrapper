## Design Concept, Bathroom, Kitchen, Furniture / Built-ins, Small Bedroom, Mistakes / Warnings

- **⚠️ Frank Lloyd Wright's "compression and release" principle — a real, named architectural concept, genuinely new to this vault, cited by architect Robert Gar**: alternating narrow/constrained transition spaces (e.g. a narrow entry hall) with open/expansive zones (a bright open living room) creates a psychological contrast that makes the open zone feel MORE spacious than its actual square footage. Attribute to Gar citing Frank Lloyd Wright, not to Krasnov.
- **Krasnov's own added, concrete application**: for a studio-to-one-bedroom conversion specifically, making the new bedroom slightly MORE enclosed/cozy makes the remaining main living zone feel more spacious BY CONTRAST, not smaller — a specific, actionable small-apartment zoning technique Krasnov derives from Gar's cited principle.
- **⚠️ Sliding/pivoting wall-panel bedroom partition (Gar's own described technique) — with an important caveat, attributed to Krasnov**: such partition walls save space effectively but provide almost NO soundproofing. Krasnov's explicit added caveat: this solution is appropriate only for single-occupant households or those not needing full privacy, not for shared/family living.
- **⚠️ A critical failure mode Krasnov himself flags watching the footage, genuinely valuable**: a second rotating/pivoting cabinet-wall element (part of a wardrobe that swings out to become a wall) LOOKS visually clever, but the resulting configured space (a sofa facing a blank cabinet back) appears to go largely unused based on the visual evidence. Krasnov's explicit caution: flashy transformable/mechanical furniture solutions can look impressive in demonstration but may create a functionally "dead" zone if the resulting space configuration doesn't actually serve a real daily-use purpose.
- **⚠️ Bathroom lighting — a detailed, multi-technique bundle** (attribute the described techniques to Gar/Underwood, Krasnov's synthesis/praise separately): hidden lights under a floating stone vanity slab creating a visual "levitation" effect; light slits/gaps illuminating end walls specifically to bring out tile texture via light-and-shadow play; niche lighting in the shower and behind the door illuminating small window openings, used together to create an illusion of expanded space; cove/perimeter lighting along the ceiling edge creating a "floating ceiling" effect that visually raises perceived ceiling height; a backlit mirror reinforcing the sense of depth; a slatted/grate ceiling structure zoning the upper part of the room WITHOUT visually "eating" volume. **Krasnov's own explicit synthesis**: light distributed across MULTIPLE levels and zones (not just one source) is specifically what let a genuinely dark bathroom palette still read as spacious rather than cramped — a strong, well-illustrated real-world validation of this vault's existing layered-lighting principle; cite as a concrete case example, don't re-log the principle itself.
- **⚠️ Wood ceiling slats in a high-humidity bathroom — a material-durability caution, Krasnov's own flagged concern despite otherwise praising the room**: wood is a demanding material under high humidity; performance depends heavily on ventilation quality and surface treatment; even with good protection, such slats require regular maintenance and can darken or warp over time.
- **Terracotta flooring in a bathroom** chosen deliberately by Underwood for an "earthy," barefoot-friendly, outdoor-adjacent feel.
- **Exposed/visible plumbing-pipe routing left intentionally exposed specifically to GAIN ceiling height** in the bathroom (rather than boxing it in with a dropped ceiling) — a real technique tradeoff (visual exposure of infrastructure vs. maximizing headroom); tag as a stylistic choice, not a universal recommendation.
- **Vertical narrow tile format used specifically to visually emphasize wall height** in a small bathroom; dark glossy tile texture used deliberately here for a sense of depth — **a specific, stated exception/context where gloss was used successfully, worth cross-referencing against this vault's multiple existing anti-gloss critiques (not a contradiction to resolve, a context-dependent counter-case).**
- **Bathroom storage relocated entirely OUTSIDE the bathroom** to another part of the apartment, leaving only functional fixtures plus a few small hidden shelves inside — a specific storage-distribution strategy for a very small bathroom, extending this vault's existing storage-by-functional-zone principle.
- **⚠️ Vertical-lift cabinet doors in the kitchen, used because of a narrow galley layout where standard swing doors would require walking backward repeatedly** — Krasnov's own nuanced caveat, distinct from earlier lift-mechanism critiques already logged (those addressed heavy fronts wearing out lift hardware on WIDE upper cabinets; this is vertical lift-up doors on TALL NARROW cabinets in a constrained walkway). Krasnov states he's generally not a fan of vertical-lift systems ("не самая удобная и надёжная вещь") but concedes it's a reasonable, deliberate compromise specifically for this narrow-layout constraint — record this nuance explicitly, don't merge with the existing lift-mechanism critique.
- **Integrated/panel-matched fridge plus a large decorative range-hood shelf painted the same color as the hood**, for a unified "single surface" read — extends existing appliance-integration content.
- **Open shelving used successfully to keep the upper kitchen zone visually light, deliberately contrasted against dark lower cabinets and richly-colored walls — Krasnov's own explicit stated position**: normally skeptical of open shelving (consistent with his many other critiques already logged), but concedes it genuinely works HERE because it balances an otherwise heavy/dark lower half. Reinforces the existing "moderation/context, not a blanket rule" nuance on open shelving, cross-reference rather than a new contradiction.
- **⚠️ A specific missing/ambiguous task-lighting concern Krasnov flags as a genuine potential flaw**: he explicitly notes uncertainty about whether task lighting is actually built into the underside of the open upper shelf above the counter — if absent, he states plainly that neither decorative light nor window daylight can substitute for direct task lighting over a work surface.
- **⚠️ Double-sided built-in cabinet functioning as a shared partition wall between two rooms** (TV/media storage on the living-room side, storage on the bedroom side) — a specific dual-function partition technique, with a flagged caveat and Krasnov's suggested fix: the cabinet doesn't reach the ceiling (likely due to existing structural beams), which visually reads as an "abruptly cut-off" volume and shortens the perceived room height.
