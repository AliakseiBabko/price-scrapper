## Rules / Heuristics — Kitchen Installation QC, 7 Points

- **⚠️ Kitchen-run-to-wall curtain-clearance gap: minimum 15cm, maximum 20cm.** When a kitchen cabinet run ends near a window, leave a dedicated gap between the last module and the side wall specifically for curtains to hang in — without this gap, curtains end up cramped against the cabinet run on one side and hanging freely on the other, reading as visibly asymmetric and constantly catching/bunching against the cabinetry when the window is viewed. Distinct from this store's existing 200mm curtain-*zone-depth* figure (that one sizes how far a curtain projects off the wall; this one sizes the horizontal gap between a kitchen run's end and the wall it approaches).
- **⚠️ Backsplash tile-to-upper-cabinet clearance: exactly 600mm is the standard, sized to fit a widely-formatted (60cm) porcelain tile with only a small grout gap** — described as a well-known industry standard that kitchen installers routinely ignore, building whatever gap suits their own convenience instead. **Fix when the gap was built wrong**: glue the tile so its top edge sits just under the cabinet bottom (leaving only a small grout-line gap), and cover any remaining leftover strip with edge trim or a matching baseboard/molding piece rather than leaving an odd sliver of exposed wall.
- **⚠️ Main cook-zone lighting AND control placement rule**: the primary prep-and-cook zone (the area between the cooktop and the sink, where the most active cooking time is spent) needs not just good task lighting, but a **light-control switch physically reachable from within that same zone** — not tucked into a hard-to-reach corner requiring the cook to walk away from the zone to activate it. Documented real QC failure: a touch-sensor light switch was installed in an awkward corner location, functionally unreachable while actively cooking.
- **⚠️ Appliance corner-placement door-swing caution**: verify a built-in appliance's door-swing/control-panel side *before* finalizing its placement in a corner run — a documented case placed a microwave in an otherwise well-thought-out spot (near the fridge, hidden from view, child-safe height) but with its door opening directly into the corner, forcing an awkward squeeze-and-reach motion to use it every time. **Fix**: choose a model with controls on the side/left and a door that opens into the open walkway, not into the corner, whenever a corner placement is otherwise unavoidable. **Practical client-side guidance**: verify every built-in appliance's (microwave, dishwasher, etc.) door-swing direction yourself before signing off on the final kitchen design — don't assume the installer has checked it.
- **Floor-to-ceiling kitchen upper cabinets: real cost and access tradeoffs, restated with a concrete cost multiplier** — a full-height kitchen (extra upper modules reaching the ceiling) can cost up to **1.5× more** than a standard-height kitchen because of the added upper tier, and the top tier remains unreachable without a step stool regardless of the occupant's height (the presenter, 176cm tall, confirms this personally on camera).
- **⚠️ Deep storage-column pull-out requirement**: a tall storage column (e.g. next to a fridge) with a narrow hinged door (typically 350mm or 400mm wide) becomes functionally near-useless at its back — too narrow and deep to reach into by hand. **A pull-out drawer/basket system is required to actually use the column's full depth and volume** — costs more than a simple hinged door, but without it the deep rear portion of the column is effectively wasted storage.
- **⚠️ Upper-cabinet depth planning around a corner-adjacent fridge**: the standard first-tier upper-cabinet depth is 300-400mm; if the design extends to full-height 600mm-deep upper cabinets and the run wraps around a corner next to a fridge (which is itself 600mm deep and can't be made shallower), keeping the upper cabinets at the standard 400mm depth there leaves an ugly, dust-collecting ~200mm shallow shelf directly above the fridge. **Better fix: make the entire upper cabinet run 600mm deep from the start where physically possible**, even though reaching a full 600mm depth (especially on an upper shelf, from a step stool) is somewhat less ergonomic — the alternative (a narrow dust shelf) is worse in practice. Deep upper storage of this kind suits long-term/infrequent-access storage best, given the reduced day-to-day ergonomics.
