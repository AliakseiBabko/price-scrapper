## Design Concept / Materials — Ten Named Detail Solutions

- **⚠️ 1. A concealed (recessed) track for a bath or shower curtain.** The same recessed-niche track normally used for window curtains, mounted in a bathroom where a curtain is wanted instead of a glass screen. She uses a custom curtain in treated fabric. **Answering the recurring question of how you remove it: there is an end cap on the track — pop the cap and the runners slide out, or simply unhook the curtain.** Relevant to [[17_Design_and_Ergonomics/analysis/Curtains_and_Window_Treatments|Curtains & Window Treatments]], which covers recessed niches for window curtains but not this application.
- **⚠️ 2. Recessed door frame ("утопленный короб") — with a critical constraint.** The frame is set into the wall so the small return wall (простенок) that a normally-mounted frame creates disappears, and the wall reads continuous with the finish flowing from room to room. **The constraint, stated as very important: recess the frame on the *hinge* side only, never on the lock side, or the handle strikes the wall.** Works best visually with floor-to-ceiling doors, and **must be drawn as a detail in advance** — it is not a site decision.
- **⚠️ 3. A second, concealed interior door inboard of the entrance door.** Not merely a revival of the old two-door entrance: her stated rationale is twofold. **Acoustic** — real sound insulation from the stairwell and neighbours. **Psychological** — proposed specifically for flats where the occupant spends long periods with the entrance group in view (her example: sitting on a sofa looking at the hall), where staring at a front door is subtly uncomfortable; an interior door reads as "another room" instead. The nearest alternative if a second door isn't wanted: an entrance door on concealed hinges and a concealed frame with a code or biometric lock, though an entrance door inevitably carries more visible hardware (locks, spyhole, handle) than an interior door, which needs only one restrained handle.
- **⚠️ 4. Cabinet fronts clad in thin large-format ceramic.** For bathrooms where the walls are tiled but a cabinet is also present, the usual workaround is laminated chipboard or enamelled MDF matched to the tile colour — which cannot reproduce the stone *pattern*. **Solution: thin large-format ceramic, available down to 6 mm, is light enough for a joinery shop to fix to a front**, whereas ordinary porcelain is too heavy. The same material then runs across both wall and cabinet front in one continuous stone pattern.
- **⚠️ 5. Microcement over a heated wall in a hallway, pantry or utility block.** Microcement is usually thought of as a bathroom material. Her case: an entrance hall needing open storage — hooks for a wet umbrella or a snow-covered coat — where the owner does not want a visually distinct material such as porcelain or panelling, but wants the wall to read as painted. **Microcement applied in a smooth, non-mottled, non-textured way reads like paint, can be colour-matched to the wall paint, and is genuinely water-resistant.** And a floor-heating mat can be laid under it to create a **heated drying wall**; they did exactly this in a utility block, with hooks and rails for drying cloths.
  - **Project note**: this project has decided against underfloor heating ([[00_Master/project_decisions|Project Decisions]]), but a heated *wall* in a utility or entrance zone is a different application and is not covered by that decision — flagged, not assumed either way.
- **⚠️ 6. Concealed shelf fixings — and the mechanism is simpler than it sounds.** Acrylic or metal shelves in a bathroom with **no visible fixings and in fact no separate fixings at all**: the plate's edges are set into the joints between tile courses and clamped there. She reports it carries real load (bottles, shampoos). **Stated secondary benefit: more hygienic** — no extra joints or brackets for dirt and mould to accumulate in. Their studio position: all fixings in a contemporary renovation should be concealed.
- **⚠️ 7. Shower glass in a concealed profile.** Compared with a surface-mounted aluminium profile, a concealed profile is **more watertight** and **more hygienic** (fewer corners collecting dirt and mould) and easier to clean, and visually the glass appears to emerge directly from wall and floor — she notes this matters most in small bathrooms. Sourced from shower-partition manufacturers rather than a general contractor.
- **⚠️ 8. Porcelain that mimics engineered board, to avoid a visible floor transition.** Where a hallway needs a floor resistant to water, road salt and mechanical wear, but the rest of the flat is engineered board or parquet, the usual result is a visible split between a stone-look porcelain and a wood floor. **Many manufacturers now make porcelain in plank formats sized like engineered board with wood tones and grain** — matched carefully, the hall floor reads continuous with the living-area floor while being substantially more durable.
- **⚠️ 9. Fill the bath through the slot overflow ("излив через слив-перелив") instead of a spout.** Applied in nearly every one of their projects and described as base practice. Two stated reasons: a wall spout **protrudes and is a real injury hazard**, and removing it leaves only a single mixer on the wall.
- **⚠️ 10. Two lighting scenarios on a single track.** A frequently missed option: **one track does not have to mean one switch.** Two switches can drive different fixture groups on the same visual track line — e.g. pendants on the track from one switch, and the track's general-light fixtures from another, ganged with the room's other spots. **Stated purpose: keep the ceiling clear of fixture clutter** — their rule of thumb being to leave the ceiling with only the minimum necessary lighting function on it.
