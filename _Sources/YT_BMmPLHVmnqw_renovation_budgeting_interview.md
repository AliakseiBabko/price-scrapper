## Contractor Selection (how to vet/choose a contractor, red flags)

- Recommended vetting approach: **check live/genuine reviews**, and if possible visit a past client of the contractor **in person** (not an "arranged"/staged reference) to see real completed work — not a project that isn't actually theirs.
- **Check the renovation contract itself** as a trust signal.
- At first contact with a contractor's lead/manager, the speaker describes relying on a personal read ("на уровне эмпатии") of whether the person genuinely lives the work, based on the in-person meeting and site visits (**speaker's own stated heuristic, not a formalized checklist**).
- Intake process the speaker personally uses (**single-account, describing their own business's process**):
  - If referred by a partner designer: they receive an existing design project, review it, and issue either a commercial proposal (КП) or a full estimate (смета) — usually a smeta, since the client is already "warm."
  - If reached directly (social media, word of mouth) with no design project yet: they ask for whatever exists (even just a BTI floor plan or just the address, from which they'll look up the layout themselves), give a rough ballpark price, and use this to qualify the client's budget before investing further time.
  - They report often asking directly how much total money the client has available (not just the stated project budget), to gauge whether to invest sales effort or not.
- **Why identical design projects get different price quotes from different companies**: (1) general construction material costs can vary somewhat but converge around the 35,000–45,000 range when contractors work "under strict accounting" (report/receipts-based purchasing); (2) the larger driver is **labor/service tier** — higher-priced contractors provide more named, specialized staff (foreman, senior master, procurement coordinator, supervising designer) and more organizational process, not necessarily "better hands" doing the physical work; (3) higher price also correlates with more established/"named" ("именитые") production staff and company reputation, which the speaker frames as a legitimate cost driver, not pure markup.
- **Red flag**: contractors at the lowest price tiers (e.g. ~20–30) concentrating all decision authority in a single foreman under high stress — cited risk of the foreman burning out, disappearing, or becoming unreliable, and clients having to just live with it.
- Framing for clients: paying more isn't automatically "better hands," it's a different service model (more oversight, more staff, more process, more accountability) — the speaker explicitly says producing similar physical work at 40 vs 80 is "roughly the same," but the surrounding service, staffing depth, and company track record differ.

## Design Project Requirements (what a proper design project package must include)

- A complete drawing set with **top-down views**:
  1. As-built floor plan of the current apartment state, with full dimensions (regardless of what's currently built/standing) — needed for accurate estimate/quote (смета) calculation.
  2. Demolition plan, also without furniture — needed to know what can safely be demolished vs. preserved.
  3. Installation/montage plan, in "clean" form without furniture overlaid — needed for accurate installation work. The speaker explicitly criticizes the common failure mode of skipping straight to a furnished montage plan with no clean/dimensioned intermediate versions.
- **Full set of wall elevations/unfolds ("развёртки")** — cross-checked against the plan drawings; discrepancies between plan and elevation should be caught and clarified (call/confirm which is correct) before construction, not during.
- **Visualizations** — the primary artifact the client uses to sign off on/accept the designer's work.
- **Nodes and sections ("узлы и разрезы")** — needed to resolve junction/interface details (e.g., how two elements physically meet) at the design stage, before the contractor is on site and has to guess.
- **Explicitly out of scope for a standard design project** (per the speaker): detailed engineering schematics for electrical (ЭОМ) and HVAC (ОВК) systems — these are produced by specialists other than interior designers, for additional cost; the speaker states clients/designers should not expect this from a standard design-project deliverable, though it can be added for modest extra cost.

## Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

- **Underestimating hidden install costs**: buyers often compare only device sticker price (e.g., built-in vs. surface shower systems) and miss that installation, fittings, and plumbing infrastructure can multiply the real price gap roughly 2x versus the device-only difference.
- **Overspending on a single flashy line item** (e.g., ~300,000 on radiators) at the expense of finishing the renovation within budget — cited as a repeated pattern the speaker has personally observed with clients.
- **Skipping proper budget interrogation up front**: not clarifying whether a stated total budget includes loans, credit lines, future income, or is a "hard, already-in-hand" number — leading to mid-project shortfalls (example given: a couple's Maldives trip consuming 2 months of income earmarked for the renovation, shrinking a stated 15 million to 14.2 million mid-project) and subsequent blame-shifting.
- **First-time renovators overestimating their own ability to self-manage** ("we'll buy everything ourselves, we have enough for everything") — the speaker frames the interviewer/contractor's job as removing rose-tinted assumptions with realistic, comparative pricing information ("show the showroom price, show contractor differences") so the client can make an informed choice, while acknowledging clients often still won't choose well without that context.
- **Skipping straight to a furnished/decorated montage plan** without a clean dimensioned as-built + demolition + montage sequence — described as a frequent, avoidable design-project defect.
- **Imitation materials at budget tiers** (tile trying to look like marble/stone, wallpaper trying to imitate clouds/florals) — called out generally as looking worse than an honest, non-imitative material choice.
- **Spending disproportionately on decorative/branded designer paint** without the underlying budget to support the rest of the renovation — paired with the speaker's opinion that some of that price premium is commission-driven.
- **Airless spray painting at budget tiers** — framed as a mistake because of hidden consumable/labor costs (masking, multi-day layer application) that make it more expensive in practice than roller application, even though the paint-quality result (in the speaker's specific negative anecdote) can end up "plastic-looking" rather than better.
