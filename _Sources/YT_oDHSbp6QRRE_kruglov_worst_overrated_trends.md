## Genuinely New Content (Full List)

**Shadow-gap and micro-baseboard, new specifics beyond this page's
existing schedule-burden/compatibility-list content**:
- **⚠️ Total incompatibility with floating (unglued) flooring**: a
  shadow-gap baseboard cannot be used at all with any flooring installed
  by the floating method (laminate or SPC-type quartz-vinyl on
  underlayment, not glued to the substrate) — not a workaround-needed
  case, a hard exclusion.
- **New cost figures for the glued-flooring case**: even where glued
  flooring (e.g. engineered board) technically allows a shadow-gap
  baseboard, a precise finish cut against the wall can cost up to
  **~1,500 RUB per linear meter (≈$20/m)**, and the mandatory damper gap
  then needs either a cork compensator or floor sealant, adding a
  further **~2,000 RUB per linear meter (≈$30/m)** — both real added
  costs beyond the flooring and baseboard hardware itself.
- **New dust-illumination failure mode**: a shadow-gap baseboard with
  integrated LED lighting continuously illuminates the dust that
  inevitably accumulates in that gap whenever the light is on — framed
  as a real, specific downside of combining the two features, not
  present on this page previously.
- **New repairability caution**: a shadow-gap baseboard embeds the
  flooring edge into the wall channel on both sides, so removing the
  floor later (repair, replacement, water-damage remediation) requires
  destructively prying the flooring back out of the wall channel.
- **Micro-baseboard (rigid aluminum L-profile/corner-guard type) — new
  material, not previously on this page**: sold in 2m/2.5m lengths,
  genuinely rigid (more so than MDF). **New failure modes**: (1) its
  perfect rigidity/flatness reveals normal in-spec plaster waviness that
  a softer baseboard would visually absorb — a wall that passes a
  normal СНиП flatness tolerance can still show a visible gap under this
  specific baseboard type; (2) where two lengths meet at an external
  corner, the two pieces never form a perfectly flush plane — one edge
  typically proud by up to ~0.5mm, enough to snag stockings/socks or
  scratch skin (children specifically flagged); a connector piece is
  reportedly in development by some manufacturers but not yet common;
  (3) floating flooring can't be tucked into this baseboard type
  starting from a single wall the way it can under a standard baseboard
  — installers reportedly improvise ad hoc workarounds.
- **Recommended alternatives, restated/confirmed**: MDF (paint-ready or
  pre-painted) or duropolymer as the reliable external-mount baseboard
  choice; concealed-mount baseboard (with a paintable inner insert) as
  the more repairable, if costlier, alternative to shadow-gap
  specifically.

**Drywall ceiling — a real cost-figure divergence worth flagging against
this page's existing Round 5 cost ladder**: this video states drywall
ceiling installation costs **~15,000 RUB/m² (≈$190/m²) for labor alone**
plus roughly another **~5,000 RUB/m² (≈$60/m²) materials**, i.e. **~20,000
RUB/m² (≈$260/m²) all-in** for a single-level ceiling finished to paint.
**The existing page's Round 5 figures (from this same channel,
`YT_lhNC30_adGc`) put single-layer drywall meaningfully lower: ~5,500
RUB/m² labor + ~3,500 RUB/m² materials (≈$60+$40 ≈ $100/m² combined),
and even multi-level drywall only reaches ~8,000+5,500=13,500 RUB/m²
(≈$150/m²).** **Recorded as an open, unresolved same-channel
divergence** rather than merged or averaged — roughly a year separates
the two videos' upload dates, which may explain some of the gap
(inflation), but the ratio (≈20,000 vs ≈13,500-9,000 RUB/m²) looks
larger than a one-year price rise alone would explain. **New timeline
figures**: a 50m² apartment's drywall ceiling (full prep-to-paint) can
take **2-3 months**; a 100m² apartment's ceiling framing alone takes
about a month, with prep/finishing adding another 4-5 months — broadly
consistent with, but more granular than, the existing page's 100m²
"~2 months" multi-level figure. **New exception case, not previously on
this page**: a drywall ceiling remains the right (unavoidable) choice
specifically where ducted supply/exhaust ventilation trunking needs to
be boxed in — the presenter explicitly carves this use case out of his
own "overrated" critique, recommending stretch ceiling for everything
else.

**Perimeter/contour ("cove") ceiling lighting — a new failure mode not
on `12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Lighting_Design.md`, which
currently frames this technique as unqualified good practice**: dust,
gnats, and small insects accumulate in the concealed channel between the
ceiling membrane and the light source over time, and once lit, the
light source casts a visible shadow through the accumulated debris,
reading as visible dirt on the ceiling — described as a real, fairly
fast (not many-years-out) cosmetic failure specific to this construction
detail, not a durability problem with the ceiling itself. **⚠️ This
softens, without directly contradicting, the existing page's
unqualified "good practice" framing** — worth flagging as an added
caveat rather than a reversal.

**Interior door height vs. entry door height — a genuinely new topic
for this page**: a fashionable near-full-height ("to the ceiling")
interior door reads visually wrong specifically when the apartment's
entry door is a standard **2m** tall leaf — the entry door then looks
disproportionately short/"hobbit-sized" next to the interior doors.
**Named rule of thumb**: keep interior door height within **~20cm** of
the entry door's own height (e.g. entry 2m → interior up to 2.2m is
fine; entry 2.2m → interior up to 2.4m is fine, since bathroom/utility/
walk-in doors near the entry are typically shorter anyway and blend the
transition). **Cost multiplier**: an oversized (2.4-2.8m) interior door
leaf costs **3-5× a standard 2m leaf**, and installation costs **5-10×**
more — plus real delivery/lift logistics issues (doesn't fit in a
standard elevator, same class of problem as oversized porcelain slabs
below).

**Large-format porcelain slab logistics — mostly corroborating this
channel's own existing content on this page (`YvtdjHJhfpU`'s ≈40,000-
50,000 RUB single-slab lift-cost figure), with one new concrete
recommendation**: this source names **60×120cm or 160×80cm as covering
"99% of needs"** for apartments/houses up to ~200m², explicitly steering
away from "super-large-format" (>160cm long side, up to 320cm) except
in genuinely large (500-1,000m²+) houses with correspondingly large
bathrooms (15-20m²+) where the material doesn't visually disappear the
way it does in an ordinary ~3m² apartment bathroom. Confirms (does not
newly establish) the existing per-slab lift-cost figure with a stated
example of over **40,000+ RUB for a single sheet**.

**Natural/artificial stone countertop comparison — adds one new
material to this page's existing granite/marble/travertine/slate
list**: **quartzite**, explicitly ranked as combining strength, low
porosity, durability, and wear-resistance better than any of the other
four — with the single tradeoff of very high cost. **New maintenance-
recurrence framing for travertine** (restates the existing porosity
caution but adds a mechanism): sealing travertine's pores is not a
one-time treatment — it must be periodically re-applied, analogized
directly to reapplying anti-corrosion undercoating on a car ("you can't
do it once and be done for life"). Marble and dark-toned artificial
stone/acrylic are both reconfirmed as scratch- and stain-prone in an
active kitchen work zone — restates, not new.

**Bathroom tile shelving — one new concrete alternative, complementing
this page's existing tiled-shelf-cost and hidden-metal-shelf content**:
ready-made stainless-steel shelf units (available via general
marketplaces and classified-ad sites, not just specialty retailers) —
some with concealed mounting, some with visible mounting, some
integrated directly into the tile joint at install time with no visible
fixing point at all — named as a genuinely good middle option between
an expensive custom-tiled niche and cheap outdated chrome corner
caddies. The source's own view: 1-2 tiled niche shelves in a genuinely
useful spot (e.g. beside the shower) are worth the cost; using tiled
shelves as the *primary* storage solution throughout is the overrated
part.
