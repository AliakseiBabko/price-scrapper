## Labor / Delivery Model — ⚠️ why in-store advice is never about the choice

Her observation from watching other customers, and it explains a gap most people notice without diagnosing:

- A lone client freezes in front of a stand carrying *"сто пятьсот"* little sheets, each with several colours.
- **⚠️ Salespeople are usually competent, but they never recommend a specific colour in the abstract — "потому что если вам вдруг потом не понравится этот цвет на стене, то им будут выслушивать ваши претензии."**
- **⚠️ So instead of the colour they talk about everything adjacent: opacity, consumption, how to apply it, how to store it.** The advice is real; it is simply never about the decision you came in to make.
- **The two failure modes she names**: holding ten palettes that are all beautiful and being unable to choose; or cheerfully jabbing at one *because* they are all beautiful, and finding it "такое себе" on the wall.

## Design Concept — ⚠️ before going to the shop

- **Hold the effect you want from the whole interior in your head, because wall colour strongly affects the mood of the entire space.** Look at reference images in advance and decide the broad class: neutral or bright, light or dark.
- **⚠️ Better still, arrive with an abstract colour *name* already fixed** — *"это должен быть лиловый интерьер"* — **so you look only at lilacs and stop registering blues and greens.**
- **⚠️ Never select paint abstractly, and never start a project's material selection with paint.** You must bring **a sample of the floor**, and ideally **the door colours** too. **Take every sample you have to the shop and choose the specific option together with the paint.** *(The same sequencing rule as `ocoPRS3d8sA` — the constrained materials are chosen first, paint last, because paint has the widest range.)*

## Design Concept — ⚠️ choose a supplier with FEWER colours

**Counter-intuitive, and she is emphatic about it.**

- **The only case where thousands of colours help**: you have wallpaper with leaves and need a green that matches a leaf exactly. **Then more is better — and the choice is not actually hard, because you know precisely what you want.**
- **In every other case prefer a supplier with a smaller palette, "потому что глаза не так разбегаются."**
- Her own working catalogues: one of **156 colours**, which she has numbered so the sheets can be re-filed in order — *"здесь достаточно цветов, чтобы выбрать цвет для интерьера."* **If something is genuinely missing, move to the next supplier and close the question.** **Little Greene** is about the same thickness at **~160 colours** and likewise enough.
- **⚠️ Her reason is physiological: the eye tires of colour as other senses tire under load.** Her analogy — choosing perfume, you smell one, two, three, and by the fourth you have stopped perceiving smell at all.

### ⚠️ The consequence for a whole-flat selection, which is a scheduling rule

- **You must look at the colours for *all* rooms at once.** Doing the living room today and the hallway tomorrow **does not work.**
- **⚠️ The method: look at everything and shortlist; then come back the next day; take a break, go for coffee, return and look again — "иначе глаз замыливается очень быстро."**

## Design Concept — ⚠️ the order of choosing when a space has several colours

Applies where there is a wall colour plus moulding, skirting and cornice colours. **The rule branches on whether the main colour is loud, and "main" means the one occupying the most area:**

- **If the main colour is pronounced: choose it first, then the accents.**
- **⚠️ If the main colour is neutral and the task is to push it as far into the background as possible: choose the bright accents FIRST, then fit the background colour to them.**

## Materials — ⚠️ overtone and undertone

- **The pigment is what reads immediately** — green, yellow. English **"overtone"**: what lies on the surface, the easily identified part of the colour.
- **"Undertone"** is the nuance. Her worked yellows: one близкий to lemon; one closer to lime because it leans green; one that is cadmium yellow.
- **⚠️ Distinguishing undertones matters most in the neutral palette**, because nuance is what makes a colour stick out from the rest of the set — **"вы не сможете подогнать цвет своих стен к цвету полов, или к цвету дверей, или к цвету обивки мебели."**
- **⚠️ The procedure: whites, whitish, greys, beiges, browns and blacks must be compared very precisely against each other to establish which colour underlies each — red, orange, yellow, blue or green. That is what makes a palette coherent.**
- **⚠️ And each undertone has its own characteristic failure: warmer shades can look dirtier; cooler shades can look less cosy.**

## Design Concept — ⚠️ order three shades, not one

- Once you have settled on a specific paint and are ready to order a tester, **take the two neighbouring tones as well — one slightly lighter, one slightly darker.**
- **Because at home the paint can look nothing like it did in the shop, and choosing from a *set* pins it down far more precisely than judging one sample.**
- **⚠️ So: three variants for every colour you need.** Two wall colours means three plus three, **plus three more for the moulding if that is a different colour again.**

## Labor / Delivery Model — ⚠️ the tester procedure, which is the core of the source

**Never choose from the fan deck.** Even taken home, a small chip gives no sense of how a wall will read at scale — **the bigger the tester, the more it tells you.**

**How to make them:**

- Order the small pots and **paint the testers onto pieces of hardboard («оргалит») or plasterboard** — or onto **a piece of the paintable wallpaper** if that is what will be on the wall.
- **⚠️ No smaller than A3.** Below that you lose the sense of the colour's brightness. *(Consistent with `ocoPRS3d8sA`, where she warns A3 is itself still small relative to a room — the two together read as: A3 is the floor, not the target.)*
- **⚠️ Do NOT paint testers directly onto the wall — they must be mobile so you can carry them round the room.** She calls this out explicitly as the mistake the logic implies.
- **⚠️ Never leave a margin of white paper or coloured backing showing around the painted area — especially the green face of plasterboard. It strongly distorts perception.**

**Where to hold them — a checklist:**

| Position | What it tells you |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Raised to ceiling level** | How it reads up there. **If the paint will run onto the cornice or ceiling, test it on the ceiling too — the colour will look much darker there than on the wall** |
| **At eye level** | The normal reading |
| **At floor level** | **⚠️ By the time paint is being chosen the floors are often already laid — so you can check how the wall colour interacts with the actual floor covering** |
| **The wall opposite the window** | |
| **The side walls** | |
| **⚠️ In shadow** — under the window, and in corners and junctions where shadow gathers | **Especially important for a near-white paint with a slight undertone: a faintly greenish tint shows up most in the corners** |

## Design Concept — ⚠️ ignoring accidental context

**A named skill, and the explanation is the best part of the source.**

- We perceive colour inside a context, subconsciously, **and it is not remotely certain that context will exist in your interior.**
- **⚠️ Her proof: Photoshop and other graphics programs have a grey background specifically so the background colour does not interfere with perception of the image.**
- **The worked case**: on a palette card a grey sits among lilacs, complex pinkish shades and aubergines. You conclude the grey contains pink pigment, because everything around it does. **But seen alone you may not see the pink at all — and even if it is there, in an interior with grey walls and an orange armchair against them, the orange may interact so that the pink disappears and the wall simply reads grey.** That neighbouring context will not be on your project.
- **⚠️ So look at one colour at a time.** Two methods: **carry a single chip («плашка»)** — shops sometimes have stands of them — rather than reading off a card that shows the neighbours; **or cut a hole in a sheet of paper and look through it at one colour**, which removes the visual noise.
- **⚠️ The flaw in the paper trick: white paper is itself very bright, and a white like white paper practically never occurs in an interior.** One manufacturer's viewing card solves this by being **deliberately not white but a neutral beige.**
- **⚠️ The same problem on site, and this one is easy to be caught by: pre-painting finished walls are usually white gypsum filler, and against that background neutral colours look far dirtier than they will in the finished interior once the white is gone.**

**⚠️ And the general statement behind all of it: brightness, lightness and warmth are properties only relative to other colours.** Her worked yellow is warm and fairly light on its own; against one pairing it is no longer warm; against another no longer light; against a third it regains both. *(The same relativity claim as `yU2zKPpDghM`, here demonstrated rather than asserted.)*

## Design Concept — ⚠️ testing under different lighting

**The largest single section, and the one with the most reusable technique.**

- **⚠️ The framing claim: "даже один цвет в разном освещении может дать гораздо больше разницы в тоне, чем два соседних цвета."** So agonising in the shop between two near-identical colours is the wrong problem.
- **Natural light constantly changes, and a room with west windows and a room with north windows have different light.** **⚠️ So test the samples *in the room that will be painted* — do not judge all the testers in the living room.**
- **⚠️ And at different times of day — morning, midday, evening, night.**
- **Perception also depends on the weather** — a sunny day against a rainy one.
- **⚠️ Some rooms' light depends on the season**: a bedroom with a tree outside the window is shaded by green foliage in summer, **which throws additional green reflexes**; in winter the light is colder with fewer shadows. **Nobody checks across seasons — too long to wait — but at least check the times of day.**
- **⚠️ The artificial-light problem on site, stated concretely: usually there is a builder's bulb of unknown origin hanging from the ceiling and nobody knows its colour temperature.**
  - **⚠️ Her fix, and it is a genuinely good one: use a variable-temperature LED panel — a common product photographers and videographers use. Set it to the temperature your lighting will actually be, e.g. 3000 K, hold it to the sample, and check the colour under exactly that light.**
  - **There are also dedicated lamps for testing samples under different lighting**, and she says one named shop definitely has one.
  - **Some manufacturers make three-dimensional samples** — she cites **Little Greene's room imitations**, which show the paint opposite the window, on side walls and on the ceiling.
- **⚠️ Reflexes: the paint reflects light and so do the surfaces near it.** Even after testing window and lamp light, an adjacent surface can throw a reflex that changes the colour — **especially relevant for neutral whitish, greyish and light shades.**

## Materials — ⚠️ the technical characteristics to know

She lists these deliberately without going deep, as things to know in theory and account for:

- **Укрывистость (opacity / hiding power)** — how many coats are needed to cover the wall.
- **Фактурность (texture)** — what grain is present: sand particles, fibres.
- **Washability** — whether it is washable, how washable, and whether it suits wet rooms.
- **⚠️ How much the paint reflects light — LRV (light reflectance value).** **"Если ваша задача сделать комнату как можно светлее" and you are choosing between two light paints, LRV can be the deciding argument.** A concrete, checkable spec for the daylight problem.
- **Fade resistance** — **and it can vary between shades from the same manufacturer.**
- **⚠️ Adhesion.** With cheaper manufacturers paint can fade faster and flake off; **with more expensive ones the paint is more flexible, so if the building settles, cracks may appear less quickly than on cheap paint.**
- **Composition, and its bearing on health.** Interiors are mostly water-emulsion, but variants exist — **oil-based paints can smell and emit harmful substances.**

## Materials — ⚠️ light versus dark, and gloss level

- **⚠️ Light paints are more practical than dark ones, and the reason is structural: under the paint there is always a light base** — light paintable wallpaper, or a prepared wall. **So a chip or a crack is far less noticeable on a light surface than on a dark one.**
- **⚠️ A trap within one manufacturer's own range, including expensive brands: dark paint and light paint have different gloss parameters even when sold in the same category.** Both may be labelled matte or super-matte, **yet the dark version can be glossier than the light one.** Worth checking rather than assuming.
- **The same manufacturer and the same colour can be supplied at different gloss levels on request.**

**The gloss ladder as she uses it:**

| Level | Use |
| :--- | :--- |
| **~5% gloss — plain matte, the default you are given** | Ordinary living interiors: living room, bedroom |
| **⚠️ ~15% gloss — the next level up** | **Her stated reason: the more gloss, the more latex in the composition, so it is more resistant to moisture and other impacts and easier to wash.** She specifies it for **a balcony or a tambour** — where there will be significant temperature swings but it is still an interior |
| **80–90% gloss** | **Very much a matter of taste and does not suit all styles.** Not for classic. **Possibly for Art Deco, or a 1960s–70s revival, which she notes is currently in fashion** |

**⚠️ And the question she says to ask before specifying high gloss: do you actually want the gloss on the *wall*, or would it be better on another plane — furniture, for instance?**

## Mistakes / Warnings

- Starting material selection with paint, or choosing paint without the floor and door samples in hand.
- Going to a supplier with thousands of colours when you do not need an exact match.
- Choosing colours for one room per visit across several days.
- Ordering a single tester rather than three.
- Judging from the fan deck; making testers smaller than A3; painting testers onto the wall.
- Leaving white paper or green plasterboard visible around a tester.
- Judging a colour from a palette card where its neighbours are visible.
- Judging a neutral against a white filler wall.
- Testing all samples in one room, at one time of day, under a builder's bulb of unknown temperature.
- Assuming matte means the same gloss for a dark and a light paint from the same range.
