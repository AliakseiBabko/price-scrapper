## Mistakes / Warnings — Designer/Foreman Coordination Failures

1. **Site-cleanliness heuristic, framed as a red flag ("бардак")**: a
   messy/disorganized job site reliably predicts a messy process — leftover
   debris, uncleared old fixtures ("выштробленные из-подрозетников" round
   junction-box cutouts left behind), etc. correlate with skipped/violated
   technology steps elsewhere on the same job. Used by the speaker as his
   own first on-site check when arriving at any object.
2. **Milled ("фрезерованный") vs. classic butt-jointed drywall corners for
   complex two-tier lit ceilings**: milling the drywall edge lets the
   painter form sharper, flatter external corners than the classic method
   (butt-joint two sheets, glue on a corner bead, then fill/sand) —
   important specifically because backlit reveal channels ("подсветка")
   visually magnify any corner imperfection. Milling costs more and takes
   longer but improves the finished look and reportedly durability.
   **Practical rule**: for any complex backlit drywall ceiling, ask the
   foreman up front which corner method will be used — it affects cost,
   timeline, and durability.
3. **Complex custom drywall/niche geometry needs a fully dimensioned
   drawing from the designer, not a verbal description** — a real defect
   traced back to this on this object: a gypsum decorative baseboard/
   trim piece ("гипсовый плинтус") specified for a ceiling niche perimeter
   didn't actually fit the niche once installed, because neither the
   designer's drawing nor the builder's own pre-check caught the size
   mismatch before the drywall was closed up. **Rule distilled**: for any
   custom node/niche in a project, always require a dimensioned drawing
   with exact measurements — skipping this costs rework time (and, per
   the speaker, client goodwill/nerves), even when the builder shares
   blame for not independently re-verifying dimensions before installing.
4. **Practicality/durability should be asked about explicitly, ranked
   above pure aesthetics** — the speaker's own stated priority order for
   any material/design choice is practicality first, durability second,
   beauty third, and recommends the client ask the designer directly: how
   is this cleaned, does it collect dust, how durable is it. **If a
   designer can't answer a practicality question about their own
   material choice, the speaker's advice is to find a different
   designer.**
5. **Textured/rough-surfaced glass block for a shower enclosure — an open
   practicality question raised, not a settled claim** (tag: uncertain/
   speaker's own open question, not a verified fact): the speaker
   questions whether a glass block with a rough/relief-textured face
   (installed rough-side-out here) will be harder to keep visibly clean
   than a smooth face, drawing an explicit comparison to his own home's
   tempered-glass shower screen, which visibly films over within 2-3 days
   of use despite being wiped down with a squeegee after every use by
   every family member. No resolution given in this video — flagged
   as a real-world open question about glass-block practicality in wet
   zones, worth tracking if a future source answers it.
6. **Material delivery deviating from spec causes cascading downstream
   defects — real example**: glass block specified in the project at 5cm
   thickness arrived on-site at 4cm. The tile installer had already
   pre-measured and laid a tile border strip sized to the specified 5cm
   before the glass block arrived, so the finished podium/border ended up
   roughly 1cm wider/off-center than designed — a visible asymmetry in
   the finished pattern, attributed to a spec-vs-delivery mismatch rather
   than installer error. **Rule distilled**: even when a design fee has
   been paid and a specific material spec provided, a pre-installation
   delivery-vs-spec dimension check is still needed before dependent
   trades proceed off the original spec.
7. **LED-strip transformer/driver placement for two-tier lit ceilings
   needs planned, ventilated, accessible storage** — mechanism stated
   explicitly: recessed LED lighting for multi-tier ceiling niches runs
   through transformers that must be (a) physically accessible/serviceable
   and (b) exposed to some air circulation, because they generate heat and
   can burn out if they overheat in a sealed cavity. A real conflict on
   this object: the designer's plan put decorative storage shelving in
   the same location the transformers needed, forcing a rework/relocation.
8. **Uncoordinated trade handoff — real defect**: the HVAC/ventilation
   ("приточно-вытяжная система") subcontractor installed their own
   control panel/junction box into a wall cavity location without
   checking it against the design plan, creating a conflict with the
   transformer-storage location above; had to be resolved after the fact.
9. **General rule: always actively coordinate/control sub-trades
   ("смежники")** — named categories: HVAC/AC installers, furniture
   makers, interior/entry door installers, window installers. Per the
   speaker, these trades typically work from their own internal
   templates/habits and don't reliably cross-reference the design
   project on their own initiative — either the client or a genuinely
   competent foreman must actively review their work against the design
   plan, or conflicts like the above recur.
10. **Real design-vs-access-clearance defect**: a dryer/washer mezzanine
    cabinet ("антресоль") was specified directly over/blocking the access
    hatch to the building's HVAC system, meaning the entire mezzanine
    cabinet has to be fully disassembled just to reach the ventilation
    system for routine service — flagged as a real oversight requiring a
    redesign with the furniture maker, since HVAC access hatches must stay
    reachable without dismantling built-in furniture.
