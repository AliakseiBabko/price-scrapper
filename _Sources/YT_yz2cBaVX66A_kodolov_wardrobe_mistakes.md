## Furniture / Built-ins — New Facts

- **⚠️ Deep-shelf "napkin-holder" retrieval problem, and the drawer fix**:
  in a deep wardrobe, pulling one folded item off a shelf can drag
  several other items with it (like pulling one napkin from a dispenser)
  — pull-out drawers avoid this because items come out bottom-to-top
  without disturbing neighbors. Real mechanism-based reasoning, not just
  a stated preference.
  - **Drawer material hierarchy for heavy contents**: cheap MDF/particle
    board (ДВП) can sag/break under heavy loads; ДСП (chipboard) is
    better; plywood (фанера) is better still. **But drawer runner/slide
    quality matters more than the drawer-box material** — cheap thin
    Chinese metal slides will loosen over time regardless of how rigid
    the drawer box itself is, causing rattling or poor open/close action.
- **⚠️ Shelf-depth rule for retrieval convenience**: a 60cm-deep shelf
  makes it hard to reach an item without disturbing others stacked in
  front; either narrow shelves to ~40cm depth, or reserve full 60cm
  depth for pull-out drawers, or accept 60cm shelves only for
  infrequently-accessed long-term storage. **Explicit exception: footwear
  stores fine even at 60-80cm shelf depth** — this rule doesn't apply to
  shoe storage.
- **Never pre-drill handle holes before handles are actually chosen** —
  a real documented case: a client ordered handles from England that
  looked fine but turned out to feel too heavy in hand once received,
  requiring a different handle; pre-drilled holes would have needed
  cosmetic filling. **Handle weight is something you cannot judge from
  photos/visuals alone — only holding it reveals it.** General planning
  rule: design/finalize hardware selection before any drilling, even
  though it means holes get drilled later in the process.
- **Door-type constraint from ceiling-mounted lighting**: hinged
  (swing-open) wardrobe doors can physically collide with ceiling spot
  lighting ("софиты") if the wardrobe and lighting were designed by
  separate parties without coordination — sliding doors avoid this
  conflict. General planning implication: wardrobe and ceiling-lighting
  layout must be coordinated in the same design pass, not designed
  independently by different trades/companies.
- **⚠️ Door-hinge-count economy is a false savings, with a concrete
  failure chain**: a heavy door under-specified with 3 hinges instead of
  a recommended 5 will eventually shed a screw (sometimes tearing loose
  material — plywood/ДСП — with it), starting as a slight door misalign/
  poor-closing symptom, escalating to a hinge fully detaching and a
  visibly warped cabinet. Also flagged: a light sensor triggered by a
  perpetually-slightly-open door can leave a light stuck on continuously
  as a secondary symptom of hinge failure.
- **Wardrobe design must be fully customized to actual owned-clothing
  types, not a generic template** — different rod/hanging-bar
  configurations serve short items (shirts) vs. long items (coats) vs.
  folded-only storage; the mix should be based on the specific person's
  real wardrobe composition, not a one-size-fits-all module layout.
- **Hardware-shipping quality signal**: reputable furniture factories
  ship hardware (hinges, slides, connectors) in individual boxes, not
  loose plastic bags — hardware loose in a bag can scratch the furniture
  surfaces during transit/handling. A cheap "corner bracket" (уголок)
  fastening method (vs. a proper factory dowel/cam fitting) is also
  flagged as a low-quality-shortcut signal, "used only in some genuinely
  rare edge cases, very rarely otherwise."
- **⚠️ "Race to the bottom" quote-shopping specifically produces a
  eurobolt/euroscrew (евровинт) downgrade you may not notice**: comparing
  quotes across contractors and picking the cheapest can land you on
  furniture assembled with visible eurobolt heads covered by small
  circular plastic caps (described as looking like children's round
  stickers) instead of the intended hidden-fastener joinery — a real,
  checkable visual quality signal for corner-cutting during assembly
  that's easy to miss without knowing to look for it.
- **Level-checked assembly (сборка по уровню) is mandatory, with a
  named failure mode**: a wardrobe assembled slightly out-of-level causes
  its own doors to sit permanently ajar or visibly crooked — even brand-
  new furniture, assembled poorly, visually reads as old/improperly-made
  rather than new.
- **Built-in lighting must be planned/wired before wardrobe assembly, not
  retrofitted** — once a wardrobe (e.g. shelf-embedded automatic lighting)
  is fully assembled, adding lighting afterward requires substantial
  disassembly (removing shelves/panels, routing new wiring, reassembling)
  — practically described as "rebuilding almost a whole new wardrobe."
  **Rule of thumb given for any late wardrobe modification: expect
  roughly double (×2) the original cost.**
- **Built-in ironing board verdict: not recommended**, from direct
  installed experience — folding/pull-down ironing boards inside a
  wardrobe were tried on real projects and found inconvenient in
  practice, especially for anything beyond very small items; a standalone
  standing/handheld garment steamer is recommended as the better
  alternative.
- **Facade material caution — natural wood veneer with milled
  grooves ("фрезеровка"), ~30cm door width**: described as
  visually attractive but requiring real design/material judgment to fit
  the surrounding interior; sun-exposure fading risk unless the wood is
  UV-resistant, and get significantly more design-sensitive at that width
  with visible milled detailing.
- **Open vs. closed wardrobe-facade trend and reasoning**: open/exposed-
  shelf fronts are currently out of favor because they "easily and always"
  create visual clutter; closed, minimalist facades are described as
  both currently and durably fashionable — with the durability claim
  conditioned on choosing a facade material/finish that doesn't fade or
  otherwise visibly age (e.g. sun-resistant natural wood).
