## Design Concept, Mistakes / Warnings

- **Trend/name-driven art-object selection critique, with a real named example**: buying a currently-fashionable collectible object purely because it's popular/recognizable, without regard to the room's actual color/texture/style. Names **Be@rbrick** (by Medicom Toy) specifically — once a genuine art-world collectible, now so ubiquitous it reads as generic/overused rather than distinctive («мовитон»). **Flag per the advertising/brand-mention filter** — a real product reference, not a commercial endorsement. Recommends matching art selection to actual interior style instead (old-master-style paintings/reproductions for classic rooms; minimalist graphic/abstract work or rare unique art objects for modern spaces).
- **⚠️ Art scale/proportion rule — a specific, checkable sizing principle**: a large-format painting overwhelms a small room; a miniature piece on a large wall in a spacious room reads lost/insignificant. For a large wall, choose one large piece OR a coordinated pair of large pieces; for limited wall space, miniatures/engravings/small posters read more appropriately. For a multi-piece "gallery wall" grouping of small pieces on a large wall, explicitly recommends measuring/planning the exact layout BEFORE hammering any nails rather than eyeballing it — reinforces the vault's existing measured-gallery-wall-layout technique, cross-reference.
- **Frame size/weight matters distinctly from the artwork itself**: a substantial/heavy frame can add visual "weight" to a small artwork, making it read as significant even in a large room, BUT if the frame competes visually with the artwork's own content, that itself becomes a new mistake — a specific frame-vs-content-balance nuance.
- **Over-accumulation of art/decor critique**: "too much art starts arguing with itself." Recommends explicitly designating ONE piece as the room's primary visual anchor with everything else playing a clearly secondary role (his own film analogy: main character vs. supporting cast), leaving genuine empty space/breathing room around the primary piece rather than treating every wall as available real estate, and for a deliberate multi-piece "home gallery" wall, grouping by color/style/theme with either clean symmetry or a deliberately organized asymmetric arrangement — the distinguishing factor between a good home gallery and clutter is VISIBLE INTENTIONALITY, not randomness.
- **Color-palette mismatch between art and room**: a bright neon-colored poster in an otherwise calm neutral room reads as visually combative. Recommends choosing art within the room's existing color family for a supporting role, OR deliberately using one boldly-colored piece as an intentional accent SPECIFICALLY when its colors are also echoed elsewhere in the room's textiles/small accessories (pillows, accent objects) — this cross-echoing is what makes a bold-colored artwork read as intentional rather than randomly clashing. Recommends physically test-holding a piece against the wall (or carefully visualizing it in place) before purchase, since a piece reading well in a gallery/showroom setting may read too loud or too invisible in the actual home context.
- **⚠️ Art hanging height — a refined numeric range, cross-reference against this vault's existing flat 150cm figure (Round 2, `4UJIa7eVlYo`) as a refinement, not a contradiction**: center of the artwork should sit at eye level, specifically **145–150cm** from floor to the piece's center.
- **⚠️ Art lighting — a specific point, distinct from general room-lighting content**: artwork placed in shadow effectively "disappears" visually, while artwork in direct sunlight fades/degrades over time. Recommends dedicated accent lighting (directional spotlights or built-in picture lights) specifically to both make the artwork visible AND add depth to the room.
- **Art selection by room function**: a large, dramatic, dark-toned painting can overwhelm/oppress a bedroom (where calm is the priority) but works well in a living room or entrance hall (where an impressive statement piece is appropriate); conversely, small graphic works or posters suit the more intimate atmosphere of a bedroom or home office.
- **⚠️ Overused pop-culture reproduction critique, with real named examples — flag all per the advertising/cultural-reference note**: mass-reproduced iconic pop-art/famous-painting prints (Andy Warhol soup cans/Marilyn Monroe portraits, Van Gogh's "Starry Night," Che Guevara portraits, the Mona Lisa) have become so ubiquitous across posters/mugs/phone cases that hanging a cheap reproduction of one actively signals a LACK of taste/consideration rather than genuine art appreciation, regardless of the original work's real artistic merit. His specific recommendation: if you want to reference pop art or a famous movement, choose a LESS ubiquitous work by the same artist/movement instead of the most over-reproduced icon (e.g. a lesser-known Leonardo da Vinci work instead of the Mona Lisa), or commission/purchase contemporary digital art from independent artists. Also names **Ansel Adams** black-and-white landscape photography, well-framed, as a specific example of achievable "gallery-quality" reproduction done well.
