## Design Concept, Furniture / Built-ins, Bathroom, Kids Room, Mistakes / Warnings

- **⚠️ Weathered/patinated vintage objects vs. new/glossy objects (Dunst's stated preference; Krasnov's added nuance)**: Dunst states she likes objects that show age/wear rather than looking restored — real example: a vintage ship-model lamp by **Ingo Maurer**, "Uchiwa"-type model from the 1970s, deliberately left unrestored ("потрёпан... это классика"). **Krasnov's own explicit added nuance**: this works because it's a FEW distressed pieces in an otherwise clean/well-kept room — a whole room of distressed pieces would read as genuinely neglected rather than characterful, not just "authentic."
- **⚠️ Personal-memory-object placement — a concrete first-person Krasnov studio case, worth its own entry**: an early Krasnov-studio project where a client initially wanted a smooth, minimalist interior with zero personal artifacts, but it turned out the client's father had been a photographer with a large collection of old cameras and family black-and-white photos. The studio assembled the cameras and photos into a dedicated display case/vitrine; the client specifically thanked them for pushing him to include it. Krasnov's own concrete case, used as a real-world argument for actively surfacing a client's dormant family/hobby collections rather than defaulting to a sterile look.
- **Full-room wallpaper coverage (Dunst's own choice, noted approvingly by Krasnov) — a nuance against this vault's extensive existing "one accent wall only" content**: Lake August wallpaper covering an entire room (not just one wall) — recorded as "full-room wallpaper CAN work when deliberately chosen, per this designer's real choice," a counterpoint case, not a universal recommendation, alongside this vault's many existing accent-wall-only sources.
- **⚠️ Kids'-room critique bundle (Krasnov's own critique of the child's room in this house)**:
  - An entire room saturated in a single deep, muted, heavy color (dark green, "угрюмый лес"/gloomy forest) reads oppressive specifically for a CHILD's room, even though such deep/muted colors can work fine in an office/library/country-house context.
  - A light fixture shaped like a traffic-signal light flagged as thematically random with no connection to anything else in the room.
  - Insufficient/unclear main light source flagged as a genuine functional gap — a dark-toned kids' room needs MORE deliberate lighting planning, not less, since it will read genuinely gloomy at night without a clear main light source.
  - A bold, dark, room-covering wallpaper pattern on all four walls (not just one) in the kids' room specifically critiqued as creating an oppressive "enclosed" feeling. **Krasnov explicitly catches a real self-contradiction**: Dunst stated earlier in the same interview that she dislikes accent walls, yet has all four walls of the kids' room in one bold pattern — recorded as a specific, real inconsistency he identified, not smoothed over.
  - A dark ceiling in the same kids' room questioned as unnecessary additional heaviness.
  - **⚠️ Mixed/unclear room-function zoning**: the room combines a crib/bed, a sofa, and rattan furniture with no clear zoning logic, making it unclear whether the space functions as bedroom, playroom, or living room — the child himself reportedly calls it his "living room," not his bedroom, cited as evidence of the ambiguity. **Krasnov's stated OWN studio practice for kids' rooms, even much smaller ones**: deliberately zone distinct areas for play, study, and sleep so each has clear spatial logic, rather than blending functions without visual/spatial distinction.
- **Dark/moody kitchen critique with a stated compensation technique**: a dark kitchen is not inherently a mistake (Krasnov's own explicit position — "кто-то любит один цвет, кто-то другой"), but a FULLY dark kitchen makes the whole space read visually "compressed"/smaller. **His stated OWN studio practice when doing a dark kitchen as a deliberate accent**: keep the SURROUNDING spaces/background colors light, so the dark kitchen functions as a genuine accent against a light backdrop rather than the whole space reading uniformly dark and cramped.
- **Crackled/aged ceramic tile finish ("craquelure," his own word, comparing it to old paintings)** — the tile's crackle-glaze surface texture reflects light in a visually appealing, "aged"/characterful way, compared explicitly to how natural light falls on an old painting in a museum.
- **⚠️ Historic maiolica tile backsplash flagged as real, expensive, genuinely inaccessible (16th-century tile per the transcript)** — Krasnov gives a specific, actionable substitution: simple ceramic tile with an antiqued/aged finish (wide price range, including vintage-style options), or natural/engineered stone (praised for inherently unique natural pattern) as an alternative route to a similar "connection to age/nature" quality.
- **⚠️ Furniture-as-collectible-investment principle, with a third named design reference**: a **Frits Henningsen** prototype chair, described in the video as one of only ~5 ever made, most existing examples reportedly not authentic per Dunst's own claim — used as an example of collecting genuine design-history pieces as long-term investments rather than trend-following purchases. Extends this vault's existing "invest in design-history pieces" content (Eames, Barcelona chair, Round 4) with this third named example. Flagged per the advertising/brand-mention filter, framed as design-history/collectible commentary rather than a commercial endorsement.
- **⚠️ Freestanding corner bathtub — a strongly-stated critique, genuinely new to this vault's bathroom content (existing content covers shower placement/slope, not tub corner-placement)**: a bathtub placed in a room corner with no direct access on multiple sides is "a nightmare to clean" even with professional cleaning help — the wet, dark, hard-to-reach corner becomes an "excellent breeding ground for mold." **Krasnov's explicit personal rule: he would never place a bathtub in a corner regardless of how it looks.**
- **A specific styling/functionality tradeoff acknowledgment (Krasnov's own stated position, an attributed opinion, not a universal rule)**: some clients deliberately sacrifice ergonomic comfort for a specific aesthetic effect (a beautiful but genuinely uncomfortable stool/chair used more as a sculptural object than daily seating) — he explicitly states this is a legitimate choice as long as it's a deliberate, understood tradeoff rather than an accidental oversight.
- **A critique of over-accessorizing/competing visual accents in a living room** — too many bold/eye-catching pieces in one room creates a "visual argument" where none reads as a clear focal point; his suggested fix is to remove one or two competing bold pieces rather than adding more.
- **A simple painted stick hung on a wall** used as a specific example that genuine personal/sentimental objects don't need to be expensive or conventional to work as wall decor.
- **A shower-curtain rail that only extends partway along an exposed wooden beam** flagged as a functional gap — the curtain doesn't fully close/cover, a concrete installation-quality critique.
