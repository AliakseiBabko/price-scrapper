- **Permit-before-work sequencing rule, stated as mandatory, not
  optional**: obtain replanning approval *before* cutting a load-bearing
  wall opening, not after — two stated reasons: (1) some openings are
  simply prohibited by the building's structural engineering regardless
  of reinforcement, so approval can reveal a hard no before any work is
  done; (2) the approved plan itself specifies exact required reference
  points (offset from the building's exterior wall, opening width/
  height, and the specific steel-angle article numbers/specs needed for
  reinforcement) — building to a pre-approved spec means the subsequent
  inspection is far more likely to pass cleanly.

## Materials / Regulations-Adjacent — Opening Formation & Reinforcement Technique

- **Layout/marking method**: transfer the approved plan's reference
  points (worked example: 80cm offset from the exterior wall, 90cm
  opening width, 2.10m opening height) onto one wall face with a pencil,
  then transfer the same points to the opposite face — **for a thick
  wall, don't rely on manually re-measuring the second face; drill
  through from the first face at several points and connect the resulting
  holes on the second face**, guaranteeing precise alignment through the
  wall's full thickness rather than trusting two independent
  measurements to agree.
- **Cutting method — an explicit prohibition plus the correct technique**:
  **never use a jackhammer/breaker or any high-impact tool** to open a
  load-bearing wall — impact force risks damaging the wall beyond the
  intended opening. **Correct method**: drill a dense perimeter of holes
  (spacing ~3-4cm, diameter 10mm) around the full opening outline, then
  connect them — a specialized company uses a wall-saw; a DIY approach
  uses a large angle grinder with a diamond blade, one person cutting
  while a second holds a construction vacuum directly at the cut point,
  swapping roles periodically since the work is physically taxing.
- **Controlled-demolition safety rule**: never remove the freed wall
  section as one large piece — **divide it into roughly 10-12 pieces
  (~40×40cm each)** and remove them individually. **Explicit floor-
  protection rule**: lay a wood-plank deck on the floor first, since a
  large falling chunk of concrete/block can otherwise damage the slab —
  and, in a multi-story building, potentially damage the ceiling of the
  unit directly below.
- **Steel reinforcement specification, worked example (this project's
  actual approved spec)**: perimeter framing angle iron 100×100mm at
  10mm thickness; header (top) reinforcement angle iron 100×100mm at
  10mm thickness; sill/bottom reinforcement angle iron 75×75mm at 8mm
  thickness; connecting/tie rebar at 12mm diameter — **all figures
  sourced from this project's own approved engineering spec**, not a
  universal standard — flagged explicitly as project-specific, though
  the overall reinforcement *method* below is general.
- **Common beginner mistake in pinning the reinforcement angle iron to
  the wall, and the correct alternative**: **do not drill straight
  through the angle iron's face into the wall** to anchor it — a wall's
  own existing rebar sits at a shallow, predictable depth, and a
  through-drilled anchor at that spot risks hitting and damaging it.
  **Correct method**: drill anchor pins at an angle (offset from
  straight-through) so the pin path avoids the wall's own rebar layer,
  then weld the angle iron to these offset pins — securing the
  reinforcement without the risk of damaging the wall's structural rebar.
- **Weld-seam completeness rule, a second commonly-missed detail**: weld
  connecting cross-plates not just where they meet the flat face of the
  angle iron, but **also along their end/edge faces** — the same applies
  to the top and bottom framing angle irons themselves: weld along both
  the contact face *and* the end faces, not just the face-to-face seam.
- **Rebar-dowel bending technique for tying reinforcement into the
  surrounding wall**: rather than the common beginner approach (cut
  rebar to length leaving a ~45cm tail, then cold-bend it directly against
  the frame — flagged as producing an uneven bend, a tail that's too
  short in places, or an unclosable gap in the middle), the source's
  method: **drill the anchor hole at a 45° angle to the wall plane**,
  insert straight rebar into it, then **weld/heat only the segment
  adjacent to the anchor point** — the localized heat lets that segment
  bend cleanly by hand, producing a precise right-angle bend at exactly
  the needed point. Then trim the rebar to length with a small angle
  grinder and complete the weld to the angle iron.
- **Finish steps**: clean/grind all weld seams smooth, paint the entire
  completed steel assembly (corrosion protection), then fill the
  remaining gap between the steel frame and the surrounding masonry with
  M200 or M300 cement-sand mix before scheduling the inspection
  commission's acceptance visit.
