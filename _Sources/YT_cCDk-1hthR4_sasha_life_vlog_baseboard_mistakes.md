## Mistakes / Warnings — Baseboard (Plinth) Installation, First-Time DIY

- **A pre-existing wall protrusion/corner obstruction blocked the intended
  baseboard run** — the source wanted the baseboard to run continuously
  along one wall section, but a fixed wall-mounted corner element in the
  way could not be removed or trimmed cleanly, forcing a visibly awkward
  baseboard-to-obstruction junction rather than the clean run originally
  planned. **Generalizable planning point**: survey a wall for any fixed
  protrusions/corner elements along the planned baseboard run *before*
  purchasing baseboard length/corner-piece counts, since working around
  one mid-installation costs both material and finish quality.
- **Thick electrical wiring discovered running inside the wall exactly
  where they needed to cut/knock back wall material with a hammer drill
  (перфоратор) to seat the baseboard flush** — two thick cables running
  vertically inside the wall at that spot. **They could not safely remove
  the wiring or cut into the wall there**, so they left a visible gap
  between baseboard and wall at that section rather than risk damaging
  the electrical run. **Concrete, checkable safety/planning rule**: before
  hammer-drilling or chiseling a wall to seat trim flush, check for hidden
  electrical routing at that location — a real live-wiring conflict can
  force a permanent visible installation compromise, not just a delay.
- **A doorway threshold (порожек) that had been cut/trimmed poorly by a
  previous installer** (from an earlier stage of this same renovation —
  a separate trade, not this baseboard-installation session) left a gap at
  the threshold that could not be corrected without replacing the
  threshold piece entirely — removing/re-cutting it risked further
  breakage. The household's own proposed fix at the time (trimming it
  further to close the gap) was overruled by whoever installed it
  originally. **Recorded as a real QC handoff failure between renovation
  stages**: a trim-cutting mistake made by one installer/stage became a
  permanent visible defect the household had to accept and work around in
  a later, unrelated stage (baseboard fitting).
- **General first-time-installer outcome, stated candidly by the source
  herself**: multiple visible gaps/imperfections resulted from this first
  attempt, at corners and around the two obstruction points above; the
  household's own assessment was "not great, but acceptable for a first
  attempt," with a plan to touch up with paint/caulk rather than redo the
  installation. A realistic first-time-DIY quality baseline, not an
  idealized result.
- **Baseboard/corner-piece planning around irregular walls**: where a wall
  bulge, obstruction, or previous-trade defect can't be resolved, cutting
  the baseboard to size and accepting a paint-concealable gap (rather than
  forcing a structural fix mid-installation) was the household's practical
  compromise — a real, checkable fallback strategy other DIYers can expect
  to need in an older/irregular apartment.
