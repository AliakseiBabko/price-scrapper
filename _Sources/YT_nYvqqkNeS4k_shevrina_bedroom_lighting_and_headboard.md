## ⚠️ Electrical / Lighting — Three Bedroom Scenarios

She treats all three as obligatory in a bedroom.

### Scenario 1 — main ceiling light

Tracks and spots. **⚠️ She argues against relying on a chandelier alone: it does not light the room's corners adequately.** If a decorative pendant is wanted, **add at least one spot in each corner**.

**But she legitimises skipping ceiling light entirely** where the bedroom is genuinely only used for sleeping — her own previous flat had no ceiling light in the bedroom, no make-up or work zone, and she says that is a perfectly valid scenario rather than a compromise. Useful counterweight to the standard "always plan three scenarios" advice.

### Scenario 2 — atmospheric evening light, on its own switch

**⚠️ It must be both dimmed AND diffuse** — she repeats "рассеянный" deliberately, because a narrow-beam fixture cannot do this job. Candidates: a diffuse pendant, floor lamp, table lamp, or an LED strip.

**⚠️ The headboard LED strip mistake, with the correct construction.** The common approach — cut a niche and glue the strip in — **glares directly at you when you lie down or lean against the headboard**, and she calls it a coarse and very common error. **Correct detail: build a plasterboard backing and mount the strip in a corner profile so it throws light INTO the ceiling**, so only light reflected off ceiling and wall reaches your eyes. This is a construction-level version of the "mount it to throw upward" rule already recorded from `NfHyCfo1J4w` in Round 1.

**⚠️ Evening/atmospheric light must be warm — not above 3000 K** — because cool light rouses and warm light promotes sleep. She invokes this as settled ("с наукой не поспоришь"), which is a claim about circadian response rather than a design preference; recorded as her stated rationale.

### Scenario 3 — functional light

Light for named activities. Walk your actual bedroom functions — reading in bed, a lounge chair — and specify for each.

## ⚠️ The Distinction That Makes This Source Valuable: Diffuse Light Cannot Be a Night Light

**Diffuse light spreads through the whole room, so switching it on at night wakes the person beside you.** For getting up at night you need the opposite: **a directional fixture with a narrow beam — up to about 60°** — lighting only a small patch, e.g. the floor on your side of the bed. **The same fixture serves for reading in bed** while the other person sleeps, lighting only your page.

Diffuse light *can* work as a night light **only if it sits at a low level** — skirting backlighting — or is a small wall fixture, or an LED strip placed around a corner or in the passage where a wardrobe blocks it from the other person.

Her framing: **"думайте о втором человеке, который не должен просыпаться от ваших действий по ночам."**

The 60° figure is internally consistent with her kitchen video (`6y3UiXx9NQI`, Round 2), where she cites ~60° as the typical spot beam and treats the narrowness as a *problem*; here the same narrowness is the *virtue*. Same physical fact, opposite application — a good sign the number is real rather than rhetorical.

## ⚠️ Floor Lamps and Table Lamps — Plan the Switching

Table lamps usually have a switch on the body. **Floor lamps usually have an inline switch on the cable, some distance from the body, so you end up bending down or fishing for it with a foot.** Two fixes she names:

- **A socket with its own integral switch.**
- **Wire the low socket to a wall switch at bedside height**, so the wall switch controls that socket.

Small, cheap, and has to be decided at the electrical-planning stage — which is exactly the class of thing this vault's electrical pages exist for.

## Materials — What Those Headboard Walls Actually Are

A reverse-engineering taxonomy, useful for anyone working from reference photos:

- **Gypsum panels** (гипсовые панели).
- **A trowelled decorative-plaster texture** — she notes this one is **genuinely DIY-able with a square trowel**, or done by a decorative-plaster applicator.
- **MDF panels in veneer**, or **laminated chipboard** (she names Egger as a recognisable laminate).
- **Fabric wallpaper** — the "looks like textile on the wall" effect.
- **Microcement / decorative plaster.**
- **⚠️ Recessed groove/slot details made with a dedicated profile**, set into the wall at rough stage and then painted over. **She is emphatic that this has superseded MDF-panels-in-enamel for producing the same rhythm** — "если вы где-то услышали, что это обязательно МДФ-панели в эмали, то не надо бежать и тратиться; просто возьмите профиль, это недорогое удовольствие." Third statement of this from the channel (also `XGI6FS2ZdCc`, Round 1).
- **Porcelain**, or **wide-format ceramic**.
- **Upholstered headboards** — made by sofa and bed manufacturers rather than joinery shops.

**⚠️ This resolves an inconsistency flagged in Round 2.** `z4G-ocStu9o` gave thin ceramic as "up to 6 mm"; `KI2GvB0jzHs` gave "3–5 mm". Here she gives **"примерно 3–6 мм, плиты около 3 м × 1 м"**. **Read the reconciled figure as ~3–6 mm thick, slabs up to about 3 m × 1 m.**

## Design Concept — Headboard and Opposite Wall

- **⚠️ Do not make both the headboard wall and the wall opposite the bed accent walls.** Too heavy, and it reads as visual overload. **Accent the headboard; lighten the wall opposite.**
- Ways to keep the opposite wall interesting without overloading it: if the walls are already an interesting material (fabric wallpaper, microcement) that is sufficient; if the walls are plain paint, **add the recessed groove profiles for a light rhythm** — cheap; or hang **one large but restrained picture**, ideally in a colour you will enjoy looking at before sleep; or repeat the headboard's wall panels opposite, provided the headboard itself isn't overloaded.
- **⚠️ Cost myth-busting, stated twice**: the belief that slot/shadow-gap junctions are expensive is a myth — **not expensive in labour, and negligible relative to the whole renovation budget.** She cross-references her own budget video, which is `UDrpyZE_V38` (Round 2) where the actual figures appear.

## Furniture — Headboards and Pillows

- **⚠️ Ask whether you actually lean against the headboard.** She never does. **A soft headboard plus its cushions consumes 25–30 cm** of room depth. **Alternative: buy a bed without a headboard — they are cheaper — and build the headboard as wall panels**, so it becomes "архитектурный элемент, часть стены, а не часть кровати," which she says reads better and suits minimalism.
- **⚠️ Pillows: at most four, in two rows.** Don't buy decorative square cushions, and skip the "central pillow" — she calls it a classical device with no place in minimalism, "и бесполезная, и некрасивая."
- **⚠️ Elongated pillows read better than square ones** — the same "people prefer horizontal forms" argument she applies to tile format and to screens. This is now the third distinct application of that principle across her catalogue (tile, devices, pillows), which is worth noting as a consistent underlying claim rather than three separate opinions.
