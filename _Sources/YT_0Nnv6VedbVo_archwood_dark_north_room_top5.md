## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules

### 1. Color-selection adjustment for low-light rooms

- **A light/pale color in a genuinely dark room shifts hue and reads
  duller than the same color would in a sunlit room** — shadow pulls any
  light tone toward gray, and a nearby colored object (a tree, a
  brightly-colored building across the window) can tint that gray toward
  its own hue (e.g. gray skewing toward lilac/green). Compensate by
  choosing paint and upholstery colors noticeably more saturated
  ("сочнее") than you would for an equivalent sunlit room — everything
  in a dark room will otherwise look visually diluted.
- **Avoid pale intermediate tones (light gray, light beige, light blue)
  specifically in low-light rooms** — insufficient light for accurate
  color perception makes these read as a dingy/dirty white rather than
  the intended pastel. A gentle pastel palette with subtle tone
  transitions reads worse, not better, under low natural light.
- **Use the brightest white in a paint line (or one shade brighter than
  usual) for a dark room**, rather than the muted warm white preferred
  for a sunlit room, and pair it with saturated accent colors (turquoise,
  purple) for contrast. If a muted palette is still wanted, achieve it
  through white contrast *accents* (piped/edged cushions, a wall
  arrangement of white photo frames) rather than through the wall paint
  itself.

### 2. Reflective-surface placement rule

- **Do not use mirrored or glossy ceilings in a dark room** — a poorly-
  lit gray ceiling just reflects more darkness, and the mismatch between
  a visually "taller-reading" ceiling and the room's actual floor area
  creates a claustrophobic "well" effect.
- **Only place reflective/mirrored surfaces where real light already
  reaches** — a mirrored wardrobe door facing away from the window, in
  the room's darkest corner, only reflects darkness back into the room.
- **"Mirror window reveal" technique**: mirror insert panels on the left
  and right reveals of a window (not a full mirror frame including the
  sill) make the window appear to extend further/larger by reflecting
  the window opening itself. Curtains should slightly overlap the
  window-opening edge for the illusion to read correctly.

### 3. Curtain guidance for a small window in a dark room

- **Avoid fully covering a small window even with sheer tulle** — tulle
  still absorbs/blocks a meaningful fraction of already-scarce light;
  keep the window fully uncovered by day where psychologically
  comfortable. Where a fully open window feels uncomfortable (facing
  another building directly, or a low floor), select curtain fabric
  properties carefully and combine multiple curtain types for different
  times of day (blackout at night, fully retracted by day) rather than
  one fixed treatment.

### 4. Paint/surface finish selection by sheen

- **Matte paint absorbs light and reads "dull/flat" in an already-dark
  room** — a matte-finish dark-toned volume (e.g. kitchen cabinetry)
  placed in the room's far dark corner, especially if turned side/back
  to the light source, becomes visually one large uniform dark blob.
- **Glossy paint reflects maximum light but reads "vulgar" and visually
  fractures a room if overused** — use glossy surfaces sparingly, in
  small details only, regardless of the room's actual light level (not
  a dark-room-specific caution).
- **Semi-matte/eggshell is the recommended middle ground for a dark
  room's walls/wallpaper** — absorbs less light than full matte without
  acting as a mirror the way gloss does in a dark corner; the goal is a
  faint diffuse light patch, not a specular reflection.

### 5. Furniture "visual lightness" rule

- **Furniture with visible legs (sofa/bed on legs), light/glass-top
  tables, and generally "lighter"-reading pieces reduce the volume of
  heavy cast shadow in a dark room** — more visible floor area (helped
  further by a light-colored floor) reads as more open/brighter, even
  without changing the actual light level.
- **Avoid velvet/velour upholstery in a dark room** — this fabric
  depends on visible light-play across its pile to look good, and reads
  better in bright rooms; a medium-tone natural-fiber sofa is a
  stronger choice for a dark room specifically.
- **Avoid large dark-colored furniture pieces (dark bedspreads, dark
  beds/sofas/wardrobes) in a dark room** — any dark-toned volume reads
  even darker than its actual shade once placed in a low-light space.
