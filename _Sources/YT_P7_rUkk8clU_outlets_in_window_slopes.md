## Durable Facts

- **Why window-slope electrical points exist at all**: modern renovation commonly boxes in radiator piping behind a false wall/slope panel for a cleaner look, which incidentally widens the usable windowsill — the speaker recommends using this newly-available space for electrical points (both outlets and switches), not just as a shelf.
- **Two-way (проходной) bedside switch use case, restated**: a second switch for the main room light, placed in a window-slope panel near the bed, lets the light be turned off without walking to the entry switch — same underlying rule as in the companion source `iBPJimtw7k0`, given here with the added reasoning that a switch mounted *above a desk* (an alternative placement) is a worse choice because the user must reach across the desk and the switch tends to get hidden behind desk clutter (monitor, printer, etc.). **Recommended placement: in the window slope beside/below the desk, not above it.**
- **Slope-mounted outlet near a desk**: recommended specifically for phone/device charging at a desk without running a cable across the desk to a farther outlet — described as commonly used for 2-3 devices (e.g., monitor plus phone charger).
- **Safety myth-debunking — condensation cannot short/electrocute**: the speaker directly addresses a common customer fear ("won't condensation form in there and cause a short/shock?") with the physics explanation that (a) pure water does not conduct electricity — dissolved salt in water is what conducts, and (b) condensation only forms when a cold object is moved into warm humid air and its surface temperature drops below the dew point; since the temperature inside a window-slope cavity is the same as the room's ambient temperature (not artificially cold), no dew point crossing occurs and no condensation forms on the outlet's metal contacts — analogized to a kitchen knife sitting in a kitchen not fogging up either. `unverified` (physics reasoning stated by a non-specialist source), but internally consistent and worth noting as this source's stated justification, not to be repeated as fact without the same caveat.
- **Safety myth-debunking — rain ingress**: rain cannot reach a slope-mounted outlet because for rain to enter the room at all the window sash would already have to be open, and an open sash opens toward/over the slope, physically shielding the outlet.
- **Safety myth-debunking — overheating/fire risk**: the circuit breaker is sized to trip from overcurrent well before the cable or outlet contacts themselves would reach a temperature capable of igniting the surrounding panel material — presented as a general property of correctly-sized modern circuit protection, not something specific to slope-mounted outlets.
- **Installation sequence**: (1) feed the supply cable through the false-wall/piping-box framework early, during framework assembly, leaving it hanging loose along the old slope location; (2) drywall/finish work (plastering, wallpapering/painting) proceeds around it; (3) only at the very end are the slope panels themselves cut, test-fitted, then removed again to apply mounting foam to their back face; (4) once the foam has partially cured (still tacky, not hard), the panel is test-fitted again and trimmed/adjusted if the foam interferes with a flush fit; (5) holes for the electrical points are cut into the panel and the cable is pulled through; (6) a second, fresh layer of foam is applied — timed so this layer is what actually bonds the panel to the wall — and only then is the panel permanently fixed in place.
- **Why panels must be glued (not just clipped/screwed) to the slope structure**: unglued panels flex when a plug is inserted/removed, which over time can damage the panel joint or the sealant bead running along the windowsill; the speaker demonstrates that a properly glued installation resists his full body weight pressing on the outlet without deflecting.
- **Mounting hardware for the outlet itself**: no additional foam/adhesive is used to fix the socket box into the drywall-panel slope — a standard drywall-specific socket box (with spring/wing tabs that clamp the panel from behind) is sufficient; panel adhesion to the wall structure (via the foam step above) is what actually carries the mechanical load, not the socket box's own grip on the thin panel material.
- **Quality red flag**: if a slope-mounted outlet wobbles or comes loose/pulls out along with its faceplate when a plug is removed, that indicates a bad installation (usually meaning the panel itself was never properly glued to the structure) — described as a simple, checkable test a homeowner can perform.
