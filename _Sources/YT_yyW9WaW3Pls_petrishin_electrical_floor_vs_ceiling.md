## Color-Coded Conduit Convention

**Black conduit** = power/outlet circuits; **gray conduit** = lighting
circuits (chandeliers, sconces, switches); **orange conduit** =
low-voltage/data circuits (satellite TV, cable TV, internet, CCTV). Not
required (a single conduit color works functionally), but makes the
installation self-documenting and easier to troubleshoot later — the
electrician's own idea on this project.

## Decision Framework: When to Route via Ceiling vs. Floor

**Ceiling routing recommended when**: (1) the client is fine with a
stretch ceiling, which hides the conduit runs; (2) the client is fine
with a drywall ceiling drop — doesn't need to be a full room-wide drop,
a partial perimeter box with recessed lighting works; (3) the developer's
existing poured screed is already flat/good quality, so there's no
reason to pour a new screed just to bury floor wiring — cheaper to route
via ceiling and hide with drywall/stretch ceiling instead; (4) the client
is keeping the existing floor covering unchanged (e.g. an expensive
parquet/art-parquet floor) — since the floor won't be opened up anyway,
new wiring has to go via the ceiling.

**Floor routing recommended when**: (1) the client cares about every
centimeter of finished ceiling height — ceiling routing unavoidably
costs 3-5cm of height for the drywall profile/board (or a stretch
ceiling's own depth) needed to hide the conduit, which floor routing
avoids entirely; (2) a new screed is being poured anyway — typical
new-build screed thickness (4-6cm) is enough to bury wiring/conduit for
free, since the pour is happening regardless of the wiring decision.

## Structural Reasoning: Why Floor Routing Is Usually Cheaper for Outlets

**Outlets and switches are always positioned in the lower part of a
wall, regardless of apartment layout.** Floor-routed wiring only needs a
short wall chase from the floor up to the outlet/switch box (roughly
30-50cm); ceiling-routed wiring needs a *full-height* wall chase from the
ceiling all the way down to the same box — meaning more wire length,
more chasing labor, and correspondingly more cost specifically for
outlet/switch circuits when routed via the ceiling. Lighting fixtures
themselves (chandeliers, ceiling lights) don't carry this penalty since
they're already at ceiling height.

## Combined/Hybrid Routing Rationale on This Specific Object

The developer (ЖК Аэробус) requires whole-apartment soundproofing using
Шумонет-100, which itself mandates a minimum 6cm screed pour — so burying
the main power/lighting wire bundle under that pour costs nothing extra.
**Low-voltage cabling (data/TV/satellite/CCTV) was deliberately routed
via the ceiling instead**, for two reasons stated directly: (1) avoiding
an excessively thick, overlap-prone floor wire bundle (more crossings =
more installation risk); (2) avoiding overloading the ceiling with the
*entire* wire bundle, which would have complicated the ceiling
soundproofing frame's own installation. A deliberate density-management
split, not a personal-preference choice.

## Flood-Safety Reassurance Claim (Business Experience, Not a Technical Test)

The source states that routing location (floor screed vs. ceiling) makes
no meaningful difference to flood safety, citing: conduit protection,
multi-layer wire insulation, and circuit breakers/RCD protection that
should de-energize the circuit on a ground fault regardless of where the
wire physically runs. Cites 9 years/700+ completed projects with zero
client callbacks for flood-related rewiring — `single-account`, a
business-experience claim, not an independent test; relevant context for
this round's separate radiator-flood case study (video 6) but not itself
a documented incident.

## Wire Non-Replaceability Once Installed in Conduit — a Counter-Intuitive Caution

**⚠️ States directly that pulling a wire back out of an already-installed
conduit run for replacement is not realistically possible**, regardless
of floor or ceiling routing — worth flagging against the common
assumption that conduit exists specifically to allow future wire pulling/
replacement without demolition. `single-account`, `unverified` — a
genuine caution worth checking against other sources' conduit-replacement
claims if one is found.

## Junction/Splice Placement: Deepened Switch/Outlet Boxes Instead of Hidden Wall Boxes

**Avoid inaccessible junction/splice boxes hidden behind wallpaper,
panels, or a plain wall patch.** This installation instead routes all
lighting-circuit splicing into deepened switch-box recesses at the
switch locations themselves — any future troubleshooting or
re-commutation just means removing the switch mechanism, not opening a
wall.

## Conduit-to-Screed Fixing Technique Over Floor Soundproofing

**Where floor soundproofing (Шумонет) is present, conduits must not be
screw-anchored directly through it into the structural screed below** —
drilling through the soundproofing membrane creates a rigid "sound
bridge" that defeats its purpose (sound would cross from neighbor to
neighbor, or from this unit's own floor finish, through the screw
penetration). Instead, conduits are tied to a **wire mesh laid loosely
over the soundproofing layer** (no fasteners through the membrane); where
no soundproofing is present, conduits are perforated-tape-strapped
directly to the screed with masonry screws instead. A separate patch
("заплатка") is applied specifically where a conduit run crosses from
the floor up onto a wall, to prevent a direct rigid screed-to-wall
contact bridge at that transition point.
