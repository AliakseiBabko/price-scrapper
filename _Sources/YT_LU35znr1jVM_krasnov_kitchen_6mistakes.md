## Mistakes / Warnings — the six named mistakes

- **⚠️ Fridge must be flush-integrated, with mandatory ventilation clearances**: a fridge that visibly protrudes from the cabinetry line reads as an afterthought and can obstruct neighbouring cabinet doors or the walkway. Fix: a depth-matched integrated panel, or a purpose-built niche with side panels/overhead cabinetry visually absorbing the fridge into the run. **Minimum ventilation clearances**: **2 cm sides, 2.5 cm back, 2–4 cm top** — check the specific manufacturer's spec, since skipping this overheats the compressor, raises energy use, and can shorten the appliance's service life. Built-in fridges are not inherently pricier than freestanding — **the expensive configuration specifically is side-by-side** (two built-ins or a single larger freestanding unit are both cheaper alternatives at equal capacity).
- **⚠️ Upper-cabinet-to-ceiling gap** is called out as an instant "mass-market" tell, especially common with stock/modular (non-custom) kitchens where cabinet height is fixed but ceiling height varies. The empty strip visually lowers and cheapens the room, and it's a dust/grease trap that's awkward to clean and adds no storage value. **Three fixes**: cabinets built to full ceiling height; a second tier of upper cabinets (recovers storage for seasonal/rarely-used items); or, where neither fits, a decorative panel/cornice/dropped-ceiling detail closing the gap. Best overall answer: a made-to-order (not stock-modular) kitchen sized to the actual ceiling height.
- **Microwave over the cooktop**: breaks the visual line of the upper-cabinet run, becomes an off-centre focal point at eye level, and in practice usually forces sacrificing a proper cooker hood for that position — with the usual downside of grease/steam settling on nearby fronts and walls. **Preferred placement: microwave stacked with (below, or paired with) a built-in wall oven as one compact module** — a now-standard, widely-used configuration. **Ergonomic rule for the module: microwave's lower edge at roughly chest height**, so hot dishes don't need to be lifted overhead.
- **⚠️ Kitchen backsplash (фартук) is functionally necessary, despite the current no-backsplash Pinterest trend**: a bare painted/plastered wall behind the worktop picks up grease, water, and steam stains within a few months and becomes progressively harder to clean back to original. Modern alternatives beyond classic tile: tempered/toughened glass panels, porcelain slab, acrylic/aluminium panels, stainless-steel panels — all wipeable and resistant to water/heat/cleaning chemicals. For a minimalist look without sacrificing protection, a backsplash colour-matched to the wall "disappears" visually while keeping full protective function.
- **⚠️ Kitchen ergonomics, a cluster of figures**: **countertop height default 86 cm**, but **92–95 cm for tall users** — height should be chosen per household, not defaulted. Rule of thumb: standing straight with the elbow bent 90°, leave **10–15 cm clearance** between palm and countertop surface. **Upper-cabinet lower edge: 55–60 cm above the countertop** (too high strains reach; too low risks head strikes during fast movement). **Walkway/aisle width: 100–110 cm** between cabinet runs/island/wall so two people can pass and open doors/drawers without colliding; **90 cm minimum in genuinely compact kitchens.**
- **⚠️ The classic "work triangle" (sink–stove–fridge), fully quantified**: **sink-to-fridge 1.2–2 m**; **sink-to-stove ≈1 m**; **stove-to-fridge 1.5–3 m**; **perimeter sum of all three legs should stay within 4–8 m**; **enclosed triangle area 5–7 m².** Purpose: minimise unnecessary walking during cooking. **Landing-zone recommendation**: **40–60 cm** of clear countertop beside the stove/oven/fridge for setting down hot pans/dishes/ingredients — even this modest amount meaningfully improves both function and safety.
- **⚠️ Waterfall countertops — an honest tradeoff, not flagged as a hard mistake**: visually striking, reads as monolithic, works well in **large kitchens**; in **small kitchens it can overwhelm and visually shrink the space.** Costs more material (granite/quartz/composite continuing down both sides) and requires precise, skilled installation (mismatched seams destroy the monolithic effect). The waterfall panels **block open shelving or seating at the island end**, reducing that end's functionality. Higher-maintenance stone (periodic sealing/resealing) vs. a lower-maintenance porcelain/ceramic alternative that mimics the look at lower ongoing cost.
