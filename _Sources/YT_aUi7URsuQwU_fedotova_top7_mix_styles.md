## Design Concept — Style-Mixing-Within-a-Space Rules (tangential value only)

- **Five style groups, a named taxonomy**: (1) Classical (all styles predating the early 20th century — Empire, Baroque, ancient Greek/Roman); (2) Modern/Contemporary (all styles from the early 20th century on — Art Deco, minimalism, mid-century); (3) Rustic/Country (Italian rustico, English rustic, farmhouse, Russian style — identified by a shared trait: rough/coarse furniture and fabrics, regardless of country of origin); (4) Industrial (loft, techno, grunge — rough materials, exposed metal, rivets); (5) Ethnic (country-of-origin-specific styles: Japanese, Chinese, African, Moroccan, etc.). Some styles span multiple groups (Russian style = rustic + ethnic; Scandinavian = ethnic + rustic + modern, per the speaker's own classification).
- **Compatibility rules between groups** (a stated matrix, tangential to this project's actual topic but potentially useful reference if a future source needs it): freely mix *within* any single group except the ethnic group (don't mix two different ethnic traditions, e.g. Chinese + Moroccan, without risking a jumbled result); modern and classical groups can each be freely combined with any other group as a base; rustic pairs well with industrial; classical + one ethnic tradition is specifically named as producing "colonial style."
- **Rule 4 — split fixed/architectural elements from furniture/decor between the two styles being mixed, so the secondary style reads clearly**: when adding a secondary style's "notes" to a primary style's base, assign large/structural elements (built-in millwork, partitions, or conversely large furniture pieces) to one style and decor/textile/lighting to the other — a single accent pillow in an otherwise pure-minimalist room is explicitly called out as *not enough* for the secondary style to actually register; enough large-scale elements (furniture, not just decor) must carry the secondary style for the mix to be perceptible.
