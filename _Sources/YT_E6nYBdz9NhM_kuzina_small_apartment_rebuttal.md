## Design Concept — the received advice she is rebutting

- **⚠️ She opens by listing the standard set and then attacking it.** The tips she enumerates: simplify the style (scandi or minimalism, never classic); light or white walls, everything light; the same flooring throughout; use mirrors; no bright objects and no contrasts at all; small or at least lightweight furniture, scaled to the space; furniture on high legs or wall-hung so more floor shows; transparent furniture "because it's as if it isn't there"; no large pendants, just downlights; built-in furniture painted the wall colour so it merges; and simple curtains that must not pool on the floor.
- **⚠️ Her method is visible in the last one, and it is why this source is worth having**: "я не поняла, почему именно в маленьком помещении шторы не должны валяться на полу, а в среднем помещении могут." **She tests whether each rule has a mechanism, and discards the ones that do not.**
- **⚠️ Her summary objection**: follow all of them and you get "такая маленькая квартира с кучей зеркал в стиле сканди, без люстры и лучше вообще без штор, из пары прозрачных табуреток" — **interiors "под копирку."** She notes the same rules get applied to normal-sized rooms too, to make them look bigger still.

## Design Concept — "работа с коробкой," her organising idea

- **⚠️ The mechanism, which the received advice never states**: entering a room you are in a box. **The brain judges the area by seeing where the ceiling ends, where the walls are, and where the floor begins and stops.** **To stop the brain measuring, blur or remove the boundaries between those planes.** Everything below follows from this.
- **Paint walls and ceiling one colour.** Floors need not match but should be as low-contrast as possible.
- **⚠️ And the important qualification, against the standard advice: the colour need not be light.** The requirement is a **monochrome palette — and "монохромная палитра не означает чёрно-белое, это просто означает, что цвет должен быть один."** Her example is a blue bedroom where bedspread, headboard and wall land almost tone-on-tone, **with small accents in cushions differing in both lightness and colour.**
- **No accent wall. No mouldings in a colour differing from the wall plane.**
- **Rugs may zone, but must not be strongly contrasting against the floor** — they should more or less merge into it.

## Design Concept — give the eye somewhere to go

- **⚠️ The second strategy: offer the viewer emptiness or perspective to look into.** A window with a good view, or **a large opening to the adjacent room — deliberately not a normal doorway, and not filled with a door.** The rooms stay separated, but **the eye stops hitting a wall right in front of it.** She rates this as potentially transformative for an **8–10 m² bedroom**.
- **⚠️ Two scoped exceptions where it fails, both from consultation experience**: where the kitchen is permanently untidy; **and specifically the room-plus-hallway combination, because a hallway is nearly always cluttered or dirty with boots.** *(Independently restates her own rule from `Rcd9gkPC6CI` that habitable rooms should not see the hallway.)*
- **Break the upper boundary**: a wall that stops short of the ceiling, or **a glazed transom above it**, so the eye reads the ceiling continuing into the next space. Her example does both — a transom letting the ceiling plane be seen into the shower, **and no door or opening at all**, the shower wall simply being the bedroom wall.
- **Mirrors to imitate the effect where there is no adjacent room — and the placement is specific: press the mirror into a corner.** Against the ceiling on a wall it reads like a window; or **into the corner between two walls as a strip of good proportion, not too wide**, which breaks the corner.
- **⚠️ Two honest cautions she attaches, which is what separates this from the standard advice.** **Mirrors complicate spatial orientation and in a small room can cause real discomfort** — you keep half-worrying about walking into one. **The same applies to transparent furniture**: the advice is correct that it dissolves visually and does not clutter, **but you feel you do not quite command the space, and a transparent table is not very convenient to use.**
- **Shorten a wall in length, not only in height** — a small partition mid-room screening the principal zone, e.g. the view of the bed. She used this in a very small flat.

## Design Concept — her sharpest disagreement with the received advice

- **⚠️ The standard rule is that everything should be calm so the eye glides without catching, and the room then reads large. "Я с этим не согласна."**
- **She argues for accent objects and accent colours**, and her analogy carries the argument: **an eye gliding with nothing to catch on is like riding a horse with the reins dropped — the horse goes where it likes, and the rider knows even less where it is going.** **"Глазом и умом зрителя нужно управлять."**
- **⚠️ The mechanism that makes accents work *for* a small room rather than against it: draw the eye to the centre of the room and concentrate it on something interesting** — a contrasting headboard, or a picture above it — **which distracts from a low ceiling or a small floor.** Pictures work best where wall height is generous.
- **⚠️ And the attenuation that makes it practical: the attention-grabber need not be huge or bright.** It can be proportional to the room, even small. **"Историю можно рассказать и маленьким предметом, главное чтобы на него было интересно смотреть."**

## Furniture / Built-ins — storage and multifunction

- **Niches and open shelves reduce the box effect** — **especially with the back panel in a different colour or material**, which adds depth and organises "a room within a room." **A mirrored back panel also works.** Caution: do not pile everything onto them; utilitarian storage is acceptable but needs to be arranged well and dusted.
- **Vertical space is underused, especially around door openings** — "там много чего может поместиться, если над этим чуть-чуть поработать." Open shelving or cabinets instead of bedside tables. **In bathrooms, store above the door and *on* the door** (hooks for towels and robes).
- **For a small dwelling, always combine furniture with storage** — inside the bed, inside a pouffe, inside any upholstered piece.
- **⚠️ One object should carry several functions**, and her examples are specific: a workspace that closes into a small cabinet, with a fold-down shelf taking a computer; **a shelf that is also a folding table, which extends further still and can even be slept on**; a serving trolley that doubles as a sofa side table and a book/magazine stand; and a chair that is also a clothes valet — **giving a spare chair for guests while doing daily duty for clothes.** Her framing: "попробуйте утрамбовать в неё разные функции, примерно как если бы вы жили на лодке."
- **⚠️ Furniture can break the plane boundaries too, and this is the most sophisticated move in the source**: zone with furniture **detached from both floor and ceiling**, so the eye slides beneath and above it and **one space seems to flow into another across two planes at once.** Requires very stable fixing to floor and ceiling where there is no wall behind.
  - **Or detach from the floor only.** With a wardrobe this costs storage — **upper shelves are less convenient than lower ones, and long clothes become hard to hang** — but it looks stylish and genuinely adds air to a bedroom that previously had a floor-standing wardrobe in the same place.
- **Upholstered furniture: reduce its footprint by omitting armrests** — **or, where space allows, no armrests instead yields extra seating on the same piece.**
