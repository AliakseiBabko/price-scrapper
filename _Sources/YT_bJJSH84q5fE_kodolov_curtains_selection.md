## Design Concept — New Facts

- **⚠️ Heavy multi-layer baroque-style drapery (with large rosettes/
  swags) is explicitly discouraged for an ordinary apartment**, framed
  as visually consuming space rather than expanding it — reserved (per
  the source) for genuinely castle-scale ceiling heights, not a typical
  residential ceiling.
- **Curtain-rod height rule, with reasoning**: mounting the rod
  immediately above the window frame (rather than near the ceiling) is
  intuitive but visually shrinks the room; mounting it high with long
  curtains running to the floor visually raises the ceiling instead.
  **Standard proportion given**: roughly the top third of the wall
  (ceiling-to-rod) to two-thirds (rod-to-window) — flagged as a general
  default, with real exceptions requiring a designer's specific judgment.
- **Curtain-rod width/overhang rule, with reasoning**: a rod cut exactly
  to the window's own width "eats" perceived room width, the horizontal
  counterpart to the height mistake above. **Minimum 15-30cm overhang
  past each side of the window opening, ideally 30-40cm** — makes a
  narrow window read as much wider once curtains are drawn to the side.
- **Short (above-floor-length) curtains flagged as a dated, "outgrown-
  shirt" look** — the one explicitly endorsed exception: a kitchen
  window directly above a sink, where the source recommends blinds
  (french/roman/roller — "there are plenty of options") over any curtain
  style specifically for that location, rather than a short curtain.
- **Floor-length/pooling curtains — a real, non-obvious durability/
  maintenance tradeoff, not just an aesthetic choice**: fabric pooling
  on the floor is vulnerable to children, pets, and robot vacuums
  tearing/snagging it, and can be torn even by an adult accidentally
  stepping on it (worse if the curtain hangs from a full ring track —
  either the ring or the fabric itself can tear). Pooling fabric also
  needs washing more often, and heavier pooled fabric is harder to
  launder.
- **⚠️ Large-print curtain fabric is a common, avoidable mistake**:
  curtains are almost always gathered/pleated in normal use, so a large
  pattern is rarely actually visible once hung — recommends small
  prints for curtain fabric specifically, and directs anyone wanting a
  large visible pattern toward roller blinds instead (a flat surface
  that actually displays a large print).
- **⚠️ Radiator/windowsill clearance must be planned before ordering
  curtains, not after** — a real, easy-to-hit sequencing mistake: if a
  radiator or windowsill is oversized relative to the original plan, a
  floor-length curtain can end up resting on top of it, looking
  visually wrong. Fix: extend the curtain rod's projection 2-3cm further
  into the room. Cited as one more concrete reason radiators/
  windowsills need to be specified early in the design project, since
  by the time curtains go up, everything else is already purchased/
  installed and a mismatch is very costly to correct.
- **⚠️ In-floor convector clearance/safety rule, tied to convector
  type**: with a floor-to-ceiling window and an in-floor convector, the
  best placement is with the convector **between the curtain and the
  window** (creates a warm-air curtain effect). If the convector is
  water-based, curtain fabric resting on it is fine. **If it's
  electric, the source states he personally has never seen a real
  ignition case but would still not risk letting curtain fabric
  (especially a fuzzy/textured weave) rest directly on an electric
  heating element.**
- **⚠️ Sheer/blackout-layering "stereotype" explicitly debunked**: an
  older convention held that a window needed a full three-layer stack
  (sheer, semi-sheer, blackout); the source states plainly this is no
  longer necessary — sheer-only, semi-sheer-only, or blackout-only
  installations all work fine independently. If you never use one
  layer type, skip it entirely rather than installing it by convention.
- **Fullness/gather coefficient, a specific number range**: curtain
  fabric width should be **1.5-2× the rod's own width** to produce a
  visibly wavy, gathered look rather than flat drape. 1.5× gives smaller
  waves; 2×+ gives a noticeably fuller, more voluminous "premium" look
  (the source's own car-door-sound analogy: a cheap tinny door vs. a
  substantial one). Framed as a rule of thumb, not an absolute.
