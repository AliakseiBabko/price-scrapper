## Rules / Heuristics — 7 Fundamental Principles for Choosing Curtains

1. **Natural light level drives the whole decision, and layering
   ("многослойность") is the primary lever for controlling it**: room
   orientation (south-facing brighter than north-facing), floor level,
   and window size all change how a room is lit. Harsh direct sunlight
   glares off glossy surfaces and casts hard shadows — described as an
   unrelaxing effect; soft diffused light is more comfortable to occupy.
   **Layering technique**: a light sheer layer (first layer) plus a
   heavier drape layer (second layer), or a light-diffusing roller blind
   plus heavy drapes, with an optional third layer for fine-grained
   control of the "light scenario" by weather or mood — layers can be
   opened/closed independently.
2. **⚠️ Soft diffused light through a sheer curtain has a stated
   cosmetic/aesthetic side effect**: it removes harsh facial shadows,
   making a person look younger — compared explicitly to a soft-focus
   photo filter. A north-facing room with naturally diffused light gets
   this benefit automatically, without any curtain choice needed.
   `single-account`, presented as the designer's own professional
   observation. [source: [[_Sources/YT_ze87vOi2qrQ_khikhol_7_curtain_principles|ze87vOi2qrQ]]]
3. **For a poorly-lit room (e.g. ground floor), removing curtains
   entirely to maximize light doesn't meaningfully brighten the room but
   does make it feel less finished/cozy** — the recommended fix is 1-2
   light layers of a very high-light-transmission fabric. **Organza is
   recommended over voile/tulle specifically because it's woven from
   finer threads, allowing more light through.** Alternatives: skip
   sheers and use only drapes that just frame the window reveal edges
   (accepting a visible radiator, if that's not a concern), or use a
   sheer-fabric Roman blind ("римская штора") to dress the window without
   blocking light.
4. **Reflected light from the building's surroundings affects the
   room's perceived warmth/coolness, and curtain color should account
   for it**: the color of nearby exterior surfaces (a gray building, a
   neighbor's red roof, foliage, a lawn) reflects into the room. Example
   given: warm-yellow reflected light from a nearby yellow building can
   make a north-facing (naturally cool-toned) room feel warmer; green
   lawn reflection can cool a south-facing room. Curtain color can be
   chosen to reinforce or offset this reflected tint.
5. **Curtains can correct window/room proportions and geometry**: widen
   the visual footprint of a narrow window; visually re-center an
   off-center window; unify two smaller windows into one larger visual
   block by dressing them together; restore visual ceiling height in a
   room with a low window via vertical curtain lines (and
   vertical-patterned fabric reinforces the same height illusion) — the
   analogy given is an elegantly, tall-reading dressed silhouette.
6. **Curtain and wall color combination can adjust a long/narrow room's
   perceived proportions**: a warm, intense color combination on the far
   wall/curtains visually pulls that wall closer, making a narrow room
   read as less elongated; a soft, tonal (low-contrast) color combination
   between curtains and walls expands perceived space, versus a
   high-contrast combination which does not. **Heavy-textured fabrics**
   (rough weave/"рогожка," bouclé, faux suede, faux fur) add visual depth
   but overuse makes a room feel cluttered; for a spacious feel, prefer
   light, unadorned fabrics — a single accent element can still be
   included for visual interest without breaking the airy effect.
7. **Room-specific architectural quirks (niches, protrusions, unusual
   window shape) or genuine flaws (unconcealed heating pipes, unevenly
   painted walls) should each be assessed on their own terms** — emphasize
   genuine positive features, and use curtain design creatively to
   disguise genuine flaws, rather than treating either as fixed.
8. **In open-plan spaces or rooms with visually connected sightlines
   (open doorways), curtains across different windows should read as a
   unified whole even when each window needs different treatment** —
   achieved via one shared element (the same model in different fabric,
   different models from the same/coordinating fabric family, or shared
   trim/finishing details), not by forcing an identical treatment on
   every window.
9. **⚠️ Curtains are a functional material category, not just decorative
   — three named functional-fabric use cases**: acoustic fabrics for
   noise reduction (loud neighbors/street noise); flame-resistant/
   non-combustible fabrics near a kitchen stovetop, as a fire-safety
   measure; heat- and light-reflective sheer fabrics for panoramic-window
   rooms prone to overheating. Framed explicitly as real, current-market
   material options, not a hypothetical.
10. **⚠️ Curtain color as a genuine visual-illusion tool**: color choice
    can make a small room read larger or a large room read cozier, and
    affects mood via color/texture. **A notable specific technique
    offered for resolving color-choice indecision: a physiological
    "lemon test."** Place a drop of lemon juice on the tongue — a strong,
    fast salivation response (mouth doesn't stay dry) suggests lower
    baseline internal stimulation, associated (per this source) with
    extroversion and a preference for bold, saturated colors and
    pronounced patterns/textures; a weak response (mouth stays dry)
    suggests higher baseline internal stimulation, associated with
    introversion and a preference for muted colors and subtler textures/
    patterns. **Presented as the source's own claimed
    psychological-typing method, not independently validated here** —
    recorded as the source's stated technique, `unverified` as an actual
    predictive test, but a genuinely specific, checkable, reusable
    decision-making tool worth recording regardless of the underlying
    psychological claim's validity.
