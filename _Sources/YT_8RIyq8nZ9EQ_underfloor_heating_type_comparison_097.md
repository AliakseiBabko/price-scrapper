## HVAC / Flooring — Underfloor Heating Type Comparison

- **Three types, correct naming clarified**: "water" (jidkostnoy/liquid, since antifreeze not just water is often used) — hydronic, tubing embedded in screed; "electric" — direct-heating cable/mat embedded in screed or self-leveling compound; "film"/"infrared" — thin resistive film, wave-heating, laid under a floating floor covering. The two "electric" types have fundamentally different heating mechanisms (direct resistive heating of the element itself vs. infrared wave heating of what's above it) and shouldn't be conflated despite both being electric.
- **Headline ranking, then justified parameter-by-parameter**: water is best on nearly every parameter but almost never usable in an apartment (see legal-status below); film is second and should be used wherever legally/technically possible; electric fills the remaining gaps where neither water nor film works (under tile, in wet rooms).
- **Installation complexity, explicit ranking**: film easiest, electric second, water hardest — but the source argues the modern-tooling "hard to install" reputation for water systems is now a myth spread by plumbers seeking a fee premium; a modern push-fit fitting system requires only a rentable crimping tool (~1,000 RUB/day, one day is enough for a typical job) and eliminates the join-quality risk that used to require soldering/welding skill. As of 2018-2019 (source's own dating), installation complexity across all three types is roughly comparable.
- **Durability vs. reliability — two distinct, often-conflated metrics**: durability = rated service life in years; reliability = the percentage of installed units that actually survive to end of rated life. **Water: 50-year rated life, ~100% reliability. Electric: 15-year rated life, ~40% reliability. Film: 15-year rated life, ~70% reliability.** `single-account`, no methodology given for the reliability percentages — treat as the source's own field-experience estimate, not an independently verified statistic.
- **Repairability of heating elements (explicitly scoped to the elements themselves, not everything above them)**: film is the most repairable — remove baseboard, lift finish flooring, replace the element section, relay — because it sits directly under a removable floating floor. Electric (embedded in screed/self-leveling compound) has essentially zero repairability — repair requires breaking out the embedded base layer entirely.
- **Heat-up/cool-down time, a genuine trade-off, not simply "faster is better"**: water has the slowest heat-up (must warm the full screed mass) but also the slowest cool-down — best suited to being left running continuously, since maintaining temperature afterward needs only a small trickle of heat (train analogy: hard to accelerate, easy to maintain speed). Electric is intermediate. Film has the fastest heat-up (full heat in ~5 minutes over a thin covering like linoleum) but also the fastest cool-down — best suited to intermittent/on-demand use, explicit worked example: a loggia that doesn't need constant heat but should warm quickly when someone steps out to use it briefly.
- **Running-cost ranking**: water cheapest (even with a forced-circulation pump), film second, electric most expensive.
- **Finish-flooring compatibility, by type**: **water and electric are compatible with any covering** (tile, laminate, PVC tile, linoleum, carpet) since they're embedded in screed, which any covering can be laid over. **Film has serious restrictions**: incompatible with cork (low thermal conductivity defeats the point) and pointless under any covering that doesn't benefit from underfloor heat; **categorically incompatible with ceramic tile** — the film layer prevents tile adhesive from bonding to the base, and even perforated film variants don't have enough perforation area to fix this in practice, tile still detaches; **categorically incompatible with premium laminate containing metal foil backing layers** — heating a metal-backed laminate on film is functionally equivalent to putting metal in a microwave. `confirmed`, explicit mechanism for both film incompatibilities.
- **Wet-room compatibility**: water and electric have no restrictions (bathroom, WC, shower all fine). **Film is categorically prohibited in any wet room**, both for performance and safety reasons — the electrical connection to the film's heating elements sits exposed, protected only by bitumen patches, and the slightest water ingress produces a guaranteed current leak/shock risk.
- **Furniture compatibility**: general rule for all three types — never place furniture directly over active underfloor heating (efficiency loss/wasted energy for water/electric; specifically prohibited over film, which heats via directed radiation upward and could overheat/damage furniture sitting in its direct radiation path, unlike water/electric which don't get hot enough to damage furniture, just waste energy).
- **Legal status in an apartment, the single most consequential parameter**: electric and film can be used anywhere; **water/hydronic underfloor heating is illegal in a standard apartment for two independent reasons — it cannot be connected to a central-heating system, and it cannot be installed above another unit's living space** (both apply regardless of one-pipe or two-pipe central heating scheme). Only viable in the rare case of an individually-metered/individually-heated ground-floor unit (source's own estimate: under 1% of cases). This single constraint is why water, despite ranking best on nearly every other parameter, is described as "almost never usable in an apartment."
- **Noise**: electric and film are silent. Water should also be silent in normal operation — audible noise indicates trapped air that needs bleeding — but a forced-circulation water system's pump is never fully silent regardless of manufacturer claims; the pump should be positioned as far as practical from occupied space while still keeping it serviceable.
