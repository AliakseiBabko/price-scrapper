## Demolition / Flooring — Durable Facts & Rules

1. **⚠️ Real screed-height-mismatch case after a wall removal, with the
   full decision tree for fixing it**: after removing the dividing wall,
   a laser level found the two now-merged rooms' screeds were **4cm**
   apart in height. Options considered and each one's specific rejection
   reason:
   - **A small transition ramp/slope between the two levels** — rejected:
     with parquet board flooring planned, the board's joints could
     separate over time at the ramp, or the board itself could crack at
     the bend.
   - **Leveling with self-leveling compound or tile adhesive** — the
     source states this only works for a height difference **up to
     roughly 1cm**; not viable at 4cm.
   - **Pour the whole apartment's screed up to the higher room's existing
     level** — technically workable but rejected for two reasons: it would
     reduce ceiling height by ~5cm, and the source states he's **not a fan
     of "double screeds"** (pouring new screed directly over old) because
     the underlying old screed can delaminate/separate over time even if
     the new pour itself is sound.
   - **Chosen solution**: fully remove the higher room's screed down to
     the bare structural slab, then pour a new screed there set to match
     the *other* room's (lower) existing screed level.
2. **Semi-dry screed chosen specifically for its low-shrinkage property in
   this remediation context**: the source explicitly prefers **semi-dry
   screed** over standard wet cement-sand screed poured to leveling pins
   ("маяки") because semi-dry screed shrinks "practically not at all" by
   comparison — meaning the parquet board can go down directly on it with
   no additional self-leveling-compound pass first. He notes standard
   wet-screed jobs often *do* need a further self-leveling pass before
   parquet, which he wanted to avoid entirely on this job (one screed pour,
   not two).
3. **⚠️ Tool-selection rule for screed demolition, with a real rental
   price**: demolishing a 5-6cm-thick screed requires a genuinely powerful
   jackhammer/breaker (he uses a **Makita** unit) — "an ordinary small
   household breaker simply won't remove screed this thick." **Real rental
   price stated directly in USD: ~$12/day** — no currency-conversion
   question, source states the figure in dollars itself. He notes rental is
   cheap enough that maintenance/upkeep of the tool (handled by the rental
   company, not the renter) isn't a cost he has to absorb, unlike owning
   the tool outright.
4. **⚠️ Panel-house-specific demolition caution: screed thickness and
   substrate can vary meaningfully room-to-room in the same apartment, and
   this affects demolition safety**: in the room being worked, the screed
   sits on foam ("пенопласт") over the structural slab — removing screed
   there safely stops at the foam layer. But in the adjoining hallway, the
   screed was found to be **only ~1.5cm thick**, with a **rebar lifting
   loop ("монтажная петля")** protruding *higher* than the slab surface in
   that specific spot — a real, concrete example of an irregularity a
   crew could hit unexpectedly. **Explicit rule: never break into the
   structural slab itself with the jackhammer** — only remove the screed
   layer above it.
5. **Practical technique for distinguishing screed mortar from structural
   slab concrete while jackhammering (so you know when to stop)**: the
   slab is real concrete (contains coarse aggregate — limestone or granite
   crushed stone), while the screed is a plain cement-sand mortar with no
   coarse aggregate — the slab is "much stronger" and the jackhammer
   operator feels the difference in resistance immediately on contact,
   serving as a practical, in-hand signal to stop before damaging the
   slab.
6. **Original under-screed foam insulation judged inadequate on
   inspection, informing the new build-up choice**: once opened up, the
   existing foam under the screed was found to be ordinary
   (non-extruded) foam — "loose" and "soft," not extruded polystyrene —
   and judged unsuitable to reuse. Since the new screed level will end up
   ~3cm lower than the original (to match the neighboring room), a thick
   foam layer isn't practical anyway; the plan instead is a thin
   soundproofing/heat-insulation layer — either the same "евроблок"
   foamed-polyethylene sheet already used on this project's ceiling, or a
   foamed-polyethylene roll at least 1cm thick — accepting reduced
   insulation thickness in exchange for a thinner overall build-up, with
   the parquet board itself contributing some additional thermal
   insulation on top.
7. **Laser-level datum-and-triangle-mark technique, a repeatable
   measurement convention**: set one single, consistent laser-level
   reference height ("отметка") across every room, then mark it on the
   wall with a **triangle** symbol at each point (a habit from his years
   as a site foreman marking formwork/slab pour heights for carpenters).
   From each triangle mark, measure straight down to the actual screed (or
   up to the ceiling) to know the exact level at that point — the payoff:
   the laser tool itself can be packed away and taken to another job, but
   the triangle marks stay on the wall permanently, so the room's levels
   can be re-verified at any later time with just a tape measure. A
   2-meter aluminum straightedge ("правило") is used alongside the laser
   to visually/physically confirm a height differential by eye and feel.
