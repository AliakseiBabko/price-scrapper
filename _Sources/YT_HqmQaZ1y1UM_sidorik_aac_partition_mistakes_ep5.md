## Walls / Ceilings — AAC/Foam-Concrete Partition Technique and Common Mistakes

- **AAC/foam-concrete block properties and dimensions**: 625mm(L) × 250mm(H) × 100mm(W), **~10kg/block** (roughly half the weight of episode 4's silicate block at the same 100mm width) — described as the single most common material for interior partitions specifically because it's light and easy to cut, but that same low density (~50% air by volume) makes it soft and prone to installation-error-driven cracking, which the speaker attributes to technique mistakes rather than a fundamental material flaw. Retail buying note: manufacturers/stores disclose up to 10% acceptable breakage in a pallet, expected to be used for cut pieces rather than treated as a defect claim.
- **Screed must be chased (cut back) before a non-load-bearing partition is built on top of it, in a free-plan/open apartment** — cut a groove roughly 1.5× the block's width so the block sits directly on the structural base slab, not on the screed itself. **Two stated reasons**: (1) screed typically sits on a soft underlayment (foamed polyethylene or expanded polystyrene) that can compress slightly (~0.5cm) under a partition's weight, producing cracks over time; (2) under a floating-screed acoustic design, the screed is meant to have zero rigid contact with any wall/partition, since a rigid contact point transmits impact sound directly through it — a partition resting on screed defeats that design. (Not applicable to the speaker's own current partition, since he'd already fully demolished the screed in episode 3.)
- **Adhesive choice, generalizable across three block types**: "Holcim Expert" glue works for foam concrete, aerated concrete, and silicate block alike — a named, working, multi-material adhesive recommendation. Applied with a trowel plus a 6mm notched spreader.
- **Cutting tool for this specific material**: a reciprocating/sabre saw with a carbide-tipped blade — described as cutting through this soft, low-density material easily ("like butter"), a different tool recommendation from episode 4's angle-grinder-only rule for the denser silicate block (that page's cutting guidance is material-specific, not universal).
- **An alternative, more-precise base-leveling method than episode 4's mortar-first-course technique**: cut screed-leveling "beacons" (маяки) into 6mm × 15cm strips, bed them on cement plaster (Holcim) along the laser-level line at the wall's future footprint, then pour a thin sand-cement screed (Holcim M300) over the beacon-leveled strip to create a small, precisely level "foundation" for the partition — described as slower but noticeably more accurate than eyeballing a mortar bed, and not requiring the mortar-laying experience the first-course method does.
- **A simple DIY glue-spreading jig** (plywood + wood strips + a 6mm notched trowel mounted to slide along a block face) speeds up adhesive application at volume — explicitly framed as most worthwhile at house-building scale, shown here mainly for demonstration on a small job.
- **Mistake: using masonry mortar instead of block adhesive** — mortar's joint is too thick for a relatively flat, factory-dimensioned block (mortar belongs to brick, whose own irregularity needs a thick, forgiving joint); shown with a real example of a mortar-laid block wall as a negative example.
- **Classic block bonding pattern (перевязка)**: offset each course's vertical joints by half a block width from the course below/the perpendicular wall's own coursing — cited common mistake is an inconsistent or non-half-block offset that fails to properly interlock the two intersecting walls.
- **Mistake and cause of cracking: a rigid connection between an AAC partition and the load-bearing wall it abuts.** Correct technique: tie in with a **soft, flexible wall hanger (мягкий подвес)**, not a rigid metal angle bracket, every third course, with the joint itself filled with foam (not glue) — an explicit, material-specific contrast with episode 4's rigid metal-angle-bracket technique for the denser, heavier silicate block. **This is a genuine, worth-flagging distinction, not a contradiction**: the softer/more crack-prone AAC block needs a flexible tie to avoid concentrated stress, while the denser silicate block in ep.4 can take a rigid tie.
- **No primer is needed before gluing AAC blocks** — the material's own high porosity/absorbency means simply wetting it with water beforehand is sufficient.
- **Mistake: compensating for an uneven/proud block edge ("зуб," a protruding "tooth") by applying a thicker glue layer** — glue isn't designed as a gap-filler and this is a common beginner error. **Correct fix**: plane or sand the protruding edge down. A dedicated block plane exists but is described as ineffective and unpleasantly loud on higher-density block; **the cheapest, simplest fix is coarse (grit 30) sandpaper mounted to a wood block** — real price stated directly in USD: **≈$1 for a 0.5m strip** of this sandpaper (no conversion needed, already USD).
- **Level-checking rule while laying blocks**: check plumb/level from **only one consistent side** of each block, since blocks vary in flatness by 1-3mm (occasionally up to 5mm) even when sold as "first-grade"/flat — checking both faces simultaneously is called out as a real practitioner mistake ("your brain will boil"); push all irregularity to one reference side, since the opposite (uneven) face gets straightened out anyway by the ~1-1.5cm plaster coat applied later.
- **Laser-level and string-line (шнурка) layout techniques for block-by-block alignment**: position a laser a couple millimeters off the block face (not touching it) and hold a trowel/spatula against each laid block to see whether the beam is broken/deflected, indicating a misaligned block — the laser can stay in position for an entire wall's construction up to ceiling height, avoiding the need to re-string a physical line repeatedly. The classic string-line alternative: set and level the two end blocks first, then stretch a string a few millimeters off (not touching) the block face using a screw as a spacer at each end, and align every intermediate block to that consistent offset gap.
- **Mistake: leaving too large a gap (e.g. ~5cm) at the top course near the ceiling and filling it with foam alone rather than cutting a properly-sized block piece** — the resulting foam-filled void has nothing solid to screw into later, causing problems mounting a drywall-profile track or anything else attached during finish work. **Correct approach**: either fill solidly with mortar or actually cut blocks to the exact remaining height.
- **Standard ceiling-gap rule, with the underlying mechanism explained**: leave 1-1.5cm between the top block course and the ceiling, filled with foam (never glue) — foam gives "soft rigidity," meaning the partition stays firmly positioned but isn't damaged if the structural base ever shifts slightly; foam is also just physically easier to work into a gap this narrow than glue. Wet the joint before foaming, foam from both sides, trim flush once cured. (Corroborates episode 4's identical rule for silicate block, with a clearer stated mechanism.)

## Materials — Layout/Marking Technique

- **Use removable colored tape (not pencil/marker) to lay out future partition and furniture footprints directly on the floor in a free-plan apartment** — lets you preview room/door-opening placement in real 3D space (a paper floor plan often reads differently once seen at real scale) and can be re-positioned freely with no residue or visual clutter, unlike marker lines on a screed which accumulate into a confusing mess after corrections. Same "Blue Dolphin" PVC tape brand as episode 2's window-masking technique (bright color, easy to see).
- **Layout-precision emphasis**: measure and re-check partition placement extensively relative to *existing* walls before committing — the speaker spent roughly half a day on layout alone for this wall, explicitly recommending a 90°-angle laser level plus a rangefinder for anyone without an experienced mason's practiced eye.

## Cost Drivers / Material Prices — Door-Lintel DIY vs. Factory Cost

- **Factory-made block-lintel unit (a single 1.2m-long block sold specifically to span a door opening) costs 30 BYN** — explicitly named as expensive. **DIY alternative**: glue 2-2.5 standard AAC blocks together with foam adhesive (built flat on the partition's own already-level top surface, not the uneven floor, curing ~10 minutes before lifting into place) — at a standard block price of **2 BYN retail / 1 BYN wholesale**, this DIY lintel costs roughly **7-8× less** than the factory unit. USD-equivalents (trailing-6-month BYN/USD average before 2020-10-10, 2.4734 BYN/USD): factory lintel ≈**$10** (bucket-rounded from $12.13), block retail ≈$0.81, block wholesale ≈$0.40 (sub-dollar figures kept exact, bucket-rounding not meaningful at that scale). Region: Belarus level 1 (currency named directly). Year: 2020.
- **Reinforce a door opening on both sides even though optional** — cheap insurance, doesn't add meaningful time.
- **Real self-reported layout mistake**: the speaker cut a door opening at 80cm instead of the intended 90cm and had to recut an extra 10cm with a sabre saw — a concrete cautionary example for the "measure many times" rule above. Opening height for this project: 208cm (2.08m), accounting for the finished-floor screed still to be poured.
