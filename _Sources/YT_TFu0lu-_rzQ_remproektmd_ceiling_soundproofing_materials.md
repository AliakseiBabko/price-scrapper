1. **Explicit DIY-vs-professional soundproofing framing, a useful buying
   context not yet phrased this way on this page**: professional
   soundproofing companies calculate membrane layer counts and build
   complex multi-layer "pies" — genuinely effective, but priced high. A
   DIY approach using simpler, cheaper materials won't match professional
   effectiveness but can still meaningfully reduce (not eliminate) noise
   at low cost — framed as a legitimate middle option, not just "do it
   properly or don't bother."
2. **Developer cost-cutting context repeated for ceiling/floor
   soundproofing specifically**: developers commonly cut costs on slab
   thickness and on the sound-insulating/heat-insulating layers embedded
   in the screed, in both old-fund panel housing *and* new-builds alike —
   poor baseline soundproofing isn't unique to old buildings.
3. **⚠️ Mineral wool deliberately excluded from this ceiling-specific test,
   with a stated health-safety mechanism**: mineral wool is called an
   excellent sound insulator in general, but is unsuitable specifically
   for **soundproofing left exposed above a stretch ceiling** (not boxed
   in behind drywall) — airflow in the void above the stretch-ceiling
   membrane can carry loose glass-wool fibers into the air over time,
   which is a health hazard. This is a genuinely new, mechanism-based
   caveat not yet on this page's existing System Type Taxonomy — narrower
   than a blanket anti-mineral-wool rule: it applies to the
   exposed-above-a-stretch-ceiling use case specifically, not to
   mineral wool generally (e.g. inside a framed/drywall-finished
   assembly, which this source doesn't test or comment on here).
4. **⚠️ Three-material decibel-reduction test, real measured results
   (phone+speaker+decibel-meter app, ~30-second sample each, informal
   methodology — not a lab test)**: baseline (speaker on open table, no
   enclosure) = **61 dB**. Enclosed in a small box built from each
   material: **extruded polystyrene/XPS ("пеноплекс")** → 52 dB (**-9
   dB**); **foamed polyethylene, 2cm, two-layer construction ("Пенофом
   Евроблок," Russian-made)** → 47 dB (**-14 dB, best performer**); **2cm
   natural cork agglomerate** → 57 dB (**-5 dB, worst performer of the
   three)**. **Explicit finding: the cheapest-tested materials
   outperformed the most expensive one in this test** — cork, despite
   costing the most, gave the smallest measured reduction.
5. **⚠️ Real price data, stated directly in USD (no conversion needed)**:
   **cork agglomerate ~$20/m²** at 2cm thickness — the most expensive of
   the three tested. **XPS and foamed-polyethylene (Eurobloc), each
   ~$5/m²** — roughly 4× cheaper than cork at comparable thickness. Given
   the decibel results above, the source explicitly questions whether
   cork's extra cost is justified for this specific ceiling-under-stretch-
   ceiling use case.
6. **Practitioner's own stated conclusion and choice for this project's
   ceiling**: all three materials "worked reasonably well," but foamed
   polyethylene (Eurobloc) tested best and is the cheapest of the three —
   the practitioner states he will use it for this apartment's ceiling
   soundproofing. Cork is separately noted as already used on multiple of
   the company's past projects with good practical results (not

   experiment measures relative performance among cheap DIY options
