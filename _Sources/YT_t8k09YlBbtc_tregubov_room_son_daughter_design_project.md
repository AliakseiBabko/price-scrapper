## Kids Room — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Reject an "over-vertical," fully-packed layout even when it
  technically fits everything** — the designer's own first concept (a
  loft storage unit over the entry, cribs/beds packed along every wall)
  was explicitly discarded for **leaving "no air" in the room** despite
  solving the storage/sleeping-space problem on paper; a room that reads
  as visually and spatially oppressive is a real design failure even if
  every functional requirement is technically met.
- **A low ceiling (here, 2.6m) can rule out a genuine stacked bunk bed**
  — the designer explicitly could not fit a standard two-tier bunk at
  this height and instead used an **asymmetric layout**: one child's bed
  elevated on a single-level loft structure, the other child's bed kept
  low/floor-level, rather than both children stacked directly above one
  another.
- **Deliberately leave open (unenclosed) floor-to-loft-bed clearance**
  rather than filling it with storage — improves the room's felt
  spaciousness ("air") even though it sacrifices some potential storage
  volume; a genuine trade-off the designer chose in favor of openness.
- **Corner wardrobes are explicitly discouraged for a room shared by two
  children** — the designer calls a corner-wardrobe cavity "a graveyard
  for forgotten items": each of the two resulting triangular sections is
  only ~50cm usable width, workable for one child's storage but
  insufficient once split between two children. Stated plainly: "avoid
  this kind of storage system — it steals both your things and your
  space."
- **A bed placed directly against a cold exterior wall is genuinely
  uncomfortable to sleep against** — mitigation offered even without a
  full redesign: add a railing/backrest and pillows to the wall-adjacent
  bed edge, converting the sleeping edge into something closer to a sofa
  feel, improving comfort perception without structural change.
- **A glass-paned (even frosted) interior door into a kids' room is
  discouraged** — seeing movement/shadows pass by outside a translucent
  door while inside the room was flagged as a real, if subtle, source of
  low-level stress/anxiety for the room's occupant; recommendation is an
  opaque, painted door instead.
- **A sliding-door system planned specifically for an age-based privacy
  transition**: for a mixed-age sibling pair, plan a sliding partition/
  door the older child can close once they reach school age (to avoid
  disturbing a younger sibling, or vice versa for personal space) without
  needing a permanent wall — a distinct, age-triggered application of
  the general partition-methods content already recorded elsewhere in
  this project's store.
- **Under-bed/storage-orientation conversion technique**: an existing
  horizontal under-bed storage area was replaced with a **vertical,
  lift-lid storage bench** (for off-season bedding) — explicitly framed
  by the designer as "not lost space, just a change in storage
  orientation from horizontal to vertical," a reusable reframe for
  clients worried about losing storage capacity during a redesign.
- **A young child's elevated-bed staircase with integrated pull-out
  drawers for her own toys**, sized so she can quickly self-tidy before
  an older sibling complains about clutter — a concrete, child-scaled
  variant of the general "give each child their own accessible storage"
  principle already recorded from this batch's other sources.
- **Deep triple-door cabinet built into the loft-bed base doubles as
  both storage and a hide-and-seek play spot** for the younger child —
  a dual-purpose furniture technique combining storage with a genuinely
  enjoyed play function rather than storage alone.

## Design Concept — Durable Facts

- **Gender-neutral palette technique for a mixed-sex shared room**: a
  neutral grey base with a nautical/"sailor" motif was chosen
  specifically because it reads as neither overtly girly nor overtly
  boyish, giving both children a sense of representation in the same
  shared aesthetic rather than splitting the room into two visibly
  gendered halves.
- **Deliberate "openness" visual cues for a small room**: rain-themed
  wall mural, clouds, and balloon graphics were chosen specifically to
  give the brain extra "open sky/outdoor space" cues, intended to read
  as more spacious than the room's actual small footprint — an explicit
  perceptual-trick rationale, not purely decorative.
- **Decor chosen to narratively reinforce sibling relationship quality**:
  the designer explicitly asked the client about the siblings' actual
  relationship before finalizing a wall graphic depicting an older
  sibling figure caring for a younger one — a deliberate design choice
  to reflect and reinforce the family's real relationship dynamic through
  decor, not a generic motif picked without that context.
