## Mistakes / Warnings — Full-Apartment QC Walkthrough

- **Front door swinging inward wastes entry-hall floor space and traps the doormat/floor items behind it on every open** — the source states there's no structural reason large, heavy metal entry doors default to opening inward; an outward-opening door (where framing/regulations allow) avoids this entirely.
- **Drywall (GKL) ceilings, as a designer/developer upsell over stretch ceiling, carry three concrete downsides**: (1) significantly more expensive, (2) significantly slower to install, (3) if flooded from above, must be fully removed and replaced (a stretch ceiling can just be drained and reused). Also flagged as a health consideration: drywall + putty + primer + paint together introduce more chemical off-gassing than even the cheapest stretch-ceiling fabric.
- **A bathroom/WC floor should be flush (no threshold) with the rest of the apartment's floor level** — sold as outdated to still use a raised threshold; the source notes this is only legally permitted when the entire apartment has continuous (single-contour) waterproofing, not just the wet room — a real regulatory/technique gate on the flush-floor approach, not just an aesthetic choice.
- **A room's main light switch should be reachable by feel, right next to the door handle** — the described failure: a decorative/accent light switch was placed close to hand, while the main light switch was tucked far away and behind other controls, forcing a person entering a dark room to grope past the wrong switches (an accent-light-only switch) before finding the main one.
- **Moisture-resistant flooring now exists that eliminates the need for a raised transition strip/threshold at a kitchen's water-exposed edge** — described as a "well over a decade"-old flooring category; the standard water-guard threshold is called an obsolete workaround now that the flooring itself can handle splashes.
- **AC unit placement must be checked against every seating position on a nearby sofa** — a kitchen-living-room AC installed in a location that produced a guaranteed direct draft onto occupants seated anywhere along a sofa, regardless of which end; described as a placement-sequencing failure (the AC was added reactively after residents complained about its absence, rather than planned with airflow in mind from the start).
- **A tankless/instant water heater mounted at ceiling height is a real usability defect**, since its own control buttons then require a step-stool ("fifth stepladder") to reach — even though the appliance category itself is endorsed as one of the best renovation solutions for a hot-water outage, its height must keep its own controls reachable.
- **A coat closet ("fast-access changing area") must be sized to actual coat bulk, not just coat count** — a closet that comfortably fits regular jackets snags/protrudes when a bulky item (e.g. a fur coat) is hung, because the closet's depth wasn't planned for the bulkiest expected garment.
- **A large double bed needs an electrical outlet on both sides, not just one** — the defect found had zero outlets on either side of a large double bed, meaning neither occupant could charge a phone or reach a lamp switch from bed, requiring getting up to reach any switch when going to sleep.
- **An in-unit food-waste disposal unit for a short-term-rental (не для проживания, "квартира под аренду") kitchen is poor spend** — the source's reasoning: short-term rental occupants don't cook large volumes, so the unit sits unused; an unused disposal unit dries out internally and develops a persistent odor — a direct case of a fixture choice mismatched to the unit's actual use pattern (rental vs. owner-occupied).
- **A walk-in wardrobe/closet needs at least a small mirror inside it**, even when a bedroom wardrobe is present — flagged as a real usability gap for a room whose entire purpose is trying on/selecting clothes.
- **Recessed/point ceiling lights inside a wardrobe or dressing area must be checked for how they render skin/clothing color** — the defect found: the installed point lights visibly distorted the true appearance of a person trying on clothes inside the wardrobe, undermining the room's own purpose.
- **Built-in (integrated/concealed) plumbing fixtures carry a durable long-term risk beyond upfront cost**: several times more expensive to install initially, and if a mechanism inside the wall fails, the specific model may be discontinued with no replacement parts available — forcing the wall to be broken open and the mechanism swapped for a different (possibly incompatible-looking) unit rather than simply repaired.
- **Structural/load-bearing walls constrain built-in bathtub installation more than buyers expect** — in this case, mounting the built-in tub required thickening a load-bearing wall by 12cm to fit the mechanism, which then made the tub itself noticeably shorter and less comfortable than a standard installation would have been. **General rule: check whether a wall is load-bearing before committing to a built-in fixture that needs in-wall clearance — a load-bearing wall can force a fixture-shrinking compromise that a non-bearing wall wouldn't.**
- **Outlets sized/placed for a smart-speaker's oversized power brick can visually clash with curtain hardware** — a purely aesthetic defect flagged (paired outlets near curtains with a bulky external power supply visible), worth checking outlet placement against both the fixture and its actual plug/adapter bulk, not just the wall-mount footprint.
- **A large mirror should not be placed directly opposite a bed** — the stated mechanism: any movement made while falling asleep reflects in the mirror, creating an unsettling "someone's there" sensation. A behavioral/psychological placement rule, not a structural one.
- **A single switch controlling backlighting + outlets together (all off from one switch on the accessible side) is called out as genuinely good design** — positive example: the switch is on the side nearer the hand, kills accent lighting and nearby outlets together with no extra gesture-based controls needed.
- **Outlet mounting height should be checked against the specific plug/cable being used, not just switch reach** — the defect found: outlets were mounted at a height convenient for the switch above them, but a thick power cable's plug then couldn't physically seat in the outlet because it was crowded by the nearby switch plate.
- **A glass partition placed directly next to a pouf/ottoman used for casual seating creates ongoing safety anxiety**, even when the partition itself is structurally fine — described as a psychological/comfort defect (constant fear of breakage) from proximity, not a technical flaw in the glass itself.
- **Multiple switches at one bedside position, with an unbalanced count on the other side, is flagged as a real usability defect ("discrimination" of the second sleeping position)** — one side had four switches, the other just one, and that single switch on the "poor" side only controlled one fixture rather than acting as a master switch for the room — a mismatch between what a switch's position implies (it looks like a master switch) and what it actually does.
- **An enclosed/glazed-in balcony/loggia that abuts an unheated exterior wall typically needs a raised floor platform (подиум) for extra insulation thickness**, because the required insulation layer is thicker than the loggia's existing floor structure can accommodate flush with the main apartment — described as "practically always" the case for this configuration.
- **When a loggia floor can be built flush (single level) with the adjoining room, the flooring itself should run as one continuous material/contour across the transition, not be joined via a cork/foam transition strip** — described as an inferior workaround ("shameful") compared to a genuinely continuous floor material.
- **A loggia with no underfloor heating and whose light switch is located across the apartment (far from the loggia itself) is called out as a basic planning omission** — the room had only two outlets and no other services, with its switch physically inconvenient to reach from the room it controls.
- **Plank/board flooring should run in the direction of the room's main light source and, ideally, also align with the room's main traffic path** — presented as a simple, checkable orientation rule; the source notes this specific combination (light-direction-aligned, not diagonal or herringbone) is rarely seen done correctly.
- **A windowsill's rear gap against the wall should be near-zero, not a visible several-millimeter gap** — the defect found measured roughly 6mm, attributed to poor installation ("crooked-handed" trades) rather than a design choice, framed as a basic installation-quality checkpoint on window trim.
- **Cutting a single 60×60cm large-format porcelain tile into two unequal strips (e.g. 20cm + 40cm) to fake a "designer" mixed-width tile pattern is a real quality/cost trap** — consequences found: many additional cut edges, misaligned grout lines, chipped corners on the cut pieces — while the client paid roughly 3x more for the installation labor and received a visibly lower-quality result than simply using tile manufactured at the intended width. **General rule: a "designer" tile pattern achieved by field-cutting a single size, rather than sourcing the intended width/format directly, both costs more and looks worse.**
- **An undersized radiator (narrower width, reduced height) in an otherwise generously sized bedroom with its own walk-in closet leaves the room noticeably cold even in summer** — a direct case of radiator sizing not matched to room volume, independent of climate/season.
- **A refrigerator's own door panel and the matching integrated cabinetry panel above it should be planned as a single continuous line, not a freestanding narrow panel** — the defect found: a tall, narrow upper cabinet door section (built separately from the fridge's own panel) sagged over time due to its own long, narrow proportions, producing visibly uneven seams against the fixed fridge door below it. **General rule: an integrated-appliance panel line should avoid a long, narrow, independently-hinged upper section if it can be merged with a wider, better-supported unit instead.**
- **A touch/sensor-activated light fixture that's wired dependent on a separate wall switch is a confirmed anti-pattern** — if the wall switch is off, no amount of interacting with the sensor turns the light on; called out explicitly as "one of the dumbest decisions in a renovation" to make a touch/gesture control's function depend on an upstream switch that isn't obviously linked to it.
