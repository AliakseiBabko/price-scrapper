## Design Concept — the decision method (the core of the source)

**Her first question for any object: *why* do you want to take it?** Then three answers, in ascending order of validity:

1. **⚠️ Emotional attachment** — an inheritance from a grandmother, an armchair from friends, a vase bought on honeymoon, a dish bought on a trip that does not go with that vase. **Her test, which is directly usable: put it away out of sight. If a week later you have not remembered it, discard it.** Her aside — people with a dacha find discarding easier, because the dacha is where everything that once carried an attachment goes to be forgotten.
2. **⚠️ Sunk cost** — "it was expensive." Her live example: a current project where two identical Italian pendants **do not really suit the new flat stylistically, but were a very costly purchase, so the owners want to keep them.** She calls this *"повод для размышлений"* — grounds for thought, not a reason. **Recorded as a named failure mode with a real case attached.**
3. **⚠️ The best argument, and the only one she endorses: it genuinely fits the future interior — including by size.** **Pay particular attention to large and medium-sized objects.** The strongest case of all is an existing object that can be **staged advantageously and become the feature the whole scheme is built around.**

## Design Concept — souvenirs and gifts

- **⚠️ Souvenirs and gifts are frequently visual clutter** that neither adorns nor pleases — **you simply stop noticing what is around you.**
- **⚠️ Her de-anchoring test, the general form of the one above: imagine you are seeing the object for the first time, do not know where it came from, do not know who gave it to you — it was not given to you at all.** Decide abstractly whether you need it.
- **⚠️ The mechanism of the failure, stated as a design principle: many small, disparate objects do not form a rhythm — they form noise.** You believe you will look at them; in practice **you merely resign yourself to their existing where you put them.**
- **The positive cases she allows:**
  - An object that can be **a source of inspiration for the future scheme.**
  - **⚠️ A "conversation piece" (she uses the marketing term «конверсейшн пис»)** — something carrying intrigue. Her example: an African mask with a story behind it, hung over the sofa and happening to work with its upholstery. **"Классный способ сэкономить."**
  - Her worked example of the type: **the Philippe Starck citrus squeezer** — silver, thin legs, like a UFO landed on the worktop. **Everyone agrees it is not a good way to squeeze an orange; it can be used for its purpose but that is not what it is for.** It exists as a trigger for conversation.
  - **⚠️ Many homogeneous objects *do* work where scattered ones do not**: her positive example is a set of five similar unusual pieces used as art, occupying the central position in a living room. **The rule underneath: repetition creates rhythm; variety creates noise.**
- **Her negative examples, given bluntly**: a backsplash made of matchboxes or sweet wrappers — "вроде как крутая идея… но вообще это полная фигня"; a dedicated display of printed holiday photos and beads. **⚠️ Her diagnosis of the second is the transferable part: the problem is less the decor than *where it was given pride of place* — on the most visible wall by the sofa, rather than in a corridor near the entrance.**

## Design Concept — pictures and art (the category she calls a disaster)

- **⚠️ "Это прямо беда на консультациях"** — the most frequent problem she meets. Her flat assertion: what attracts you to the picture you want to bring is very often not the picture.

**Do not bring it when:**
- **It does not suit the style** — a super-contemporary picture in a classical scheme. **⚠️ Possible, but then you must commit to eclecticism from the start**, deliberately. *(Same rule as the tile source `X3emRuW-PbA`: commit to the mixture or do not claim the style.)*
- **It suited the *old* interior and the new one will be different.** You disliked the old scheme, you like the picture, **but they no longer work together — the picture was good in a context you are demolishing.**
- **⚠️ The largest category: pictures that suit nothing, anywhere, ever.** She is unsparing — not even a child's room. And her observation about frequency: **clients disproportionately own exactly this kind, and arrive wanting to build the scheme around it.** Her advice: do not, **and do not store it either** unless you are selling the flat.
- **⚠️ Series of canvases meant to hang in a row forming one image** — generally poor work, and worse when they are supposed to hang at staggered heights or in zigzag or longitudinal compositions. **"Лучше вообще без этого."**

**Do bring, or buy:**
- **Abstract work that does not strongly affect the style.**
- **⚠️ Large work — at least a metre on one side, preferably more.**
- **⚠️ Work in a good, expensive frame («багет»).** Her reasoning: **the picture itself may be quite ordinary, but the frame ties it to the interior and makes it read as more expensive.** A cheap way to raise perceived value — **and a real cost line to plan for, because it is charged separately.**

**If you cannot judge art yourself:**
- **⚠️ Hire a specialist or trust a gallery to select it.** Her analogy is her own repeated failure at buying cosmetics — going with friends, relatives, mother and sister all failed, **because people choosing with you choose for themselves.** Only a specialist who knew skin type and colour matching solved it. **The general principle: a companion is not a substitute for an expert, because a companion applies their own case to yours.**

## Design Concept — reproductions and decor

- **⚠️ Never bring reproductions of famous paintings — never, even one you did not recognise and only later discovered was a Matisse.** Her reason is saturation: the internet is full of interiors repeating the same picture. **Her historical parallel, which is the memorable part: it is the same phenomenon as the bears-in-the-pine-forest print that used to hang everywhere — only now it is an abstract woman, and it is a Matisse.**
- **Take instead an unknown author, or a reproduction of someone you had not heard of** — it will work far better.
- **⚠️ The cost-versus-constraint rule, and the sharpest budget content in the source**: if the object is cheap and not significant, **why should the rest of the interior be adjusted to it** — matched by colour, or worse by size, so that the picture fits one pier and not another and therefore **the cabinet goes in the other pier?** Her figure: **a 2,000 RUB reproduction** driving that. *"Какая-то очень странная история."*
  - **⚠️ Generalised, this is a rule worth keeping: the constraint an object imposes on the plan should be proportional to its value. Being forced to hunt for a place for a thing is the signal that it fails the test.**
- **What is worth carrying over or buying instead: rare posters, etchings («эстампы») or engravings («офорты»).** Archives of 20th-century material are findable in Moscow — exhibition posters from France, Queen performance bills. **Her figure: ~10,000 RUB, plus framing.**
  - **⚠️ The criterion she extracts from it, which generalises past posters: "эстетическая ценность этого предмета больше, чем его денежная стоимость."** That is the test for whether a decor object is worth its constraint.
  - **And the effect she is buying: it makes the interior ironic and alive, and gives the home a history — it looks as though it was collected rather than decorated in ten minutes**, even if it was. Applies equally to rare books, rare lamps, small tables.

## Design Concept — textiles and tableware

- **Plain beige curtains are not worth discussing** — bring them or do not, it changes nothing. **The question only arises for patterned textiles.**
- **⚠️ Two reasons patterned textile carried over usually fails**: (1) if the new home was designed without accounting for it, it probably will not fit; (2) **the same textile looks different under different lighting and in rooms of different size.**
- **⚠️ Her own working method, stated as standard practice: samples of cushions and curtains are always tried *at the client's*, not viewed in the showroom** — because the showroom is not where it will hang. **A directly adoptable procedure.**
- **Tableware, bed linen and bathroom accessories must match the style** like anything else.
- **Her worked case, which is the general rule for sentimental objects**: drinking cognac at a contemporary bar counter from your grandmother's service — **fine, if you will actually use it.** **⚠️ But do not bring the service if no one will use it, it does not suit, it takes up space, and the porcelain cannot go in the dishwasher.** *"Чтобы что?"* The saving on a new set of mugs does not justify it.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- Carrying an object across because it was expensive (sunk cost), rather than because it fits.
- Building a scheme around a picture that suits nothing.
- Bringing a series of canvases intended as one staggered composition.
- Reproductions of famous paintings.
- Letting a cheap object dictate the furniture plan.
- Judging patterned textile in a showroom instead of in the room.
- Keeping unused tableware to save on buying mugs.
- **Giving carried-over sentimental decor the most visible position in the flat.**
