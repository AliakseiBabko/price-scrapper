## ⚠️ Mirrors — Not Every Mirror Enlarges

This substantially extends the mirror material already in this vault, which had two conditions; this adds three more about the mirror itself.

- **A small mirror does not enlarge a room.** **Several small mirrors in a composition actively clutter it and make it read smaller.**
- **⚠️ Oval and framed mirrors read as decor, not as an optical device** — and what a small flat needs is something that "ломает пространство," breaks it, so the brain reads it as larger than it is. Decor doesn't do that.
- **⚠️ Only floor-to-ceiling mirrors, in the right places, achieve the effect.**
- **⚠️ Placement rule, and this is the new and concrete part: don't centre a mirror on a wall.** Set it into an internal corner so **one edge butts against the perpendicular wall**, or place it at a window. Pushed to the edge of the room, it reads as a continuation of space rather than as an object on a wall.
- Plus the condition already on record from `XGI6FS2ZdCc`: **what is reflected must not be visual noise** — better a clean wall or a plain cabinet than a cluttered worktop.

## ⚠️ The Counter-Intuitive One: Furniture Toward the Centre, Not Against the Walls

**Placing furniture toward the centre of the flat gives more sense of space than pushing it against the walls.** Her mechanism: **the walls stay clear, so you see their full height floor-to-ceiling, and it is verticals that produce the sense of ceiling height** — which is where the feeling of volume comes from, not floor area alone.

Worked applications: float the sofa after the dining group rather than against a wall; and **an island actually lightens a kitchen** because it frees the wall verticals for the eye. **This directly supports the island argument in her minimalism-mistakes video** (`Bnuim5NjgCU`), where she argues against L- and U-shaped kitchens — the two positions are the same idea reached from different directions.

## Materials and Furniture — What Makes a Small Room Read Smaller

- **⚠️ Avoid active textures** — not only busy stone patterns but **white marble with contrasting black veining**, and **concrete especially**. Take a calmer, less mottled texture if you want concrete at all.
- **⚠️ Avoid bulky furniture, and tall beds with tall headboards first of all.** Her reasoning is that space is felt in **volume — ceiling height — not just floor area**, and such beds eat it. **The lower and simpler the bed, the more space is felt.** Consistent with the 40 cm rest-furniture rule from Round 2 (`GcTaKRQ3QC0`), reached independently here.
- Same for dining chairs: a chair with lighter rails reads lighter and is also physically less bulky than a massive one.
- **⚠️ Eliminate unnecessary wall steps and jogs** — at planning stage, at rough-finish stage, or finally with joinery by turning the resulting niches into storage. Extra corners "засоряют помещение визуально, отвлекают на себя внимание, рассеивают его," making the space read cluttered and harder to parse.

## ⚠️ Colour and Finish Count — the Strongest Statement of a Recurring Position

**Don't use many colours, and don't use many tones either.** Her named failure: sofa one tone, curtains a second, walls a third, rug a fourth — **"если взять пипеткой цвет с каждого предмета и поместить кружочками рядом, получится просто палитра производителя красок."**

**⚠️ The rule scales with area: the smaller the flat, the fewer tones — and for very small flats, one colour in one tone throughout.**

> **This is the third statement of this position from this channel** (also `Bnuim5NjgCU`'s "50 shades of beige", and the two-wood-tones rule). **It is in direct tension with sources already in this vault** — Vittoria Selina's "stretch the beige across several lightnesses" and Студия Wall Street's "three to five coffee shades together" from the 2026-08-30 colour batch. **Routed as a Perspectives block rather than merged.**

**Don't use many finishes either** — concrete plus marble plus wood plus slat panels plus backlighting plus fabric wallpaper plus soft panels, all in one small flat. **Her prescription: settle on two or three materials** (e.g. smooth paint, microcement, wood) and **use рейки as the single decorative device** — which also raises perceived ceiling height — rather than adding 45°-angled panels, soft oval panels and rattan on top.

## Design Concept — Less Decor

**⚠️ Drop the compulsion to fill every horizontal surface.** The pattern she names: a composition on the low table, more decor on the island, something else on the dining table. **One large fruit bowl on the dining table is enough; a couple of magazines or a tray and one candle on the low table.** Her practical argument: life will add mugs and candles anyway, so buying decor for every surface up front is wasted money and effort.

## ⚠️ Three Myths She Debunks

Worth recording because each contradicts commonly-repeated small-space advice:

1. **Niches do not make a flat look smaller.** They break up otherwise dry wall and cabinet-front surfaces well — provided you don't fill them with small decor. "Книги ни нише, ни людям ещё никогда плохо не делали." Same for shelves: the problem is what's on them, not the shelf.
2. **Furniture on legs is not required to lighten an interior.** There is no such rule — **a low piece with simple forms works just as well.** *(Note: not a contradiction of her Round 2 advice to add legs to a bulky compact sofa — there, legs fix a specific proportional failure. Here she is rejecting legs as a universal requirement.)*
3. Implicitly, the first myth-adjacent point of the video: **mirrors and white walls are real but banal**, and are not what actually decides whether a small flat works.
