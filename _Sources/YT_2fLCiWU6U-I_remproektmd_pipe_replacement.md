## Plumbing — Durable Facts & Rules

1. **Real documented case, likely the same incident referenced in Video 1 of this trial batch** (`yt_zBF3iPJfbaw`) in less detail: a new-build client declined RemProektMD's recommendation to replace polypropylene heating-radiator pipes with Rehau PEX. A pressure test ("опрессовка") showed no pressure drop at the time. A leak later occurred at one of the polypropylene joints — this time located under bathroom tile, found "very quickly" by luck. The speakers explicitly generalize the risk: the same defect under **parquet/engineered wood flooring** would be far worse to diagnose and repair, since locating a specific leaking joint is inherently difficult (compared to a joint leak visible on a downstairs neighbor's ceiling, where you still can't pinpoint exactly which joint failed) and removing a single parquet plank to access it is "a whole undertaking" in itself.
2. **Stanislav's human-factor explanation for polypropylene joint failure timing**: welding/fusing a polypropylene pipe joint depends heavily on installer technique and consistency (heating each piece for the correct duration before joining) — a poorly-made joint is often **not detectable visually** at the time of installation, and can hold for years before failing suddenly ("like a delayed-action bomb"). This explains why a passing pressure test at handover doesn't guarantee long-term joint integrity.
3. **Polypropylene joint internal-diameter narrowing, demonstrated with a real cut-open joint — independent cross-channel corroboration of an existing store finding.** Stanislav cuts open both a good-quality and a deliberately poor-quality polypropylene joint on camera: the good joint shows a uniform, unnarrowed internal bore; the poor joint shows a visibly narrowed cross-section from excess/uneven weld material. **Stated consequence beyond the narrowing itself**: a narrowed joint can reduce delivered water pressure at downstream fixtures — described as a real, sometimes-encountered practical symptom ("some fixtures don't get enough pressure"), not just an aesthetic/structural concern. **This independently corroborates Zemstandart/Alexey Zemskov's existing claim in `12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Pipe_Material_Selection.md`** ("every polypropylene weld/fusion joint measurably narrows the pipe's internal diameter... visible when a real joint is cut open crosswise") — RemProektMD is an unrelated, non-Zemstandart channel, making this the first cross-channel corroboration of that specific mechanism in this store.
4. **Rehau PEX press-fit system, human-error resistance claim**: the fitting's internal diameter is manufactured to match the pipe's own internal diameter exactly, so **no narrowing occurs regardless of installer skill** (unlike polypropylene, whose joint quality depends on installer technique). Installation sequence described: slide the crimp ring onto the pipe, expand the pipe end with an expansion tool, insert the fitting, compress the ring — described as a process simple enough that installer error is effectively eliminated ("it's unrealistic to make a bad joint" with this system). This corroborates, from an independent channel, the existing store's Kruglov/Ontario-sourced claim that PEX is "low-skill-tolerant."
5. **Developer cost-cutting rationale for cheap polypropylene in new-builds** (companion point to Video 1's same claim): the speakers reiterate that developers commonly install the cheapest polypropylene pipe with many concealed joints under the screed specifically to save cost — framed as a real, recurring pattern across the projects they've worked on, not a one-off case.
6. **RemProektMD's own stated practice**: on nearly every new-build project, they recommend replacing pipes leading to both heating radiators and plumbing fixtures in wet rooms — but the decision is left to the client, and if declined, they perform a pressure test and disclose (per point 1 above) that a passing test doesn't guarantee no future leak.
