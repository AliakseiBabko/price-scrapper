## Mistakes / Warnings & Cost-Saving Strategies

- **⚠️ Heated floor should be scoped to only two real-need locations,
  not applied by default wherever there's tile**: an insulated loggia
  (only if actually insulated — an uninsulated loggia gains nothing from
  it) and the entryway (its real function there: melting snow tracked in
  on boots and drying the resulting puddle, not comfort). Everywhere
  else — kitchen, bathroom, bedroom — heated floor is framed as pure
  comfort spending, not a technical need, and is expensive to add
  (floor system + a thermostat/sensor unit priced comparably to the
  floor system itself, plus install labor).
- **⚠️ Correction on first read**: this is actually a **same-channel
  restatement**, not independent corroboration — this exact channel
  already carries this recommendation on `12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Soundproofing.md`
  (`YT_2cHetaq1bt0`, an earlier Kruglov/Ontario video). It does,
  however, independently corroborate a *separate*, cross-channel
  Zemskov-sourced note on `Budgeting_Guide.md` making the same
  floor-vs-ceiling recommendation. Restated here with an explicit
  mechanism not previously recorded: impact noise from an
  upstairs neighbor's footsteps transmits partly through the rigid
  structural connection between the floor slab and the building's
  concrete columns/walls, not only straight through the ceiling — so
  soundproofing only your own ceiling doesn't fully block wall-borne
  transmission, while cheap roll soundproofing under the neighbor's own
  floor addresses the noise at its source. **A new fallback named**: if
  the upstairs neighbor won't cooperate, soundproofing just the wall
  behind the bed's headboard (if a shared wall exists there) is
  described as a real, effective partial mitigation — narrower in scope
  than this store's existing general-wall-soundproofing note.
- **"Imperial style" decorative elements are named as a concrete,
  specific expensive-detail category worth cutting for budget**:
  recessed ceiling outlets, wide ceiling-to-wall crown molding/baguette,
  and (double) baguette picture-frame moldings applied to walls — all
  named as high-cost, low-durability-benefit choices to specifically
  question if the goal is saving money.
- **⚠️ Flush/recessed linear LED fixtures carry the same cost driver as
  concealed doors/baseboards — extensive painter finishing labor at
  every seam**: a linear LED fixture set flush into the ceiling or wall
  (especially one turning a corner from wall to ceiling) requires
  meticulous drywall-seam finishing comparable to concealed-mount
  hardware elsewhere in this store's existing content. **Direct
  cost-saving substitute stated**: use the same fixture type in a
  surface-mounted or pendant/track form instead of a flush-mount/
  recessed one — same lighting function, far less finishing labor.
- **LED backlighting (niches, baseboards, shelving) accumulates cost
  per-decision, not as one line item**: each individual LED strip
  location requires its own install labor plus a transformer and
  usually its own switch — framed as a "death by a thousand cuts"
  budget effect across many small decorative lighting choices rather
  than one big line item, worth reviewing in aggregate across a whole
  apartment's design.
- **⚠️ Flooring installation-method cost gap, with a concrete worked
  price comparison — extends video 1's abstract 1,000/2,000/3,000
  RUB/m² method-ambiguity mention with a real price example for
  parquet/engineered board**: floating install on underlay costs
  **≈750-800 RUB/m² (≈$10/m²)**, while the identical board glued
  directly to a leveled screed or self-leveling compound costs
  **≈2,000+ RUB/m² (≈$20/m²+)** — nearly double, for the same finished
  floor. **Material-specific constraints on this choice, previously
  unrecorded at this level of specificity**: laminate can *only* ever
  be installed floating (gluing is not an option regardless of budget);
  quartz-vinyl and parquet/engineered board (both have locking-joint
  variants) can be installed either way — choose floating whenever the
  material allows it, purely for the cost saving, with the tradeoff
  being a glued floor's better localized-repair characteristics.
- **⚠️ Wall preparation is named as ≈50% of total renovation labor cost
  regardless of the final finish chosen (tile, wallpaper, or paint)** —
  a major, previously-unquantified cost lever in this store's wall-
  finish content. **A second ≈50% cost gap exists within wall
  preparation itself**: preparing walls for paint costs roughly double
  preparing the same walls for wallpaper. **"Just paint the walls" is
  flagged as a misconception** — a true paint-ready wall needs a full
  multi-layer process: primer, two coats of leveling ("starting")
  putty with inter-layer sanding, a fiberglass-mesh (стеклохолст) layer,
  then two more coats of finish putty — a materially heavier prep
  process than wallpaper-ready walls require.
- **Don't apply decorative wall-prep/finish spend to surfaces no one
  will ever see** (behind fitted wardrobes, behind kitchen cabinetry) —
  a specific, named instance of a general "don't pay for invisible
  finish quality" principle.
- **Wallpaper-vs-paint durability/practicality is framed as genuinely
  context-dependent, not a fixed hierarchy**: how much a wall surface
  gets brushed against, and whether pets are present (which can damage
  either wallpaper or decorative plaster), matter more than an inherent
  material ranking — both are viable if installed correctly by a
  competent crew, including in a new-build apartment.
