## Planning Rules / Quantities/Measurements — Seven Reasons to Demolish As-Built Developer Partition Walls

- **A narrow entry hallway whose width equals the front door's width should have at least one of its bounding walls demolished and rebuilt**, to make room for four specific entry-zone functions the source considers standard: (1) a quick-access coat area for outerwear in current use, (2) a lit "makeup zone" with its own task lighting for close facial inspection before leaving, (3) storage beneath that zone, and (4) a full-length mirror for a head-to-toe check including shoes.
- **A wall with a 3-4cm out-of-plane step/ledge (uneven adjoining wall faces) cannot be corrected with masonry infill or a drywall-on-frame buildup — only re-plastering, and even that has a limit.** Masonry units are too large for a 3-4cm gap; drywall framing also can't be mounted in that little clearance. Re-plastering is possible but only up to a point: if the true offset between planes is ~40mm, the required leveling coat becomes too thick for plaster to hold (it will detach), and even if it doesn't detach, that thick a layer takes very long to cure, delaying downstream work (electrical, skim-coating). **Rule: past roughly 40mm of wall-plane offset, demolish and rebuild that wall section to the correct plane rather than trying to level it with any surface material**, then patch only the small remaining offset with a thicker (but still workable) plaster coat.
- **Bathtub-fit feasibility has explicit numeric width thresholds**: minimum workable bathtub width is 70cm; a genuinely comfortable width is 80cm. **The wall segment (bortovka) beside the tub's entry edge must exceed the tub's own width by at least 10cm** — so a 70cm tub needs at least 80cm of adjoining wall width, and an 80cm tub needs at least 90cm — specifically so the door casing/trim doesn't land flush against the tub's rim (a strip of the wall's own tile/finish should remain visible between door trim and tub edge). A defect found: an adjoining segment of only 58cm (with an even narrower 42cm segment nearby) fails this threshold outright, making the intended tub impossible to install as planned even though the room's total length appeared to have enough space. A wall niche cutting into one end of an otherwise long-enough run also disqualifies that run for a tub, even with apparent linear space to spare.
- **Free-standing (structurally unconnected) partition wall segments that "live their own life"** — i.e., built without proper corner tie-in (обвязка) to adjoining walls or lintels — **will develop cracks along every un-tied joint over time**, no matter how the seam is patched or bridged afterward. **Rule: any rebuilt or newly built partition must be tied in at every corner it meets, including tie-in to the exterior wall**, not just load-bearing-to-load-bearing connections; developers are described as routinely omitting exterior-wall tie-in specifically.
- **Doorway (rough opening) width needs an explicit formula, especially in apartments with tall ceilings**: minimum recommended rough-opening width is **1.10m (110cm)**, derived as an 80cm door leaf needing a 90cm opening, plus two 10cm side reveals (bortovka) — 90 + 10 + 10 = 110cm. **A 60cm door is called "narrow and shabby," acceptable only as a last resort for a WC or small storage closet** — never for living spaces, and especially not paired with tall (2.3m) door heights, which the source says makes an undersized door look worse still. **General door-width targets: minimum 70cm everywhere, minimum 80cm for living-space doors.** **Corridor width recommendation, specifically for tall-ceiling apartments using 2.3m doors: minimum 1.2m (120cm).** This numeric derivation (110cm opening ⟵ 90cm door + 2×10cm reveals) is a genuinely new, checkable formula for this store, extending its existing bare "70cm/80cm door" figures with the reveal-allowance logic behind them.
- **A pantry/storage closet with very shallow corner reveals (bortovka) on both sides of its opening can become almost entirely unusable for storage** — the defect found: two ~2cm reveals left only a hook-sized sliver of usable wall on each side, wasting nearly the room's full ~2m² floor area since almost none of it could be reached without navigating around the narrow entry. A described "fix" some designers attempt — closing one opening and cutting a new one on the opposite side to reclaim two separate storage zones — reintroduces the un-tied-wall cracking risk above, and the added structural work (cutting a new opening, adding a lintel) can cost more than simply demolishing and rebuilding the whole partition from scratch.
- **A pantry (or similar storage room) directly adjoining a corner kitchen module run needs its usable depth checked against the kitchen module's own standard depth, not just the pantry's raw floor dimension.** Standard kitchen module depth is **600mm**. A defect found: a 98cm-deep pantry pocket next to a corner kitchen run left only a very tight, uncomfortable residual space once a corner kitchen module was installed against it — that corner, being cramped, would also take physical wear/scuffing quickly (source estimates needing touch-up roughly every 2 weeks). Moving the dividing wall to fit the kitchen better only makes the adjoining pantry narrower/more useless and can eliminate the reveals needed for a valid opening there — the source's conclusion is the whole wall configuration (kitchen + pantry) needs to be replanned together, not adjusted piecemeal.
- **A 80mm-thick gypsum-block/paz-greben (tongue-and-groove) partition wall is explicitly called an inadequate soundproofing thickness** — described with a demonstrated example: knocking/pushing on the wall by hand or foot visibly flexed and audibly transmitted through it, and repeated flexing produced a visible horizontal crack even where reinforcing mesh/fiberglass fabric had been applied under wallpaper. **Rule: an 80mm paz-greben partition should be treated as insufficient for real sound isolation between rooms**, independent of surface finishing choices layered on top of it.
