## Rules 1–2 — which finishes actually matter

- **⚠️ Rule 1: an item's significance follows its visual area, so not every wood finish deserves equal effort.**
  - **Least important**: small items — chair legs, wooden armrests on upholstered chairs, small non-accent tables.
  - **Middle**: worktops and chests of drawers.
  - **Most important**: **floors, doors and wall panels.**
  - **⚠️ Doors are the special case, and the reasoning is the useful part**: a door occupies no more visual area than a chest, **but doors repeat from room to room and tie the whole apartment together, so you have to reckon with them in every room.** If the doors are wood, treat them as elevated importance.
  - **The practical payoff, stated explicitly: you do not need to keep hunting for a perfect match for chair legs.** Some finishes deserve more thought than others, and knowing which saves the search.
- **⚠️ Rule 2: choose a principal finish deliberately — and do not let area choose it for you.** "Если исходить исключительно из площади, то получается, что самым важным всегда будут полы, а это не так." The principal finish can instead be the brightest — floors as background, with the table and chairs leading, or a bright chest on the room's central axis to which everything else is tied. Companions are then selected against that principal.
  - **⚠️ The counter-case, which is a procurement reality rather than a design rule**: sometimes you fit the principal to a standard instead. Chairs are her example — a shop offers only one or three leg finishes with a given upholstery. **It is easier to choose the chairs and adapt made-to-order wall panels to them than the reverse, even when the wall panels are the principal finish.**

## Rules 3–4 — undertone, and the experiment that reframes the tone-count question

- **⚠️ Rule 3: match by undertone («подтон»), not by name.** Most woods have a warm undertone — yellow, pink, orange or red. Some are obvious: **walnut goes red, as do palisander and mahogany.** Some are not: **oak can be a "chameleon" — under any light it reads slightly olive/greenish, and under cold light it goes bluish.** The eye has to be trained, because undertone drives compatibility more than anything else.
- **⚠️ She then tests the received wisdom and partly rejects it.** The claim she read online: "the more wood tones, the more eclectic the interior," implying one tone for a modern, laconic result. **Her first objection is definitional — eclecticism means mixing *styles*, not mixing finishes**, so several finishes do not make a room eclectic.
- **⚠️ The decisive experiment, and it is the most valuable thing in the source.** She takes a carefully tone-matched interior and edits it twice:
  1. **Change brightness only** — darken a partition, keeping the yellow undertone. Result: **more varied, less laconic — "но это не хорошо и не плохо."** Still a good interior.
  2. **Change brightness *and* undertone** — make the same partition reddish. Result: **"это уже не разнообразный интерьер, а просто вудхаус."** The colours fight, it is unclear whether red or yellow leads, **"интерьер разваливается."**
- **⚠️ The conclusion this yields is sharper than any tone count: vary brightness freely; do not vary undertone.** The failure mode people attribute to "too many wood tones" is actually an undertone collision.
- **⚠️ Rule 4: do not mix warm and cold wood.** Some finishes go grey, greyish-blue («сизый») or very green. She notes she could not find a single example of a designer deliberately colliding the two.
  - **But warm and cold are relative, like small and large.** Two finishes each reading cold in isolation: put them together and one becomes the warm one. A very green-blue finish makes a merely cool one read calm by comparison.
  - **⚠️ The prescription if you have cold wood and want warmth: take it from the wall colour, not from a second wood finish.** Mixing warm and cold wood is "только для любителей жареного мороженого."

## Rules 5–7 — count, contrast, and texture load

- **⚠️ Rule 5: a maximum of three wood finishes** — three makes the interior read whole, because each finish then covers more than one object, and it lets you build a hierarchy. Worked example: wall panels (primary) → chairs (secondary, slightly paler) → a chest (tertiary, palest, low-contrast against its wall).
  - **⚠️ And she immediately relativises her own rule.** She found a designer who specialises in combining many wood tones as a signature and **counted five to seven finishes in one seemingly simple interior** — beautiful and not chaotic, **because structure is still present.** "Такому правилу, правилу трёх, не обязательно следовать." **The count is a heuristic; the structure is the requirement.**
- **⚠️ Rule 6: the differences must be obvious.** A room with many *similar* wood tones can read as a mistake — it looks like someone attempted tone-on-tone and failed. **Choose tones with a larger gap.** Scoped: applies only to interiors deliberately built on multiple wood tones.
- **⚠️ Rule 7: only one bold texture** — texture here meaning grain and annual rings, i.e. how variegated the wood is. Bold grain is typical of exotic species and **reads best on a large flat object — a chest, or wall panels.** Do not duplicate it: two bold finishes will not join, because it is unclear which should dominate. **Support a bold finish with plainer, more uniform woods, or with painted furniture.** Her example of a room built on one very bold wood has no wood companions at all — even the chair and table colours were selected to fall inside that wood's tone.

## Rule 8 — texture is tied to style far more tightly than colour is

- **⚠️ Wood colour alone cannot establish a style.** Wenge or chocolate floors suggest Art Deco but work in a classical interior; matte light oak suggests Scandinavian but also works in classical. **"По одной отделке стиль определить невозможно" — colour can support a style, not define it.**
- **⚠️ Texture, by contrast, is style-bound.** Loft and country use rough, rustic, deeply brushed wood. **That wood cannot be used in classical**, which carries little wood texture — classical uses polished wood, or wood at roughly **15% gloss** under lacquer, and no more. Rustic will not go there.
- **⚠️ The prescription, and it is quotable: "лучше использовать одну фактуру в разных цветах, чем один цвет в разных фактурах."** One texture across several colours beats one colour across several textures.
- **Worked example**: against a rough brushed floorboard, a smooth light walnut that matches on *undertone* still fails — **one is rough, the other too smooth, and the textures fight.** A saw-marked finish with relief matches far better. **Better still, match texture and colour together** — she finds greyish-blue veins inside the brushed board and pairs it with a rough wood that is grey outside and yellow inside, inverting the relationship. Simplify by using painted wood where the tone peeks through.
- **Use one texture across the whole interior for wholeness** — touching the bar stool legs, the floor and the table should give roughly the same sensation.
- **⚠️ She criticises published examples that texture-play**, which is worth noting because they came from articles presenting them as good interiors: a semi-gloss floor with very matte rough wood on the table — "сочетание такое себе"; a glossy floor with brushed wood **on the ceiling** — "если бы сделали просто белый потолок, было бы лучше, перемудрили."
- **It can work, but it is a skill**: simple floors, fresh white walls, and against them a genuinely aged console — "выглядит круто, но так нужно уметь."

## Rules 9–10 — the two most practical

- **⚠️ Rule 9: when in doubt, separate. "При сомнении разделяйте."** If two tones are doubtful together, put a buffer between them. Her examples: a dark coffee table over very light floors reads harmonious **because a rug sits between them**; and if wall panels and a wardrobe are doubtful together, **move the wall panel so the wardrobe stands against a painted wall instead.** Create a buffer zone rather than forcing the match.
- **⚠️ Rule 10: use physical samples — "довольно очевидно, но почему-то на практике так никто не делает."**
  - **Choosing wood from a photograph is useless** — a photo never conveys it.
  - **⚠️ And choosing from memory fails too, which is the subtler and more useful point.** You see light oak at the flooring shop, remember it, then go and choose furniture. **The two shops have different lighting**, so you have no idea how that floor looks under the other light.
  - **The correct method: ask the flooring seller for a sample board against a deposit, carry it to the furniture shop, and compare there.**
  - **A newer service she endorses: sample delivery** — someone brings the shortlisted boards to your site **so you see them under your own lighting.**
  - "Ни один нормальный дизайнер не выбирает оттенки дерева просто по фотографии."

## Design Concept — her closing qualification, which matters as much as the rules

- **⚠️ Wood does not always lead. "Часто формы предметов и палитра важнее для стиля, чем оттенок дерева."**
- **You can build an interior on contrasted wood tones, but you do not have to.** Her example: a bedroom with very specific bluish floors used throughout the house — **they are not the lead in the palette at all.** The room is built on wall colour and textile selection, and the floors are simply woven into the general fabric. **"Вполне достаточно, чтобы дерево не противоречило общей концепции и вписывалось в палитру."**
