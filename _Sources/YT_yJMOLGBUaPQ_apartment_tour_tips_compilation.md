## Durable Facts

- **Two-way ("проходной") switches for adjoining rooms**: all mutually-adjacent passage rooms (e.g. entry → corridor → living room, or corridor → bedroom) should share one switch group controlling the same light circuit, switchable from every entry point into that connected chain — so a person never has to backtrack to turn off a light in a room they've already left. Stated as a standing rule across the speaker's projects.
- **Dedicated "duty"/night light circuit**: a separate, always-on (never turned off, day or night) low-level light — distinct from the main light — installed in hallways/entries/bathrooms to allow navigating the apartment at night without triggering bright main lighting. Controlled from at least the entry door and typically from multiple points; supplements a bright/glare-prone main ceiling fixture when the customer wants overhead light anyway.
- **"Master switch" for the whole apartment**: a single switch (by the entry door in the example) that can shut off ALL lighting circuits — main and duty light — apartment-wide in one action, useful for a customer leaving with several rooms' lights left on. Framed as somewhat more expensive to wire but a high-convenience feature.
- **Dedicated makeup-zone lighting and outlets**: a mirror/vanity area intended for applying makeup should have its own dedicated light source (separate from general room lighting) plus its own switch and at least one outlet, distinct from a full-length mirror elsewhere in the room, because general room lighting alone isn't sufficient for close facial inspection.
- **Video door-intercom placement**: mount it in the "clean zone" (living area), not the "dirty zone" near the entry/shoe area — so answering it doesn't require walking across a dirty floor in socks/bare feet, which the speaker calls a "critically important" but frequently ignored design detail.
- **Thin ("утоньшённый," ~6 mm) door casings require matching ultra-thin baseboards.** A thick baseboard meeting a thin casing leaves a visibly cut/exposed baseboard edge at the joint — always acceptable to pair a thick casing with a thin baseboard, never the reverse.
- **Hidden vs. exposed door hinges — cost delta is small relative to overall project cost.** Cited example: a plain exposed hinge cost ~700 RUB vs. ~3,000 RUB for a normal (non-fully-concealed) hidden hinge — a ~2,300 RUB difference the speaker frames as ~0.004% of a full renovation budget, arguing it's never worth economizing on given how visually poor cheap exposed hinges look.
- **Avoid glass-insert panels in interior bathroom/WC doors** — cited reasons: visible fingerprints, and significantly worse sound isolation into/out of the room compared to a solid door.
- **Electric-point layout via centerline referencing**: instead of dimensioning each electric point from a wall corner, define a virtual centerline (e.g. centered on a future bed) at a set offset from the corner, then dimension each point symmetrically from that centerline. Produces provably symmetric point placement (e.g. matching sconces/outlets either side of a bed) regardless of minor furniture-placement shifts, and is presented as a technique any designer could adopt.
- **Kitchen backsplash-to-countertop material match**: using the same slab material (e.g. porcelain/quartz) for both backsplash and countertop, joined with a thin clear sealant bead instead of a trim strip, is cited as cheaper than expected (compared vs. a basic porcelain-tile backsplash) while producing a seamless, higher-end look with no visible baseboard/trim needed at the joint.
- **Kitchen outlet placement rule**: every open/free counter zone should get its own outlet; the main cooking zone additionally needs its own task-lighting switch. Every free auxiliary counter zone likewise gets its own outlet.
- **Avoid touch/capacitive-sensor under-cabinet lighting controls for standard-tier kitchens** — cited as unreliable (doesn't reliably respond to a hand-wave despite marketing claims) and less convenient than a simple physical switch, especially with wet/dirty hands (which can still press a physical switch but may be reluctant to touch a sensor pad). Speaker allows it may be appropriate for premium-segment kitchens but not standard-tier ones.
- **Vertical radiators** are presented as an increasingly common alternative to standard horizontal radiators for three stated reasons: aesthetics, higher heat output for a given footprint, and fitting in spaces too narrow for a standard radiator. No brand recommendation given by design.
- **Level (flush, threshold-free) flooring across the whole apartment** is achievable specifically because a combined sound+waterproofing membrane is applied continuously under the whole floor — since most customers want floor soundproofing anyway, and that membrane already provides the necessary waterproofing, going threshold-free (single level, no door-sill steps) becomes a natural extension rather than extra work. Ties directly to the single-contour waterproofing content in this batch's other sources.
- **Access-hatch (люк) selection**: standard sagging-mechanism access hatches are described as unreliable at any size — speaker states he now personally excludes ("removes to the trash") any other hatch type on his projects. A non-sagging mechanism type is described as openable/closable one-handed regardless of size/weight, versus standard hatches needing awkward multi-point force (suction cup, foot, elbow) to budge; demonstrated load-tested by the speaker's full body weight with negligible deflection. One specific manufacturer/storefront is named in the source as the sole current Russian producer of this mechanism — recorded here only as context for the claim, not as an endorsed brand per this project's no-brand-names convention; the durable fact is the sagging-vs-non-sagging mechanism distinction itself.
- **Exhaust-fan switch placement**: should be located near the toilet itself (not elsewhere in the bathroom), so it's within immediate reach for prompt odor control.
- **Separate laundry/closet room ("прачечная-гардеробная") preferred over combining laundry function with a bathroom**, where floor plan allows — reasoning given: a single shared bathroom becomes a traffic bottleneck if it also houses laundry; a separate utility room can hold a large dirty-laundry hamper, both washer and dryer (or a drying cabinet), an additional towel warmer, a ceiling drying rack, and general storage (cleaning supplies, spare toilet paper stock) without competing for space with bathroom fixtures.
