## Planning Rules / Layout Technique

- **Never leave a partial-depth wall stub/jog (a wall that returns only partway, e.g. ~10cm, before continuing on a different plane)** — a common bad developer condition. Rule: demolish and rebuild such stubs flush; the extra material/labor cost is easily recovered by avoiding two extra external+internal corners at stretch-ceiling install and an ugly protruding baseboard/cornice detail at that corner.
- **A deliberately-kept narrow wall "pier"/return next to an opening (here, ~60cm) can be the key structural anchor for a large adjacent storage build-out** — rather than fully opening the width for a more spacious-looking doorway, the narrow pier let this project form a proper opening jamb *and* install a large built-in wardrobe covering all outerwear/shoe storage plus a fast-access changing zone, extended by an angled corner module to also fit a server-equipment rack. A narrower opening with more storage can beat a wider opening with less.
- **Wet-zone (bathroom/WC) relocation is more likely to be approvable when the new location sits above a hallway/corridor in the unit below, rather than above another unit's living space** — real practitioner claim about relocation-approval feasibility criteria. `single-account`, level-2 regulatory-adjacent claim, not independently verified — does not clear this store's stricter bar for the separate regulations store.
- **Consolidate cramped, multi-way doorway junctions (where several traffic flows converge in one tight spot) by redistributing entries through a wider adjoining space** — real example: bathroom, WC, and kitchen entries originally converged in one narrow, congested corridor; relocating the wet zone (per the point above) allowed widening the bathroom/WC entries and routing the kitchen entry through the living room instead, producing two comfortably isolated wet rooms and a wide kitchen entry.
- **Technique: convert an oversized, over-functional room's excess area into dedicated storage by moving a dividing wall inward while leaving the vacated strip on the far side as a deep recessed closet** — preserves the smaller room's full rectangular shape and function while gaining a genuinely large closet, described as a "non-standard" move worth considering whenever a room is functionally oversized.
- **Technique: shorten a long, narrow, poorly-proportioned room (whose length is fixed by a load-bearing end wall, preventing a comfortable bed-to-TV viewing distance) by building a new partition partway into it**, converting the "lost" far end into a separate walk-in closet/dressing room with its own door — resolves the original room's bad proportions and creates additional storage without losing any of its core function. A load-bearing wall constraining a room's length doesn't have to be worked around structurally — the usable portion can simply be redefined smaller.
