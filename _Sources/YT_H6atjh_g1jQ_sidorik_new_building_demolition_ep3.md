## Regulations / Permits / Approvals (Belarus level 1 — routed to `16_Legal_and_Regulations/`)

- **Unlike a described Russian practice, a Belarusian developer will not pay a cash penalty or apartment-price discount in lieu of fixing a defect** — per this practitioner's direct, Belarus-named statement, the developer's only two postures are: fix it themselves (typically stretched out over a long time), or leave it to the buyer to fix at their own expense. No negotiated financial settlement path was observed as available in this speaker's own Belarus experience.
- **Realistic defect-remediation timeline, real example**: a window/sash replacement took roughly **1.5 months** from complaint to resolution; the speaker's broader experience is that developers try to stretch minor-defect fixes over multiple months regardless of any nominal timeline that may exist on paper.
- **Screed cracks are not a screed-replacement claim** — the most a buyer can expect from the developer for cracked/damaged screed is a patch/fill of the crack itself, not a full screed replacement.
- **A buyer's own unqualified/uncredentialed acceptance inspection carries the same legal weight as one performed by a credentialed specialist** — per the speaker's direct answer to a viewer's specific worry about this: "a defect exists or it doesn't," independent of who identifies it or what training they have.
- **Deep glass-pane chips/scratches are grounds for full sash/glazing-unit replacement, not just cosmetic accommodation** — confirmed with a real example (two of this apartment's largest glazing units replaced by the developer after the speaker flagged deep chips at acceptance), reinforcing episode 1's general rule with a concrete outcome.

## Demolition (new taxonomy area — no dedicated wiki page exists yet, see Target Page(s))

- **Two-person demolition technique for a masonry partition wall**: one person on a jackhammer (отбойный молоток — speaker prefers a medium-size unit specifically because it's easier to hold up against a vertical wall), the other on a sledgehammer (кувалда, described as equally essential as the jackhammer itself), alternating/working in parallel; a rotary hammer (перфоратор) as an auxiliary tool, angle grinder for cutting embedded rebar, standard hammer for finishing work. Full PPE required: dust mask, safety glasses, hearing protection (earmuffs or earplugs).
- **Improvised dust-suppression technique: repurposing an airless paint sprayer ("безвоздушка") to mist down dust during demolition** — the speaker's own idea, explicitly planned for reuse across future demolition jobsites when the sprayer isn't needed elsewhere for its normal paint-spraying purpose.
- **Real construction data point on developer-built masonry partition walls found in this apartment**: brick, ~3 cm plaster coat, facade reinforcing mesh, with structural reinforcement embedded every third course ("армирован... каждый третий ряд") — described as a genuinely substantial, non-trivial wall to demolish, not a lightweight partition.
- **Remove the toilet bowl before demolishing surrounding bathroom walls**, to avoid it being broken by falling debris/impact.
- **Shut off the heating riser before breaking up a screed** — if a heating pipe embedded in the screed is accidentally struck, this limits the water release to only what's already in the pipes inside that one apartment, rather than the full building heating system's pressure/volume.
- **Never strike blindly once a pipe is located under a screed** — break the screed on both sides of the identified pipe and clear the broken material by hand near it, rather than continuing with the jackhammer close to a known pipe location.
- **A heavier rented jackhammer, rested on the screed itself rather than held up by the operator, is the right tool for full screed removal** (contrasted with the lighter, hand-held-preferred hammer used for the vertical wall demolition above) — worth renting a heavier unit specifically for this stage rather than using the same tool for both jobs.
- **Concrete-surface prep before plastering: grind monolithic walls/ceilings/ventilation-duct surfaces with a concrete grinder to remove cement laitance ("цементное молоко") and open the surface pores** — stated mechanism/reason: a concrete-contact primer ("бетоноконтакт") commonly fails to bond specifically at the laitance layer, so removing it before priming/plastering avoids a later adhesion failure. Applied to the ceiling as well, for the same reason, before any plastering — grinding overhead is harder (has to be held above the operator), so the crew alternates by section (one person grinds one rectangle, rests; the next person takes the following section) to manage fatigue.
- **A real accident during screed demolition, with a useful structural takeaway**: a toilet bowl tipped over during the work (unnoticed amid the demolition noise) and spilled its tank's water — no damage to the unit below occurred because the **monolithic floor slab does not pass water through it**, illustrating a real (if accidental) practical test of the monolithic-slab's own waterproofing/isolation property between floors, distinct from any applied waterproofing membrane.
- **Post-screed-removal floor prep**: the exposed floor is typically covered in leftover mortar/plaster/cement splatter from the building's own construction phase (~5 mm of extra debris beyond the bare slab) — clean and flatten it with a concrete-cleaning grinder attachment plus a vacuum before installing a soundproofing membrane, since the membrane needs a clean, relatively smooth substrate to perform correctly.

## Planning Rules — A Practitioner's Decision Framework for What to Demolish and Why (real, worked, itemized)

**Why demolish bathroom/WC partition walls in a new-build apartment, even when they're structurally sound** (5 stated reasons, all this same project):
1. The room footprint is being resized (toilet room shrinking, bath enlarging).
2. The developer's partition walls are unnecessarily thick (15-16 cm as-built) and can be rebuilt at 12 cm including plaster — gaining ~4 cm of usable width per affected side.
3. The existing bathroom has no true 90° corners and uneven/crooked plaster, which must be removed regardless (plaster this poor can't just be skimmed over).
4. A developer-built false wall (фальшстена) exists at 12 cm thickness specifically so residents never need to cut into the monolithic structure for plumbing/electrical runs — a sound idea in principle, but oversized; the speaker plans to rebuild it much thinner, sized exactly to the pipe diameters it needs to conceal, and to omit it entirely behind the toilet (where a furniture-based rear structure will serve the same concealment purpose).
5. The room's outer boundary relative to adjacent living spaces stays fixed — only the internal split between toilet and bath is being changed, not the overall wet-zone footprint.

**Why demolish an already-relatively-flat developer screed, even when it looks acceptable** (5 stated reasons, all this same project):
1. A soundproofing underlayment will be installed on the bare substrate beneath the new screed — needs the old screed removed to place it correctly.
2. The developer's screed is thicker than necessary; removing and replacing it thinner recovers ceiling height.
3. Floor-routed electrical cabling needs space under the new screed (the ceiling is not being dropped in this project, so this is the only place to route it).
4. Even a relatively flat developer screed isn't flat enough for a truly precise finish — but pouring a new, deliberately leveled screed avoids needing a self-leveling compound (наливной пол) afterward, since the new screed is poured level from the start.
5. Chases (штробы) need to be cut into the base for new partition walls to sit on, which is easier to do on an exposed slab than through an existing screed.
(A sixth, related benefit noted separately: removing the screed also allows a full inspection/revision of the embedded heating system and makes radiator replacement easier without a screed layer in the way.)

## Cost Drivers / Labor Prices — Debris Removal Logistics

- **Debris-removal workflow used in this apartment**: bags of debris carried to the elevator, taken down by elevator, then moved from the lobby to the building's own dumpster container via the self-built hand cart from episode 2 — no truck/hauling service needed because this specific building has a ground-floor dumpster and no stairs blocking the path (**not generalizable** — the speaker notes explicitly this depends on a given building having no stair obstruction and an on-site container).
- **Informal student labor for bulk debris hauling**: roughly 90% of this apartment's debris removal was done by hired students, with the remaining 10% done by the speaker himself on the day the students didn't show up — a real, if informal, labor-sourcing pattern for the most physically repetitive/low-skill part of demolition.
- **Real debris-volume figures for this ~61 m² apartment**: ~15 tonnes total debris across the whole demolition phase; of that, ~6 tonnes was screed alone (10 cm screed thickness); total demolition phase duration ≈5 days.
