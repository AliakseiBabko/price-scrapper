## Regulations / Permits / Approvals (Russian-code-sourced, secondary reference only — see Region note)

- **Plaster flatness tolerance (Russian СНиП/СП, as cited by the
  speaker)**: max **3 mm deviation per 1 m** measured vertically, not to
  exceed **10 mm over the full wall height**; horizontal-plane deviation
  also up to 3 mm/m, with local unevenness allowed up to **4 mm per 1 m²**
  — checked with a 2 m+ rule/rail plus a small bubble level taped to it
  (improvised ~2.7 m level), or a laser level for speed.
- **Screed acceptance tolerances (same code)**: perimeter gap to walls
  should be **≥10 mm**, filled with a damper/expansion tape running the
  full perimeter; slope should be **no more than 2 mm per 1 m of run**;
  gap between the screed surface and a 2 m rail/rule should be **no more
  than 2 mm**. Checked by laser level plus a square/tape to watch beam
  deviation while walking the apartment, or by rail alone if no laser is
  available.
- **Ceiling height norm cited**: **2.655–2.855 m** allowable range for a
  monolithic-frame building per this project's design documentation
  practice, with a stated as-built tolerance of **±1.5 cm** across a whole
  apartment for monolithic construction — the speaker explicitly notes
  measuring "isn't useful" once a discrepancy falls inside that ±1.5 cm
  band, since no claim can be made against the developer for it.
- **90° corner tolerance**: not itself in the cited code, but the speaker
  personally checks every corner for square (checked with a large
  carpenter's square) specifically because it matters for later cabinetry/
  furniture fit in bathrooms/kitchens — a self-imposed practitioner check,
  not a regulatory requirement.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Developer Handover Tactics (from personal, on-camera experience)

- **"Sign first, keys after" pressure tactic**: developer or its
  representatives ask the buyer to sign the acceptance act *before*
  handing over keys, sometimes softened as "you have 20 minutes to look
  at the apartment, then get your keys and be glad it was built at all."
  Speaker's own stated legal view: this is baseless — a buyer can inspect
  for as long as needed and bring any specialist to the walkthrough.
- **Psychological pressure during the walkthrough**: developer staff/
  foremen/contractor reps crowd the buyer during acceptance, rushing them
  and asserting fabricated technical justifications for real defects
  (e.g. claiming a tilted windowsill is intentional "for water drainage,"
  or that hollow-sounding plaster is intentional "for better
  soundproofing"). Advised response: don't yield to the pressure, record
  every real defect in the acceptance act regardless.
- **"Agree but stall" tactic**: developer agrees to every defect claim on
  paper but does nothing, running out the clock until the buyer — unable
  to start renovation and wanting to move forward — gives up and signs
  the act anyway. Described as the most common of the three tactics.
- **Defect masking before viewing**: covering scratches on windows/
  radiators/windowsills with film or stickers, or patching a
  chipped/holed windowsill with filler — cosmetic concealment that
  resurfaces once the buyer notices it later, after the act is already
  signed.
- **Pre-whitening ("false finish") tactic**: developer skim-coats
  (шпатлюет) bare walls/ceiling white purely to make a raw-concrete
  apartment look more finished/appealing to a buyer at point of sale — the
  speaker's assessment is this is a net negative for the buyer, not a
  bonus, because the skim coat is typically the cheapest available
  material and low-quality workmanship, meaning it has to be stripped and
  redone anyway rather than saving any real work. Attributed to developers
  generally lacking good in-house finishing specialists.
- **Area/quantity-inflation techniques cited as a general developer
  pattern** (not this speaker's own apartment): thinning interior walls,
  protruding structural columns into rooms, leaving un-enclosed niches —
  all cited as ways a developer pads a unit's billed floor area with
  functionally useless space the buyer still pays for.
- **Real-world recovery example (personal anecdote, Brest, level-1
  location for this anecdote only)**: in an earlier, unrelated building
  the speaker's household bought into (a one-room panel-building
  apartment in Brest), a lawyer-by-training future resident of a
  previously-handed-over building in the same complex cross-checked the
  actual as-built work against the project/estimate documentation
  ("проектно-сметная документация") and within a week documented enough
  overbilled/unperformed work that **every apartment across that building
  and the buildings handed over before it** (including the speaker's own)
  received a refund of **several thousand US dollars per apartment** — cited
  as evidence that comparing project/estimate paperwork against actual
  as-built work is a real, financially material check, not just a
  formality. (No date given for this anecdote; not usable as a
  current-year price data point, only as a technique example.)

## Planning Rules — Acceptance Toolkit and Method

- **Recommended tool list for a developer-handover walkthrough**: bubble
  level (speaker uses 1.2 m), portable/rechargeable work light (battery-
  powered lights preferred over relying on a single working outlet, which
  may not exist yet), tape measure (plus a laser distance meter for
  speed), a sheet of A4 paper (used for the ventilation suction test,
  below), a 2 m+ straightedge/rule (taped to the bubble level to make an
  improvised long level), a large carpenter's square, a small stepladder,
  a marker/pen and notebook.
- **Ventilation check method**: open a window (ventilation won't register
  as working with windows closed) and hold an A4 sheet against the kitchen
  or bathroom vent grille — if the sheet is sucked against the grille,
  the vent is drawing air correctly.
- **Electrical check method**: with only a distribution panel and one
  outlet typically live at handover, test the outlet directly; for a
  panel building with wiring already run to every outlet, walk the
  apartment with a simple test lamp/bulb screwed into a socket adapter and
  confirm live power at every outlet individually.
- **Recommended acceptance path**: three options given — do it yourself
  (using a checklist like this video), hire an independent inspection
  specialist/organization, or bring the same finishing crew who will do
  the renovation. Speaker's own paid-inspection practice: he charges for
  a stand-alone acceptance inspection, but refunds that fee against the
  renovation contract if the client then hires his crew for the actual
  work.
- **Heating-season-specific checks**: if accepting in winter, use a
  thermal-imaging camera to find window seal gaps (poor closure shows as
  a cold streak) and to trace underfloor/wall heating-pipe routing
  (chalk-mark actual pipe locations on the floor, since the developer's
  as-built schematic is not always accurate) — useful before any future
  wall/floor penetration work.
- **BTI-style area/technical-passport measurement is described as low-
  value to redo personally** — a state body (БТИ, cited as the Russian/
  regional equivalent process) re-measures and issues the official
  technical passport regardless of what the buyer measures themselves;
  the buyer's own remeasurement mainly matters if the purchase contract's
  price is itself area-contingent (both an under- and over-build scenario
  are described as real, contract-dependent outcomes).

## Quantities / Measurements

- **Area discrepancy, real example**: contract stated 62 m², as-built came
  to 61 m² — contract was written so that neither side owed the other for
  the difference either way ("сколько получится, столько получится").
  Given as a real-world illustration of how developer contracts can
  structure area risk, not as a universal clause to expect.
- **As-built ceiling height, real example**: contract/sales conversation
  promised 2.7 m; project documentation actually specified 2.67 m;
  speaker's own multi-point laser-distance-meter measurement across the
  apartment read **2.66–2.67 m**, inside the code's ±1.5 cm as-built
  tolerance band cited above — noted as a case where a shortfall from a
  verbally-promised figure was not actionable once checked against the
  real documentation and code tolerance. Speaker notes some other units in
  the same building read closer to 2.7 m because the installed screed
  there was thinner; his own unit's screed was thicker, consuming more of
  the vertical allowance.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Fixture/Finish-Specific Acceptance Notes

- **Entry door**: not worth a thorough inspection — developer-supplied
  entry doors are almost always the cheapest available and get replaced
  during renovation regardless; only confirm it opens/closes for the
  interim period.
- **Windows** (called out as the single highest-defect-rate item):
  check presence of handles/hardware and decorative hinge covers (often
  missing), inspect weatherstripping on both sash and frame, inspect
  frame/sill for unremovable marks using raking/side light specifically
  (marks are easy to miss in flat daylight), circle any chip/crack found
  on the glass itself with a marker (grounds for full sash/glazing
  replacement, not just cosmetic touch-up), verify plumb/level with a
  bubble level (windowsill should be level horizontally; a forward tilt on
  the vertical axis is treated as normal/intentional for drainage),
  confirm opening sashes move and adjust freely. Weld splatter and angle-
  grinder sparks are cited as a specific, avoidable cause of window
  damage during the broader construction process.
- **Radiators/convectors**: visual check for chips, paint damage, and any
  substance that could damage the finish; check joints/connections for
  water staining; confirm wall-mounting bracket is level; for a convector
  specifically, the most common defect cited is a bent decorative grille.
- **Plumbing/sanitary fixtures**: developer-supplied fixtures are expected
  to be fully replaced during renovation except the water meters, which
  can be kept; visual check is mainly to catch a hidden pre-existing
  failure (the speaker cites a real case of a cracked sewer stack
  concealed under tape) rather than to evaluate finish quality. Toilet
  bowl condition is worth checking specifically because an intact one can
  be reused as a construction-phase toilet, cleaned, and resold afterward.
