## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's minimum foam-joint thicknesses** (measured at the joint's thinnest point, since walls aren't always parallel to the frame): sides ≥20mm, top ≥30mm; bottom 15mm if the frame sits on pre-built block masonry, or 35-40mm if on a standard building parapet.
- **Never add jamb-extension/expander profiles around the frame perimeter, per Zemskov** — his reasoning: it's an unreinforced plastic-to-plastic joint with minimal rigidity and poor thermal resistance; modern insulation (~1cm) + furring (~2cm) + finish panel (~8mm) plus the frame's own 50mm width already fit within a normal foam-joint gap without needing an extender.
- **Zemskov's glazing quality floor**: 70mm (7cm) frame profile minimum; double-glazing unit ≥40mm thick; gaskets that stay soft/elastic long-term (not just when new — he demonstrates a ~1-year-old developer-standard gasket already brittle "like dried wood"); adjustable, robust hardware with a minimum of 5 strike/keep points per frame, secured firmly enough to resist a forced-entry attempt from outside.
- **Zemskov's sash-minimization economics**: a sales rep's commission scales with order price, creating a structural incentive to sell more opening sashes than needed — his own rule: minimize the number of opening sashes, since (a) moving parts eventually fail, (b) hardware (especially good hardware) costs real money per sash, and (c) an opening sash reduces the actual light-transmitting glass area versus a fixed pane.
- **Zemskov's sectioning rule for a long glazing run**: divide total length by an optimal single-section width of 65-75cm; **never fabricate/transport/install a single unit longer than ~3m** — a 6m run must be split into at least 2 units (3m each), and on a high floor with a narrow stairwell, into 3 smaller units instead, since an oversized unit may not physically fit up the stairs and hauling a large frame up on ropes at height is a real risk Zemskov explicitly warns against.
- **Zemskov's worked 6m/8-section sash-configuration examples**: (1) with the end sections built out as opaque sandwich-panel infill (e.g. behind built-in end cabinets), place just 2 turn-tilt sashes strategically for cross-ventilation — everything else fixed glass; (2) if all 8 sections must be transparent, the two end sections (which can't open — their trim would overlap the hinge zone) get fixed panes plus an adjacent turn/turn-tilt sash specifically so the end panes can still be reached for cleaning, with the middle kept mostly fixed and one central turn-tilt sash for ventilation. **General principle: minimize opening sashes to the smallest number that still allows full-length cleaning access and adequate cross-ventilation**, not more.
- **Zemskov's anchor-fixing rules**: the frame must resist both positive load (wind pushing in) and negative load (a heavy sash slamming/pulling on its hinges during a draft) — **use direct anchor fixing as the default**; thin flexible mounting plates are for preliminary alignment only, never as the final fixing (he explicitly tells a client to have installers redo the work if he sees only mounting plates used). **Anchor embedment**: ≥40mm into concrete, ≥60mm into brick; fastener length must pass through the frame, the foam joint, and the first masonry course, then ≥20mm into the second course. **Support blocks** (опорные колодки) go under every vertical post, plus mid-span if a section exceeds 90cm — **must be polymer/plastic shims, never wood** (he explicitly rejects an old-GOST wood-shim allowance as outdated/misapplied).
- **Zemskov's frame-alignment rule — the single highest-value technical detail in this source**: install the frame so its **inner face aligns flush with the parapet's inner plane**, not the outer plane. Aligning to the outer plane (which he says most installers default to, for unclear reasons) creates a cold bridge at the sill/reveal junction that no amount of later insulation fixes. Aligning to the inner plane instead pushes the weak point to the *outer* side, where the subsequent insulation layer covers and neutralizes it — producing a properly insulated junction that won't frost.
- **Zemskov's post-glazing, pre-insulation sealing rule**: developer-standard sealing (a vilaterm foam-backer cord dabbed with some sealant, found in panel-building vertical/horizontal joints and brick-building ceiling joints) is, per Zemskov, cosmetic only — it doesn't stop cold-air infiltration (he demonstrates poking straight through to outside air with a finger). **Remove all of this fake sealing and refill generously with professional spray foam** — he states not to worry about foam expansion deforming brick/concrete at this scale.
- **Zemskov's AC line-routing procedure, given step by step**: determine AC unit position first (referencing other videos on that topic); route refrigerant lines, drain line, and power cable as one flat bundle/"sleeve" (шлейф) hugging the wall, not a loose round bundle — a flat bundle needs less clearance under the trim panel and won't intrude into the glazing plane. **Determine which of the two adjoining walls (loggia-side vs. apartment-side) protrudes further** using a straightedge held against both faces and a square, then measured with a tape — drill from the side that protrudes more, since drilling from the wrong side misses the target entirely. Drill 4 holes (2 for refrigerant lines, 1 drain, 1 power cable), each sloped for condensate drainage — either by angling the drill itself precisely enough to land the hole at the wall corner, or by first chiseling a shaped brick recess ~10-15cm deep to guide a gentler bend, correcting any drilling error afterward with a rotary hammer once the actual exit point is confirmed.
