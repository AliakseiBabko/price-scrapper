## Planning Rules — method, and this is the part that generalises

- **⚠️ The Neufert caveat, and the single most useful methodological item in the source.** The ergonomic figures everyone googles come from **Нойферт (Neufert)** — first published 1959, now in its 41st edition, still valid because "размеры наших ног, плечей и так далее не сильно изменились." **But those published figures are the minimum tolerable, not the comfortable.** Her prescription: **add 10%, better 15%, better 20%** to a Neufert minimum to get a dimension that is genuinely comfortable.
- **⚠️ The one number that covers most cases: 1 m to 1.2 m.** Enough to pull a chair back from a table and pass behind it; enough for a corridor. "Если есть метр или метр двадцать, то обычно это оно."
- **Where two sources disagree on a figure, take the larger.**
- **⚠️ Planning order: tasks → constraints → furniture.** People start from "we need a three-seat or corner sofa, where does it go" — a good question asked at the wrong point. Tasks means zoning and **room assignment**: which part of a single-window room is kitchen and which is living; what the second room of a two-room flat actually is (a living room with a sleeping place, or the son's room).
- **⚠️ The verb-list method — her central technique.** Instead of starting from furniture, **write down the verbs: what you actually do in this room.** Her bedroom list: sleep, make love, read, use a tablet, do live streams, watch TV, put on makeup, drink coffee in bed, do sit-ups.
  - **Then filter the list twice — what you do now, versus what you want to do later.** Her worked example: you drink coffee in bed not because you enjoy it but because the kitchen table seats one and your husband gets there first — **that is a process to design *out*, not to design around.** Conversely there are things absent now that you want (lying and staring at the ceiling because it relaxes you).
  - **Then decompose each verb into requirements.** Intimacy needs dimmed light — is a sconce enough, does it need a dimmer, should the light be diffuse or low at ankle level? Sleep may need blackout (a client whose children woke at 5am without it) and silence — **and in a three-room-plus flat that becomes the criterion for choosing which room is the bedroom.**
  - **Sightline analysis per verb**: for a live stream, what is in frame behind you, is the door visible, can a partner cross the room. She cites the BBC interview interrupted by a child as the failure case.
- **⚠️ "In one evening you can only make a very bad layout."** Her explicit rebuttal of the "plan your flat in one evening" genre — such a layout is furniture scattered without any thought about the occupants' actual needs.
- **⚠️ "Layout is not about an изюминка."** Second rebuttal, aimed at people hunting for a special twist on 10 m²: **don't reinvent the wheel unless you are a strong architect. Layout is the logic and functionality of space; the twist comes from textiles, decor, or interesting furniture shapes.**

## Planning Rules — constraints, and a Russian-jurisdiction set

- **⚠️ Habitable rooms are limited by window count, not by area (RU norm).** A 100 m² flat with three windows cannot be a four-room flat, because **a habitable room by norm requires a window** — so you get a kitchen or kitchen-living plus two rooms, and the surplus area has to become dressing rooms or a larger bathroom. She notes people do stuff beds into windowless niches, but "жилая площадь без окна как бы запрещена." (RU jurisdiction.)
- **Wet zones are essentially immovable unless you are on the first or last floor (RU)** — a small expansion at the expense of the corridor is permitted, no more. Kitchens likewise only at the expense of the corridor, and **a gas kitchen cannot be moved at all** and must be an enclosed, separated room. (RU jurisdiction.)
- **⚠️ Entrance-door swing direction is legally constrained (RU)**: where your door adjoins a neighbour's, your door must not be able to block the neighbour's exit — the stated scenario is a fire evacuation where your door is jammed or left open. (RU jurisdiction.)
- **The entrance door cannot be moved at all**, because the landing is common property. Her worked frustration: the classic narrow entry opening onto a long corridor that dead-ends at the bathroom door, where the pier is under 60 cm so no wardrobe fits — **and moving the front door 15 cm would solve it, and you may not.**
- **Load-bearing walls**: an opening is sometimes possible, removal and relocation never; and even the opening is not always permitted. Avoid touching them if you can.
- **Pylons («пилоны») are worse than a load-bearing wall in her view** — a large square column carrying the slab, which developers present as a small square on the plan with partitions drawn into it. The whole problem is hiding it.
- **Radiator relocation is a real constraint on annexing a loggia.** Removing the under-window block leaves the radiator with nowhere to go if there is no pier left or right of the new opening, and an in-floor convector is "тоже не всегда возможно."
- **⚠️ Riser distance is a plumbing constraint on the fashionable "kitchen niche" in a corridor.** Even where the move is approved, the drain run grows from ~0.5 m to ~3 m — **it clogs more and is harder to rod out.** The closer the WC and sink sit to the risers, the better.
- **Use standard furniture sizes rather than fighting them** — a chair seat is ~45 cm, and there is no point commissioning a custom chair; a chair is a genuinely difficult object that not every workshop can make.
- **⚠️ Her rebuttal to shaving ergonomic minimums, a recurring client argument.** Clients reason: the corridor "should" be 1–1.2 m, but the door leaf is only 70 cm and we stand in it fine, so why 1.2 m — especially when the washing machine then won't fit in the bathroom. **Her answer: one step through a wall thickness at 70 cm is fine; ten steps down a narrow corridor is not — and the door into a 70 cm corridor will not open if its own leaf is 70 cm.**

## Planning Rules — walk-through rooms, and room shape

- **⚠️ She argues *for* walk-through rooms («проходные комнаты»), against universal client resistance** — "у меня не было ещё ни одного клиента, который бы сказал: давай мы сделаем побольше проходных комнат." **In a 100 m² flat a central corridor can consume ~15 m²** — an entire room's worth of area that exists only to let you avoid crossing a room. Accepting one walk-through room instead returns that area to living space.
  - **She attributes the resistance to two things**: a residual cultural fear of Soviet communal-flat «уплотнение», and the once-a-year visiting mother-in-law driving a permanent guest-room decision — "весь остальной год по этому поводу мы будем ходить по коридору."
- **⚠️ Room shape is a parameter that finishes and furniture cannot fix.** She sees clients trying to correct layout errors with finishes or rearrangement; sometimes possible, often not. Correct shapes are proper geometric figures — squares and rectangles, not triangles or trapezoids. A rectangle plus a bay («эркер») is fine; **modern architecture often makes the bay a "ship's prow" shape, which destroys the axes.**
- **⚠️ Why axes matter, stated as the mechanism**: they let you order the space immediately — where the centre is, where the largest object goes, where the pendant hangs, what a row of fittings centres on. **With an irregular room shape the centre is not obvious even when it can be computed geometrically.**
- **Lay in symmetry first, then break it.** Once the axis exists it is easy to add asymmetric moments — one armchair larger, a side table one side and a chest the other — and the space still reads as correct. A purely symmetrical living room can look "скучновато," which is what the asymmetric moments fix.
- **Axes also rescue over-saturated interiors** — heavy ornament, many details, carving. The axis "не даёт этому интерьеру рассыпаться на куски." She cites a Kelly Hoppen bedroom that reads as laconic but has complex ceiling geometry, all resolved on the axis.
- **⚠️ Fully asymmetric layouts are much harder, and she advises against them for DIY** — "не каждый дизайнер может это сделать хорошо." **Her reason is worth keeping: more options is not freedom, it is simply more choices, and it ends in worse-quality work.**
- **⚠️ Diagnostic for a plan that cannot be saved**: she analyses a Houzz plan where the central circulation zone is **14 m²** while the two children's rooms are **14 m² and 11 m²** — the circulation space is both larger than either habitable room and better-proportioned. **Verdict: the apartment did not match the brief and "дизайнер в этом случае бессилен."** If the pass-through zone out-measures and out-shapes the rooms, the problem is the flat.

## Hallway / Entrance

- **Developers minimise hallways for the same reason clients do** — "extra metres in the hallway or in the living room?" — and the consequence is that two children cannot be got ready for school simultaneously and there is nowhere to hang anything, while in practice guests are received in the kitchen anyway.
- **Her own project example, reported as a partial failure**: she tried to steal wardrobe depth from the guest WC and could not take it fully, because the washing machine door then would not open. The wardrobe corner still protrudes into the hallway — **but there is a wardrobe, against the developer's single hook.**
- **⚠️ A hallway is a pass-through zone, so you can be bolder there** — you do not spend enough time in it to tire of a strong move. Good place to state the style the guest will meet further in. Her example: in a soft-loft flat, do **hard** loft in the hallway specifically.
- **The hallway is largely for guests** — the first thing they see, "символ гостеприимства."
- **⚠️ The buffer-zone argument, which is her reason for requiring a mirror**: we play different social roles — the person who comes home is not the person who goes to work — and **the hallway is where the mask changes**, moving between the ordered, controlled world of the flat and the chaotic, sometimes aggressive world outside. **Therefore there must be a mirror.**
- **Tie the hallway to the world outside the front door.** In a Stalinka with a minimalist or loft interior, mix styles in the hallway specifically — place an Empire-style object that belongs to the building — "таким образом вы завязываете внешнюю и внутреннюю среду."
- **Provide a defined place for small items** — keys, phone — so the morning departure is not a search.
- **⚠️ The "выход на красоту" principle**: opening the front door should land the eye on art, a picture, a sculpture, or at minimum a mirror. **Caveat she volunteers**: feng shui says no mirror directly opposite the front door; she is not an adherent but agrees that specific placement is not great.
  - Same principle elsewhere: entering the living room you should see most of it, with wall decor or a picture over the sofa — better than facing a wardrobe.
  - **⚠️ From habitable rooms you should not be able to see the bathroom or the hallway** — the stated reason is realism, not theory: outerwear ends up on open hooks and shoes drip melting snow, and you do not want to sit on the sofa looking at it.
  - **Do not let the entry sightline land on the side panel of a wardrobe** or any other furniture — frontal comprehension reads better.
- **⚠️ A well-planned hallway makes it self-evident to a guest where to go.** She credits this to classical architecture rather than claiming it. The failure scenario she narrates: you are detained at the door by the management company arguing about ЖКХ charges, and the guest sidles past with no idea where to go — past a spouse in curlers, past a children's room. The hallway should route them to the living room.
- **Axes in a hallway are what make patterned floor-tile layouts work** — she likes carpet-like tile layouts, and they only succeed against a proper axis.
- **Where an added door to a dressing room or WC breaks the hallway's symmetry** and pushes a console or pouffe corner into the space, **"просто нужно скруглять"** — round it off; there is nothing else to be done.
- **⚠️ A planning answer to the courier problem**: an audience member said her kitchen is visible from the open hallway and she hates couriers seeing in. **Her answer is a layout one — arrange it so the courier's sightline lands on a composed vignette rather than into the flat.**

## Bedroom — dimensions and clearances

- **We spend around 30 years asleep**, and even a 28 m² studio has a sleeping place — a function that cannot be dropped.
- **⚠️ Bed size is not mattress size** — a distinction she flags as a common planning error. **For a 160×200 mattress the bed is roughly 180×210, and better planned at 180×220** — the side rail («царга») has width, the headboard occupies depth, and on comfortable beds the headboard is **angled back**, taking more still.
- **Nightstands must not butt the bed — leave 5–10 cm**, so a bedspread can be pulled off and bedding changed.
- **⚠️ Around the bed, Neufert's minimum is 70 cm — "это очень мало," better at least 1 m.** For a bed detached from the walls, **at least 70 cm each side**, and at that dimension you cannot form openings there; more is better.
- **Headboard height should not be half the wall height.** On a 3 m wall do not make it 1.5 m — go lower (~1.2 m) or higher (1.8 m+). Halving a wall visually reads badly. *(Compositional, not a planning dimension.)*
- **⚠️ Chest of drawers: allow the chest's depth plus 60 cm.** Her worked arithmetic: a 45 cm chest whose drawer pulls out 40 cm, given only 70 cm of clearance, leaves 30 cm for your knees — and the bottom drawer may need you to crouch. "Так нельзя, так не работает."
- **⚠️ Hinged wardrobe doors: leaf width plus 60 cm**, so you can step back rather than manoeuvring around open doors.
- **Standard Russian bed height is up to 40 cm** — explicitly contrasted with American beds.
- **American-height beds with a thick mattress**: she tested one in a showroom and reports that sitting on it is like a bar stool — at average height her feet do not reach the floor. **Excellent to lie on, poor to sit on — which is precisely why a bench or pouffe is needed to dress on.** It also takes the discarded duvet or clothes, and gives extra storage.

## Bedroom — wardrobe and bed position

- **The wardrobe usually lands on the wall adjacent to the door, and then "спорят две красоты."** The primary beauty is the headboard, so **the wardrobe must be muted — paint it the wall colour, and ideally build it in.**
- **⚠️ If built in, you drop the ceiling over it, and the common error is sizing that drop to the carcass alone.** A 60 cm wardrobe **plus a 10 cm cornice overhang is 70 cm** — the dropped ceiling and the niche must be set out to 70, or the cornice protrudes into the room.
- **Where spouses wake at different times, give the wardrobe zone its own lighting** so one can dress without waking the other.
- **A wardrobe on the same wall as the door is better** — it is not in view on entering.
- **⚠️ Never use a rounded corner module wardrobe** (the Soviet-style curved end used to soften a protruding corner): "это просто адски адский ад, это никогда не выглядит хорошо."
- **Plan the dead zone behind a nightstand set against a wardrobe** — that corner of the wardrobe is blind and should hold things not needed daily.
- **⚠️ Bed position rule: a person lying in bed should see whoever comes through the door** — so **do not put the headboard on the same wall as the door.** Given a choice of two layouts, this decides it.
- **Entering from the long side of a rectangle is easier** — a longer wardrobe fits, with no protruding corner to resolve.
- **Symmetrical wardrobes either side with a bridge over the bed**: the advantage is that everything is on one wall, so there is no competition between the wardrobe's appearance and the bed's. **Where nightstands do not fit, put small lit niches in the wardrobe ends** for a phone.
- **Bed in an alcove** — she likes it; some clients dislike sleeping enclosed by three walls, and it works better for one occupant than two.
- **Elongated bedrooms**: use the length in front of the bed — a desk (**then plan floor sockets**), a bench, or a deeper small sofa. If a desk disturbs sleep, separate it with a screen — **screens are unpopular in Russia and much used in America.** Alternatives: a niche in the wardrobe for a TV or art so you are not looking at wardrobe doors, or a reading corner with a floor lamp and armchairs.
- **A bed detached from the walls also needs something behind the headboard** for a book or a cup, since nightstands are not available in that arrangement.

## Kids Room — safety

- **Ideally no balcony**, or a fully blockable exit onto it.
- **Windows: tilt-opening («откидные»), not casement, and preferably key-lockable.**
- **No Roman blinds, and no blind or curtain with a cord** — the child will pull it, "и это не всегда заканчивается хорошо."
- **Doors with limiters and slow closing** so fingers are not trapped.
- **Sockets with child shutters («шторки»).**
- **Furniture must not be able to tip onto the child — fix it to the wall** where there is any risk. The child must not be able to fall out of bed.
- **Fewer hard surfaces**, rugs, corner protectors — scoped to small children.
- **⚠️ Lay the electrics for the *second* layout now.** With a one-year-old, design the age-7-or-10 layout at the same time and install those sockets during the renovation. **Then the later change is cosmetic only — re-paper, rearrange, buy new furniture — with no rewiring.** One of the highest-leverage items in the source.

## Kids Room — zoning and layout

- **Four zones for four activities.** Ideal is to screen the sleep zone so **the child cannot see the toys from the bed.**
- **The largest and lightest room is often given to the children** — treated as more necessary than the living room.
- **Play stations, each scaled to the child**: a small play kitchen, a shelf unit with books, a small drawing table — every activity with its own delimited zone and its own storage, so the child can orient and choose. **The mechanism is the child's own agency** — "мы сегодня будем готовить искусственную картошку или мы будем переодеваться в принцессу."
- **Favour problem-solving and creative toys (Lego) — and plan where they go.**
- **The child touches the interior, not only looks at it** — tactile variety is developmentally useful.
- **Shelves must be scaled to the child: the smaller the child, the lower the most interesting shelf.** Open shelves so a small child can reach without help.
- **Storage by category** — clothes, school books and toys held separately.
- **Do not throw away or hide the child's drawings.** She cites a recent Cosmoscow exhibition where half the show was adult artists deliberately painting like small children. It need not hang in the kids' room.
- **⚠️ Girls need larger beds than boys** — girls use the bed as a play surface and boys generally do not.
- **Provide space for an adult to sit beside the bed.**
- **⚠️ Do not buy themed beds — a Cinderella carriage, a car.** **Two reasons: they constrain imagination** (a plain bed can be a carriage, a house, a ship at sea; a car can only be a car), **and the child tires of it within days while it is an expensive object.** She reports clients doing it anyway.
- **A guest zone inside the kids' room** is a feature she rates: visiting children can shut themselves in and play unobserved, with somewhere to sit and plenty of free floor left.
- **Common failure: a sofa competing with a wardrobe**, leaving a dead zone behind sliding doors.
- **Bed set head-to-wall rather than along a wall is a poor option unless there is a separate playroom** — it consumes floor area and the child must constantly walk round it.
- **⚠️ Better: bed in a corner — and her stated logic is light-based. The darkest corner goes to the bed; the lightest part goes to study.**
- **Custom joinery is what makes a small kids' room work** — she highlights shelves worked from both sides, serving the study zone and the bed zone at once.
- **Two children without bunk beds** reduces the room to a bedroom with a workspace, and nothing else fits.
- **⚠️ Podium beds — a plan she abandoned, and the reasons are the value.** Raise a podium 30–40 cm with beds rolling out beneath. She dropped it: **very hard to execute, few joinery shops can make a good pull-out-from-podium bed, and it depends on windowsill height** — the child must not be able to go out of the window from the raised level.
- **⚠️ Bunk beds — the lighting must be worked out deliberately, and she tested the failure herself.** The sconce must be reachable from the bed, **and there must be a two-way switch by the door.** Without it, a child who falls asleep with the sconce on in the top bunk means a parent climbing the ladder past a sleeping child — **"я кстати пробовала, я реально сама просто на этой лестнице не помещаюсь."**

## Q&A — the numbers the questions forced out

- **⚠️ Dressing room, absolute minimum depth when entered from the end.** Asked whether a 60 cm wardrobe plus 60 cm to turn in is enough: **no.** Reaching a top shelf you occupy more space and need a step back. **And the opening has to be resolved**: the smallest door leaf is 60 cm → **the opening must be 72 cm, plus at least 10 cm around it for architraves = 82 cm; plus 60 cm of wardrobe = 142 cm.** She adds that she does not personally use 60 cm leaves.
  - **⚠️ Her alternative when that depth is unavailable — enter from the long side and treat it as a very deep wardrobe**: one step in, large sliding doors, free space behind you to step back into, **and two "wings" at the sides for the vacuum cleaner and the ironing board.**
- **Cutting a dressing room off an existing room**: **minimum 60 cm for the wardrobe zone plus ideally 1 m in front, plus ~10 cm of partition = 1.7–1.8 m** to take. **Do it to an elongated room ("как вагон метро"), not to a good square one** — "лучше я бы не рекомендовала его портить."
- **⚠️ Podium height for a pull-out bed**: standard Russian bed height is up to 40 cm; **allow at least 10 cm for the frame and cladding, so the podium lands around 50 cm** — but confirm with the joiner, since it depends on the pull-out mechanism. **Lower is better**: more headroom on the podium and more clearance below the windowsill.
- **⚠️ A 2.70 × 4.70 m bedroom — and she says openly that she cannot solve it.** At 2.70 m, a bed 210–220 deep leaves ~50 cm to squeeze past. Options offered: **a bed built into a full wall-to-wall podium** with the mattress set in (a Japanese arrangement, not necessarily a Japanese style), moving the sleeping zone to the window — **with the radiator position calculated so it does not interfere with sleep**; or stealing a niche from the adjacent room along the long wall, with the obvious question of what that does to the other room; or two single beds along the 4.70 m wall, at the cost of sleeping separately. **"Я бы рада была помочь, но я просто не знаю, как сделать так, чтобы из 2,70 было бы 3,70."** Recorded because a practitioner declaring a constraint unsolvable is more useful than an invented fix.
- **⚠️ Kitchen worktop height, and split-level worktops.** She has seen multi-level kitchens (hob lower, sink higher) and **would not get carried away**: on a kitchen up to ~3 m long she would not do it, because **the corner between the two levels becomes a dead zone** where you cannot work next to the sink. On height generally: **standard bases with worktop are 84–85 cm and do not suit everyone** — she recently made a pair of very tall clients a worktop over 90 cm, tried it herself, and confirms it is genuinely better. **Her test: in the shop, put your hands on a worktop and see whether you would raise or lower it.**
- **Basin depth and splashing**: deeper genuinely does splash less (more slope), **but it depends on the mixer as well as the basin** — some mixers make it hard to get your hands under a shallow basin. **Given a choice, take the deeper one.** A guest WC basin of ~20 cm depth splashes badly; she had a project where a larger basin turned out not to be fittable, and notes you have to listen carefully to what the showroom says will actually fit.
- **⚠️ Colour in small apartments — she pushes back on the premise.** "Светлая комната всегда выглядит более просторной" is **"скорее клише"** and depends on what you put in the room. **Saturated colours do eat space — that is true. But dark rooms often look very good, and enlarging a small room is not always the right goal** — specifically, **pass-through zones like a hallway or corridor are better in more saturated or darker tones.** She did an almost-black bedroom of ~11 m² and rates it highly; her own 9 m² room has blue wallpaper, dark curtains and graphite carpet and reads as a lounge.
  - **Colour only oppresses when it is uniform** — a single green everywhere becomes unbearable; muted grey-greens balanced with a light sofa and touches of blue or red do not.
  - **⚠️ Buy paints with complex pigments — good European, American or English brands rather than the cheapest.** Their advantage is that the same paint reads differently under different light, cooler by day and warmer in the evening. Her analogy: alexandrite.
  - **If you did want to enlarge: mirrors.** If a dark scheme palls, change the textiles for lighter patches — **and art reads very well on a dark ground.**
- **DIY layout software**: Sweet Home 3D, Homestyler and others, some fairly professional. She tried Sweet Home, played with it, and went back to AutoCAD. Notes that men with engineering backgrounds frequently arrive at consultations with a 3D layout already made.
- **⚠️ Workspace for an older child.** Often placed at the window, **especially in an elongated room, because it makes the room read shorter.** Her favourite detail: **custom shelves left and right of the desk turned to face the worktop rather than frontally**, so things can be stacked against them; combine orientations across the run. **And plan for the equipment** — a client's solution she rates: **a printer hidden inside a desk drawer with its own socket inside and no visible cable hole**; pull the drawer out and take the sheet straight from the printer.

## Design Concept / Furniture — storage capacity is bounded by retrievability

- **⚠️ A drawer is full before it is full.** From lagom decluttering literature: professional organisers put **two large frying pans into a 90 cm drawer and told the client the drawer was full**, though there was visible space above. **The mechanism: there is a limit to how much can be comfortably retrieved.** Fill the remaining volume and you must unload everything to reach the bottom. **Capacity is set by access, not by cubic volume** — and she uses this as the analogy for the whole flat being bounded by its configuration.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- **Sizing a dropped ceiling over a built-in wardrobe to the carcass depth and forgetting the cornice overhang** (60 + 10 = 70).
- **Putting the headboard on the door wall.**
- **Rounded corner-module wardrobes.**
- **Themed children's beds.**
- **Bunk beds with no two-way switch at the door.**
- **Shaving corridor width to the door-leaf dimension.**
- **Moving a kitchen sink far from the riser for a "kitchen niche"** — approval is not the binding problem, the 3 m drain run is.
- **Split-level worktops in a kitchen under ~3 m** — dead corner between the levels.
- **Making a layout "in one evening," and hunting for an изюминка in the layout rather than in the furnishings.**
