## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Flooring board/plank layout direction changes a corridor's apparent
  proportions**: laying boards lengthwise (parallel to the long walls)
  makes the corridor read as longer and narrower, because a wide "edge
  border" strip runs along both long walls; laying boards crosswise
  (perpendicular to the long walls) makes the same corridor read as
  wider. Neither is objectively correct — the designer frames this as a
  deliberate choice based on the visual effect wanted, not a right/wrong
  rule.
- **Large-scale flooring/tile patterns make a space read as more cramped
  and compact; small-scale patterns make the same space read as more
  open** — an independent lever from board-direction above, stackable with
  it.
- **Vertical lines/elements visually raise ceiling height; horizontal
  lines visually shorten wall height and widen the room** — horizontal
  lines (paint bands, wall panels, moldings) are therefore generally
  better suited to a narrow corridor than vertical ones.
- **A dark accent wall at the far end of a long corridor visually
  shortens its perceived length.**
- **A dark ceiling reads as lower than a light one**, avoiding a
  "well/shaft" feeling in an over-tall corridor. **If ceiling height
  exceeds ~3m, painting the ceiling dark alone isn't sufficient** to
  rebalance the room's proportions — extend the dark color partway down
  the adjoining walls (a color band starting at the ceiling) to further
  compress apparent height while also adding apparent width.
- **Light-fixture mounting height reinforces (or undermines) the dark-
  ceiling illusion**: recessed spotlights keep the visible light source
  high (weakest effect); surface-mounted/flush fixtures lower the visible
  light source (stronger effect); pendant/hanging fixtures lower it
  furthest, maximally distracting the eye from the room's real ceiling
  height. A concrete, stackable technique alongside the dark-ceiling/
  dark-band trick above — low-hanging chandeliers were specifically noted
  as putting the upper wall/ceiling into shadow, hiding real height
  almost entirely in one real example shown.
- **To visually shorten an over-long corridor, color-block it into
  distinct zones** rather than treating it as one uniform run — options
  demonstrated: (a) paint just the end wall dark, as above; (b) extend
  that end-wall color onto an adjoining surface (e.g. the ceiling near it)
  to visually "cut off" a segment of the corridor; (c) align a color
  division with a genuine architectural feature (e.g. paint the section
  of corridor that has a locally lower ceiling in a different color, to
  mark it as its own sub-zone) rather than an arbitrary point.
- **Real budget-renovation case (a friend's apartment, currently
  in-progress at filming time, importsubstitution-driven material
  choices due to budget constraints)**: a structural load-bearing beam
  that could not be removed became the natural physical boundary for a
  color transition from the entryway's paint color to the kitchen's own
  accent-wall color — a concrete example of using an unavoidable
  structural constraint as the color-block boundary rather than fighting
  it. Region unresolved (no city named); real project but `single-
  account`, in-progress at time of filming (no final cost/outcome yet).
- **Create a focal point directly in the corridor's forward sightline**
  (an accent wall, artwork, or object grouping straight ahead as you walk
  down it) so the eye isn't drawn to a "boring dead-end wall" — the
  designer's stated reasoning: people pay much less visual attention to
  side walls while moving through a pass-through corridor than to
  whatever sits directly ahead, so the far/end wall's treatment matters
  disproportionately more than the side walls'.
- **When a corridor segment sits directly opposite an always-open (no
  door) living-room entrance, treat that segment as effectively part of
  the living room's own interior** — in the cited real project, artwork
  placed opposite the living-room doorway shares the living room's own
  decorative-lighting circuit/switch (one button triggers both the living
  room's accent lighting and the corridor artwork lighting), rather than
  getting a separate switch. **General corridor-lighting rule stated
  explicitly**: don't give corridor accent/picture lighting its own
  switch by default, since a corridor is a pass-through zone — combine it
  with the main ceiling-light switch (with the doorless-adjoining-room
  exception above as a specific variant of that same "don't over-switch a
  pass-through zone" principle).
- **Avoid large-format artwork/photos in a narrow corridor** — appreciating
  a large piece requires standing back at a real distance, which a narrow
  corridor doesn't allow; prefer small-format artwork/photography instead.
- **Mirror orientation must be chosen deliberately relative to the
  direction you want to expand**, not defaulted to "any mirror enlarges
  the room" — a full-length mirror on a corridor's long wall would expand
  its *length* (an already-long corridor reading as even longer/near-
  infinite), which may not be the desired effect; orient/place a mirror
  to expand width instead if length is already the corridor's problem, not
  its asset.

## Furniture / Built-ins — Durable Facts & Rules

- **A piece of furniture (console, chest of drawers) that sits below the
  height of a person's extended-arm fingertips reads as leaving the space
  "free," even though it occupies the same footprint as a taller piece** —
  the designer's stated mechanism: people perceive their immediate personal
  space at roughly arm's-length height; furniture below that height line
  doesn't register as encroaching on it, so a low console/tumba in a
  narrow corridor feels roomier than a standard-height chest of drawers of
  the same depth. Cross-referenced by the designer to her own earlier
  small-apartment-rules video (not separately fetched in this pass).
- **A wall-mounted (floating/hanging) shoe cabinet, or one on tall legs,
  reads as more spacious than a floor-standing shoe cabinet of the same
  capacity** — stated mechanism: "the more visible floor, the more
  spacious the room feels," an explicit general principle distinct from
  (but complementary to) the arm's-length furniture-height rule above.
- **Shallow-depth furniture suits a corridor's limited depth by
  necessity, and bookshelf storage specifically is well-suited to it** —
  a mixed approach (floor-standing shelf sections near the entry,
  wall-hung sections further down the same run) keeps progressively more
  floor visible as the corridor continues, compounding the spaciousness
  effect above.
- **A wall-hung (not floor-standing) storage cabinet is specifically
  practical for a corridor's "wet zone"** (storing outdoor gear that's
  wet/damp on arrival — the designer's example: an umbrella) since it
  keeps the floor itself dry and easy to clean underneath; pairing this
  zone with a heated floor speeds drying further (a design combination,
  not just a single-feature recommendation) — corroborates this batch's
  existing heated-floor-for-drying theme from the INMYROOM TV sources,
  applied specifically to a hung-storage-plus-heated-floor combination
  rather than an open floor area.
