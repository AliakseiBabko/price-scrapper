## Materials / Equipment — Mechanized Putty Toolkit

1. **Equipment list, function-labeled**: a putty-spraying machine
   (doubles as a primer and paint sprayer); a **two-whisk mixer**
   ("двухвенчиковый миксер"), stated to mix putty more thoroughly than a
   single-whisk ("жираф") mixer; a vacuum-attached sander for dustless
   sanding; a dedicated work lamp (called an essential, not optional,
   accessory — matches this project's existing raking-light QC-lamp
   convention but framed here as a *working* tool, not just an
   inspection tool); and a set of specialized putty trowels/spatulas in
   several widths (40/60/80 cm — 80 cm preferred as the main size
   specifically because it reduces arm fatigue over a full shift; 40/60
   cm reserved for detail work).

## Rules / Heuristics — Application Sequence and QC

2. **Pre-putty surface prep**: sand the plastered surface first
   (removes small debris/crumbs left by the plaster and knocks down
   minor high spots) using a coarse-grit disc with a vacuum attached for
   dustless sanding, then prime — the sprayer reaches close enough to
   corners that a separate manual corner-priming pass usually isn't
   needed.
3. **Putty batch rest/maturation time, per manufacturer spec**: the
   plaster-based putty ("гипсовая шпаклёвка") used here needs roughly
   **3 hours** of rest after mixing before application, per the
   manufacturer's own instructions — mixed in large batches (example: 4
   bags at once) to keep the sprayer continuously fed.
4. **Day-split room sequencing to protect fresh corners**: putty **two
   walls of a room on day one, the remaining two walls the next day** —
   not because of drying time on the finished walls, but specifically
   because working an adjacent wall while a shared corner from the
   just-finished wall is still soft/uncured risks damaging that corner.
   A genuinely new corner-protection sequencing rule, distinct from this
   project's existing corner-QC content (which covers *inspecting* a
   finished corner, not *sequencing* work to protect an unfinished one).
5. **Two distinct sander types, an explicit speed-vs-finish tradeoff**:
   an **orbital sander** (disc rotates directly) removes material fast —
   good for leveling base plaster/rough putty — but is less delicate; an
   **eccentric/random-orbit sander** (a smaller, free-rotating 4mm
   stroke) gives a more delicate, refined finish but at lower
   productivity. One named unit has a built-in LED ring light around the
   disc (a genuine convenience for seeing the work surface while
   sanding) at a higher price point — flagged as a real but optional
   upgrade. **Corners still require manual/hand sanding either way** —
   neither machine reaches into a corner.
6. **Two distinct finish paths after sanding+priming, by planned
   covering**: for **wallpaper**, sand → prime (twice) → done, ready for
   paste. For **paint**, an additional stage is required: apply
   fiberglass scrim ("стеклохолст") onto the putty or a dedicated
   adhesive, then 2-3 further coats of finishing putty, sand and prime
   again, repeating the putty/sand/prime cycle as needed until the
   surface is paint-ready — a materially longer path than the
   wallpaper-bound one. Consistent with, and slightly more granular
   than, this project's existing general stekloholst-for-paint content.

## Numeric Data — Speed Benchmark and Crew Composition

7. **Real quantified machine-vs-hand speed comparison, ~70 m² object**:
   first putty coat took 2 days, second coat 2 days, sanding 2 days —
   **6 total working days with the mechanized method**, versus an
   estimated **minimum ~2 weeks (14 days) if done entirely by hand** —
   roughly a 2-3x speed multiplier, consistent with the narrator's own
   stated "two to three times faster" framing. Machine work is also
   described as physically less exhausting than the equivalent hand
   work.
8. **Two-person crew composition and fatigue-based task rotation**: one
   worker runs the spray machine and does the trowel-finishing pass
   (physically tiring during the 2-day application stage), the other
   handles sanding once application is done (tiring during the 2-day
   sanding stage) — the two stages' fatigue loads are staggered so each
   worker gets a lighter stretch while the other is in their hard phase.
   A third master is explicitly called "too many" for a job this size
   (not enough volume to keep three people usefully occupied); an
   additional unskilled helper (tool-washing, minor cleanup) is useful
   *if* available, but not treated as a standard crew requirement.
9. **Trade-specialization speed/quality rationale**: staying within one
   narrow trade (putty/finishing specifically, rather than also taking
   on ceiling work or other trades) avoids the re-learning/tool-
   changeover overhead of switching between different work types, and
   repeat-project experience within the same narrow trade compounds into
   real quality gains (knowing the specific nuances/gotchas of just this
   one task) — offered as the practitioner's own explanation for why
   staying specialized, not generalizing, increases both speed and
   quality over time.
