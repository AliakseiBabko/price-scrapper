## Entrance / Hallway — Durable Facts & Rules (the core case)

- **A prior owner relocated the kitchen out of its original zone into the
  corridor/hallway**, splitting what was originally a studio-style layout
  into two separate rooms — the current owner bought the apartment
  already in this configuration (at a price matching a standard one-room
  unit, despite the listing describing "two rooms," which she suspected
  was a "catch" and then found unusually appealing rather than a defect)
  because she was "used to every square meter being used productively and
  having no true 'dead' corridors." A concrete real-world example of a
  household **deliberately accepting** a kitchen-in-corridor layout as a
  value trade rather than a compromise forced on them.
- **This household's own retrospective critique of the layout, stated
  directly, is the single most load-bearing planning lesson in this
  source**: "if I designed this apartment myself, I would put a proper
  entry storage/closet zone *first*, then the kitchen zone — so you walk
  in, hang up your things, and *then* proceed further into the apartment."
  As built, the kitchen sits immediately at the entrance with **no room
  for more than one person to remove shoes/outerwear at a time** — with 3
  household members, they take turns dressing/undressing at the door
  sequentially. **General planning principle extractable from this**:
  when a kitchen (or any function requiring bending/standing/prep space)
  is placed directly at the entry, verify there's still a genuine
  shoe-off/coat-off transition zone ahead of it — don't let the kitchen
  function crowd out the basic entry-transition function entirely.
- **The "dirty entry zone right next to food prep" concern, tested against
  5 years of real living**: the household reports this has never actually
  been a problem for them, attributing it to their own lifestyle (they
  don't track in much dirt/mud). Recorded as a real counter-example to the
  intuitive worry that kitchen-at-entry is inherently unhygienic — but
  explicitly **lifestyle-dependent**, not a general reassurance; a
  household with more mud/snow tracking, kids, or pets might have a
  different real outcome.
- **IKEA bathroom wall cabinets (15cm depth) repurposed as slim open
  kitchen wall storage** for small items (glasses, cups, tea/coffee,
  spices) in a space too narrow for standard-depth kitchen wall cabinets —
  a genuinely reusable technique: check a shallow-depth product line
  (bathroom cabinets) as an alternative to a kitchen line specifically
  when depth, not width, is the binding constraint. Directly relevant to
  a hallway/entry-adjacent kitchen (or any narrow zone) needing shallow
  storage without a bulky footprint.

## Furniture / Built-ins & Kitchen Appliances — Durable Facts & Rules

- **A narrow (45cm-wide) kitchen island cabinet built specifically to
  house a built-in oven**, leaving more countertop width (80cm) for
  comfortable seating at the island and keeping the walkway clear — a
  concrete, dimensioned real build (standard 45cm cabinet carcass fitted
  with the oven, closed with a countertop on one side and a false panel
  on the other). Real DIY execution: designed by the homeowner herself,
  built by her spouse.
- **Compact narrow-cabinet storage repurposing**: a planned pull-out
  bottle rack in a narrow cabinet was abandoned in favor of simply storing
  large (10L) water jugs there instead — another real example (see this
  batch's earlier "temporary solution kept because it worked better"
  theme) of the as-built improvisation beating the originally planned
  fixture.
- **Two-burner cooktop placed with its long edge against a wall (rather
  than along the countertop's open front edge), directly under an angled
  (not vertical) range hood — a real, ongoing usability complaint**: large
  pots/pans touch the wall panel behind the cooktop, radiate heat onto it,
  and steam escapes sideways past the angled hood rather than rising
  into it. **Stated as a specific planning caution learned only after the
  fact** ("I wish I'd known to position the cooktop along the countertop's
  front edge, not against a wall, before installing this") — a concrete,
  checkable rule for any narrow/compact kitchen: cooktop orientation
  relative to the hood matters as much as the hood's own spec.
- **A bathroom wall panel (Leroy Merlin, bathroom department) substituted
  for a proper kitchen backsplash/apron behind the cooktop** — cheaper,
  glossy, wipeable, wide color range; **5 years of direct cooking-heat
  exposure with no observed warping/swelling**, a real durability track
  record for this cross-category material substitution (though the guest
  interviewer voices some independent skepticism about long-term heat
  tolerance despite the positive 5-year result — flagged as `single-
  account`, not independently lab-verified).
- **A multicooker placed too close to an active cooktop melted its own
  base/feet** when the cooktop was switched on unintentionally — a real
  safety incident. The household's stated mitigation is **unplugging the
  multicooker from its outlet whenever the cooktop is in use**, rather
  than relocating it, since a steamer is their main daily cooking
  appliance and the multicooker only gets plugged in occasionally. A
  concrete counter-currency mistakes/warnings entry: verify small
  countertop appliances aren't sited within heat/splash range of an
  active cooktop, especially in a tight compact-kitchen footprint.
- **Compact ("tabletop") dishwasher chosen deliberately (this household's
  third one) for a family of three**, explicitly **not** as a fallback
  compromise but because its capacity matches their actual daily fill
  rate (they run it nightly rather than waiting 2 days to fill a
  full-size unit) — real capacity example: 2 large pots, food-storage
  containers, several cups/plates fit per run. A useful counter-example to
  an assumption that full-size is always necessary — sufficiency depends
  on household size/usage cadence, not dwelling size alone.
- **No drawers at all in this kitchen** (a real, acknowledged design
  compromise from the tight footprint) — even storing cutlery is
  described as inconvenient. Flagged as a caution: verify a compact-
  kitchen design retains at least one functional drawer (cutlery at
  minimum) rather than optimizing it away entirely for cabinet/counter
  space.
- **Kitchen furniture cost data (Moscow, Leroy Merlin catalog line, not
  independently confirmed as Moscow-specific pricing vs. national
  catalog)**: full kitchen furniture set (cabinets, countertop, one wall
  cabinet, false panel; **excluding appliances**) purchased ~2018 (5 years
  before this 2023 video) for **51,000 RUB**; the same/equivalent current
  line (facades redesigned since) now costs roughly **~100,000 RUB** —
  roughly doubled over 5 years. A specific SKU-level data point: a 240cm
  countertop model ("Рашель"/similar name, ASR-uncertain) cost **5,500
  RUB** at purchase, **~11,000 RUB** at time of filming — also roughly
  doubled. Both figures are catalog/brand prices, not an independent
  market survey — flagged accordingly, but genuinely useful as a 5-year
  inflation data point for budget-tier kitchen furniture specifically.
- **Whole-apartment furnishing-only budget (explicitly distinct from a
  full renovation budget)**: ~**600,000 RUB** total to furnish/equip the
  entire apartment (kitchen furniture, appliances, beds) — **the
  household explicitly did not do structural/finish renovation work**
  (floors/ceilings were inherited as-is from the prior owner, who had
  already done the kitchen-relocation replan) — this figure is a
  furnishing/equipping cost only, must not be cited as a comparable
  renovation-cost benchmark against this store's other turnkey/self-
  managed construction-cost figures.

## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules (secondary content, briefly noted)

- **A partition/divider improvised from vintage secondhand French-style
  glazed pocket/sliding doors**, bought via a classifieds listing (Avito)
  requiring the buyer to dismantle and transport them personally — real
  cost: ~10,000 RUB purchase + ~17,000 RUB total including
  dismantling/transport. Deliberately chosen "aged" (20+ years old,
  visibly worn) to match the rest of the apartment's secondhand/vintage
  aesthetic rather than looking new/glossy — **and reported soundproofing
  performance is genuinely good** (thick veneered solid-wood leaves with
  overlap, plus curtains hung over them): one partner can sleep while the
  other watches TV loudly enough that dialogue is inaudible but not
  disruptive. A real, specific soundproofing data point for a
  secondhand-solid-wood-doors-plus-curtain partition approach, distinct
  from a purpose-built soundproof wall.
- **A recent (1-month-old at filming) DIY replan**: gave the apartment's
  original small bedroom to a child once they outgrew a crib, and
  converted part of the original ~20m² living room (an awkward ~6-8m
  long × 3m proportion) into a private adult sleeping zone via a
  mid-room partition — corroborates this batch's existing theme (from
  the Anuta Vlady source) that an unusually long/narrow room needs a
  structural division, not just furniture-perimeter arrangement, to read
  as usable. **Honest, unresolved caveat volunteered by the household**:
  they hadn't yet tested how the new sleeping zone's window/curtain setup
  handles bright Moscow summer mornings, since the partition was only a
  month old — a good example of a source reporting a real limitation
  rather than claiming an untested solution works.
- Loggia/balcony annexation to a child's room, cited as "the best
  decision in this apartment," gaining >3m² of usable space —
  corroborates this project's existing loggia-annexation value theme from
  a different room.
- A minimal-consumption/decluttering household philosophy (deliberately
  limiting clothing volume) meant a single shared closet sufficed for
  the whole apartment's clothing storage — an alternative model to
  "always build maximum storage capacity," contingent on the household's
  own consumption habits rather than a layout constraint.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- **Explicitly self-identified "biggest mistake"**: installed a
  kitchen-style waterfall/cascade mixer faucet over a bathroom vessel
  sink purely for its aesthetic (seen in a showroom display) — real
  ongoing downside: after washing hands, you must reach to shut it off
  with wet hands, and water constantly drips onto the counter at that
  spot, requiring a small towel kept there permanently. A concrete,
  ongoing form-over-function bathroom fixture caution.
- Classic collapsible drying rack (no dryer machine) fits fully extended
  in the bedroom zone between the bed and window, positioned along a
  radiator for faster drying, while still leaving a walking path clear —
  a real small-apartment laundry-drying solution where no dedicated
  laundry/drying zone exists.
