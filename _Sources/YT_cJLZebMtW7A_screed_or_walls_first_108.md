## Planning Rules — Screed-First vs. Walls-First Sequencing Decision

- **Two named methodologies**: "Method 1" pours screed across the whole apartment as one continuous operation before any walls/partitions are built; "Method 2" builds all walls/partitions first, then pours screed room-by-room afterward. Neither is universally correct — the source frames this explicitly as a decision to make per-project, not a fixed rule, then gives the actual deciding factor at the end (wall material — see below).
- **Method 1's stated advantage — fast, simple, accurate beacon leveling**: with no walls yet, one laser-level setup at the room's center lets every beacon across the whole floor be referenced and leveled from that single position, rather than needing to re-transfer a level reference room-to-room.
- **Template-stick technique, genuinely new and broadly applicable beyond this specific sequencing question**: instead of repeatedly reading a tape measure against the laser-level plane at each beacon (e.g. reading "99cm" over and over), cut a wooden batten/rod, hold it against the beacon at the room's highest point, and mark the laser's crossing point on the rod once with a marker — then level every other beacon by matching the laser line to that single mark on the rod, rather than re-reading a tape measure each time. **Two stated advantages with mechanism**: faster (matching a mark visually is quicker than reading and comparing a number), and more accurate (a tape measure's blade flexes/bows over distance, introducing error a rigid wooden rod doesn't have). `confirmed`, explicit mechanism, genuinely new technique for this store — the ~3-minute cost of making the template is explicitly framed as paying for itself many times over in saved time and accuracy.
- **Method 1's "flatter screed" advantage is significantly overstated, per the source's own field-experience argument**: (1) a genuinely continuous single-pour screed across an entire apartment is rare in practice — hand-mixed mortar (bucket + mixer) can't physically produce and place several tonnes of material in one working session, so even a "continuous contour" screed ends up poured in separate batches at different times, with the same seam-level-mismatch risk as a room-by-room pour; (2) a genuinely empty, obstruction-free apartment is rare in practice — tools, screed-mix bags, other materials, and crew facilities (temporary toilet, changing area, shower) routinely occupy floor space, again forcing a piecemeal pour regardless of the room layout; (3) **the decisive point**: nearly all modern finish flooring requires a thin self-leveling correction layer over the screed before laying the final floor covering, and that self-leveling layer flattens variation up to ~3-8mm regardless of how the underlying screed itself was sequenced — so the end result is effectively identical between the two methods once the correction layer goes down. **Exception, explicitly carved out**: if the space is genuinely fully open with nothing in it yet, and a concrete pump truck is being used for the pour, Method 1's single-day full-contour pour is unambiguously the right choice — this is the one case where its theoretical advantage is actually realized in practice.
- **Method 1's genuine, unqualified downside**: if walls are built after screed, plastering work inevitably drops mortar/plaster material onto the finished screed surface; unless cleaned immediately, it bonds permanently to the screed and later requires jackhammer removal — described as heavy labor and a real risk of screed damage. **Building walls first, then plastering, then pouring screed last (referenced to the zero/working-reference system covered in the companion video) avoids this failure mode entirely.**
- **Method 2's genuine downside, and its explicit mitigation**: transferring a level reference room-to-room via intermediate marks (rather than one single central setup) accumulates real error — the source states this typically amounts to 3-8mm across a project. **Mitigation, genuinely new pre-planning technique**: before any walls are built, while the space is still fully open, set up the laser level once at the center and mark reference points at ~1m intervals across the *entire* future apartment footprint (not just the current room) — every future room then inherits at least one mark from that single original setup, which can be used to re-establish the level reference in that room later with effectively the original setup's own accuracy, rather than accumulating transfer error room-by-room. **This premarking technique is presented as reducing Method 2's own error to be no worse than Method 1's** — closing the accuracy gap between the two approaches, contrary to the common assumption that Method 1 is simply "more accurate."
- **Premarking technique's own extended rule, directly connecting to the companion video's zero/working-reference system**: when placing these premarked reference points, mark them not at an arbitrary height but at exactly **99cm above the corridor's own finished floor level** — the reasoning for this specific height (and the underlying "zero mark"/"working mark" concept) is explicitly deferred to the next video (`yt_VHfUDBr0p4Q`, already processed in this store). Also mark every surface that won't itself be plastered later (window/door reveals, ventilation shaft faces, pipe risers), not just walls — a mark placed only on a wall that later gets plastered over is lost.
- **The actual deciding factor, stated as the single most important point in the video**: **wall construction material**, not personal preference or either method's marginal accuracy/flatness advantage.
  - **Masonry walls (aerated concrete block, foam block, etc.) → build walls first, screed after.** Reasoning ("volume-fill economics," заполнение объема): the floor area a future wall will occupy gets filled with cheap, light, easy-to-place block material instead of with screed — and screed is meaningfully more expensive, heavier, and more labor-intensive per unit volume to produce and place than the equivalent volume of aerated block. Worked illustration: at just 10cm screed thickness across a 100m² apartment, the volume "wasted" filling future wall footprints with screed instead of block can reach 1-2m³ — a real cost/labor difference once compared per-cubic-meter between the two materials (cost, weight, delivery/handling effort, and mixing/placing labor all favor block over screed for that specific volume).
  - **Drywall-on-metal-frame walls → pour screed first, then build the frame on top.** Reasoning, two parts: (1) a metal track profile screwed to an uneven raw subfloor bends/warps to follow that unevenness, producing a visibly bowed wall — screeding first gives the frame a flat reference surface to sit on; (2) the same volume-fill economics apply in reverse — filling that future-wall floor footprint with a stud-and-drywall assembly (especially double-layer drywall with insulation) is more complex and expensive per unit volume than filling the same footprint with ordinary screed, so it's cheaper/simpler to let screed occupy that volume and build the lighter wall assembly on top of it afterward.
  - **Explicit myth-debunking aside**: building a drywall stud "cube" (1m×1m×1m in the source's own framing) is undeniably faster to assemble than pouring an equivalent screed volume in isolation — but at real-project scale, factoring in the volume-fill economics above (especially for a double-layer, insulated drywall wall), a cubic meter of screed ends up cheaper and simpler than the equivalent cubic meter of drywall-on-frame construction. The source explicitly anticipates and pre-empts a "drywall is obviously faster" objection with this reasoning.
