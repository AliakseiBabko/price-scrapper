## Kitchen / Planning Rules — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Original problem**: a 35cm-thick multi-layer wall previously separated a cold loggia from two warm rooms (kitchen and living room) for insulation — but it also crippled the living room's usability, since a glazed section stood where the room's only viable furniture wall would otherwise be, forcing an impossible choice (a client-required corner sofa + facing TV had nowhere to go except mounting the TV on the glazing itself).
- **Zemskov's fix sequence, tying together several companion-video techniques from this same batch**: removed the useless glazed partition and replaced the old aluminum exterior glazing with new warm PVC glazing (making the loggia genuinely warm); built a proper partition wall between loggia and living room, creating a home-office zone on the loggia and a normal, fully furnishable living room with a corner sofa and TV zone; removed now-unneeded old insulation build-outs (since the loggia was now warm on its own), enlarging the loggia itself further; and finally **removed the old foam-block wall between loggia and kitchen entirely, rebuilding that boundary as a shared recessed storage niche** — the main subject of this source.
- **Zemskov's durable planning principle, stated directly**: "no kitchen has ever had too much storage" — regardless of how generous a kitchen's footprint already is, always add extra storage zones wherever the layout allows, given how much a kitchen inherently accumulates (dishware, food, spices, appliances, mugs, glasses, and more). Illustrated with this project's own numbers: 13m² total kitchen, ~9m² free floor area after cabinetry — generous compared to Zemskov's own observation that a typical ~6-7m² kitchen (before furnishing) still functions perfectly well, yet he still added dedicated extra storage here.
- **Zemskov's shared-niche technique — the core reusable idea**: rather than building a single straight wall (which here would have created one 83cm-deep closet on the loggia side — too deep to use effectively at that width, since the back portion becomes genuinely unreachable/unusable), **recess the wall to split what would be one uselessly-deep closet into two separately-accessible, properly-usable-depth closets** — one opening into the kitchen, one into the loggia — sharing a single recessed niche between them. **Resulting real dimensions**: 40cm depth on both the kitchen side and the loggia side, each roomy enough that a person can stand inside with room to spare (demonstrated on camera).
- **Stated use suggestions for the loggia-side compartment** (fitted with two doors, giving access to two separate storage zones behind the shared niche): general overflow storage, textbooks, canned goods, or — by simply omitting shelving in one section — long items like skis and snowboards that don't fit standard shelving depth anywhere else.
- **Zemskov's general closing principle**: it's worth slightly shrinking an auxiliary/secondary space to meaningfully grow a primary space's usability, **as long as the auxiliary space's actual functionality isn't gutted in the process** — a reduced-but-still-functional secondary room is a good trade for a genuinely usable primary room.
