## Design Concept — what "zoning" actually means (the load-bearing definition)

Prompted by a real consultation: clients wanted a 13 m² children's room "zoned" into sleeping, changing, play and work areas.

- **⚠️ Her answer, and the most transferable claim in the source: "и так не бывает" — that many zones do not fit in 13 m².** A zone is not a piece of furniture.
- **⚠️ The operative definition: putting a desk in a bedroom is not a work zone. It is a change of layout.** A zone exists only when the *space* is treated — **a dropped ceiling over the desk, dedicated fittings above it, a fragment of wall screening it, a different wall material in that area.**
- **Her second example makes the rule general**: a wardrobe standing in a bedroom is not a changing zone, it is a wardrobe standing in a bedroom. It becomes a zone if there is a recess to step into, **its own lighting, its own ceiling colour, or a different floor.**
- **⚠️ Practical consequence for this vault: "zoning" on a plan should be audited against this test.** If nothing but furniture placement changes, the zone is notional and the drawing is overclaiming.

## Design Concept — when a work zone must be separated, and when it need not

**She answers "no, not always"**, and gives the discriminating cases rather than a rule.

**Separation NOT needed when:**
- The person has a calm temperament and is not irritated by their surroundings while working.
- **⚠️ The work uses different faculties than speech** — her example is handwork (cooking, knitting, beadwork), during which one can converse without being distracted. **A criterion based on the task, not the room.**

**Separation IS needed when:**
- **Active Skype or phone negotiation** where background noise must not carry — **sliding partitions help, but only if the room's area is sufficient.**
- **Video calls specifically**, because of what is behind you. Her example is socks drying and a spouse crossing the frame in nothing but.
- **Work requiring high concentration** — her example is invoicing clients, working with figures, which cannot be done in a through-route.
- **You cannot get into work mode at home**, and the day becomes a circuit from computer to fridge to kettle to cat and back.

## Design Concept — desk position

- **⚠️ Her own lived case, which is the useful detail: whoever sits in the through-route gets interrupted.** She could not work while her husband walked past and pinched or kissed her each time; swapping seats fixed it. **His residual complaint is the other half of the rule: the person passing sees your screen.**
- **⚠️ The received "put the desk at the window" advice gets a substantial rebuttal**, and the reasons are worth keeping:
  - **In a very large number of standard plans the window faces the door**, so anyone entering looks at your back — uncomfortable — **and sees your screen.**
  - **Sun in your eyes, or on the monitor.**
  - **⚠️ In winter the radiator is under the window, so you are heated separately from the rest of your body.**
- **⚠️ Her fix, which keeps the daylight and drops the drawbacks: sit side-on to the window.** If there is decent wall left and right of the opening, **a corner desk** puts you close to the window with natural light and removes every listed problem.

## Design Concept — separation methods and their costs

- **Sliding partitions** — need floor area.
- **⚠️ Open-through shelving units («сквозные стеллажи»)** — they do not block insolation, so the rest of the room keeps its daylight. **But the honest limitation she attaches: sound is not blocked at all**, so they solve the visual problem only. *(Directly relevant to the glass-partition trade-off in `gwa1wsTYYLE`: light-passing separation is not acoustic separation.)*

## Labor / Delivery Model — staging, and the one-room case

- **⚠️ Do not try to solve the workspace in one go.** Set up a desk and the minimum needed to work now, **but plan for storage of files and materials to be needed later.** An explicitly phased approach.
- **⚠️ The one-room flat with three people**: you can still claim a workspace. The method is **partitioning off a section of the *table*** and stating firmly that mugs, books and pies do not go on it and no one eats there.
- **Psychological anchors substitute for physical separation**: objects carried over from the office — posters behind the monitor, a plant, the small dog that used to sit beside you — used to mark that the working day has begun.
- **Rituals do the same job**: fixed hours (9 to 6), working clothes rather than slippers, and the household may raise its questions **only after six.**

## Design Concept — the wall behind the desk

- **⚠️ She contradicts the common advice and reports a negative result from her own trial**: pinboards, chalk and marker boards covered in reminders and keyboard shortcuts **did not help her** — every time she forgot a shortcut she looked it up on the computer and never remembered that it was written on the wall in front of her. **"Если вы хотите, чтобы это помогало вам в работе — она не поможет."**
- **⚠️ Her alternative, and the design principle behind it: you are in a living room, not an office.** Decorate over the desk so it does not read as office — so that when you get up for tea you look around and see what you had stopped noticing.
- Her own summary position: **"чем эстетичнее, тем лучше"**, and she notes she sees plenty of examples where it is not — mentioning worktop materials that are pleasant to touch while working.
