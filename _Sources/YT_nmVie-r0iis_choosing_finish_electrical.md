## Durable Facts — Selection Process

- **Narrow top-down, not bottom-up**: first list the functions needed per room/device (plain switch vs. dimmer vs. multi-way switching vs. programmable thermostat for underfloor heating, etc.) to filter the ~thousands of SKUs down to roughly the ~20 product collections that actually offer every needed function — attempting to browse and choose directly in a store is described as impractical. `unverified`, single-account.
- **Bring physical samples to the store**: photos/renders of the overall design style, plus a physical swatch of the main surface finishes (a wallpaper scrap or a painted piece of drywall) — because devices that look good on a display stand can clash with the actual interior; comparing against real samples narrows the field to ~3–4 finalist options. `unverified`, single-account.
- **Don't shop by brand name**: claims every manufacturer/brand has both strong and weak collections, so "brand X is good, brand Y is bad" is not a valid selection criterion; treats a salesperson pushing one specific brand as a red flag. Instead, evaluate by **country of manufacture** and **terminal connection type**. `unverified`, single-account opinion.
- **Terminal connection type — screw vs. spring-clamp**: screw terminals require precise torque and are prone to being under- or over-tightened even by experienced installers, and screw connections can loosen over time from thermal expansion/contraction cycling. Spring-clamp (self-clamping) terminals remove this installer-error risk and are recommended especially for DIY installation. `unverified`, single-account, though the underlying mechanism (thermal cycling loosening screw terminals) is a commonly cited principle.
- **Check the wiring diagram printed on the back of the device**: better-quality devices print a connection diagram and strip-length guidance on the back; auto-stripping terminals (internal blades that strip the wire automatically, no separate stripping tool needed) are cited as another quality indicator.

## Durable Facts — Common Buying Mistakes

- **Don't mix collections/series across every room** — there is no rule requiring one uniform device series for the whole apartment; each room can have its own color/shape/series. `unverified`, framed as correcting a common (self-interested) retail sales pitch.
- **Avoid flush/flat classic frames on heavily textured walls** (decorative plaster, deeply embossed wallpaper) — the flush edge won't sit flush against the texture and looks bad; use "floating" frames with a spacer standoff instead.
- **Use socket covers/caps in the kitchen** — easier to wipe clean of grease/dust than cleaning inside an open socket with a brush, despite the inconvenience of opening the cover each time.
- **Don't buy finish-stage devices too early** — install cheap temporary ("times") sockets without decorative frames during construction, and only bring the real finish hardware onto site once wallpapering starts, since earlier-installed nice devices/frames get damaged, lost, or thrown out during ongoing renovation work.
- **Avoid illuminated (backlit) switches almost everywhere** — the indicator light disturbs sleep (bright point glowing in a dark room) and most people ask to have it removed; more importantly, with LED bulbs/strips, an illuminated switch's internal capacitor causes visible flickering (bulbs) or a permanent dim glow (LED strips) because the capacitor charges/discharges through the load. `unverified`, single-account, but the flickering mechanism is a widely known LED/capacitive-switch interaction, not novel.
- **Don't overpay for splash/waterproof-rated outlets everywhere** — reserve them for locations where water will routinely reach inside the socket box (e.g., a socket mounted inside a shower enclosure); ordinary outlets are already adequately splash- and brief-immersion-resistant per the speaker's own on-camera demo (pouring water on a live standard outlet). `unverified`, anecdotal demo, not a certified test.
- **Don't buy finish electrical devices at open-air/wholesale construction markets** — counterfeit devices there are visually indistinguishable from genuine ones, and failures typically surface only months later once roughly half the installed devices in a batch fail; buy only from authorized retailers. `unverified`, single-account.
- **USB-equipped outlets recommended for phone-charging locations** instead of an ordinary outlet with a permanently plugged-in charger block, for appearance and dual-device charging convenience.
- **Be cautious with touch/capacitive switches** — described as having the highest return rate of any device category because they require a precise, sustained touch (vs. a quick slap of a mechanical switch while walking past) and can be unreliable; recommended only if the buyer has personally tried and is comfortable with the interaction model.
- **Avoid wooden switch/outlet frames in wet rooms** regardless of water-resistant treatment claims — wood remains a natural material vulnerable to humidity/temperature over time.
- **Buy dimmers only after finalizing light fixtures/bulbs** — not every light source supports smooth dimming, and dimmers come in leading-edge vs. trailing-edge types that must match the bulb/driver's supported dimming type or they won't work together reliably.
- **Don't combine an underfloor-heating thermostat into the same frame/series as sockets and switches unless it's the matching brand** — cross-brand thermostats forced into a matching frame are typically feature-limited, and using a non-original thermostat can void the underfloor-heating manufacturer's warranty; keep the thermostat in its own frame, separate from the switch/outlet run.
- **Device durability is rated in cycle count, not years**: cited standard is roughly 2,000 minimum switch-position or outlet insertion/removal cycles (~10 years of typical use); most modern devices comfortably exceed this (up to ~100,000 cycles cited, `uncertain` — figure garbled in transcript), so paying extra for "ultra-durable" marketing beyond the cheapest tier is generally unnecessary except for the cheapest Chinese-made products.
- **"Safety" marketing claims about sockets/switches are largely misleading**: actual electrical safety (shock protection) is provided by breakers/RCDs inside the panel, not by the socket/switch device itself; the only legitimate device-level safety feature is child-safety shutters, which don't matter for adult safety since all legally sold devices meet the same baseline standard. `unverified`, single-account opinion.
- **Pigmented-through-the-mass plastic vs. surface-painted plastic**: mass-pigmented plastic (all the way through) doesn't show scratches because the material below the surface matches the surface color; surface-painted plastic reveals a lighter base material when scratched. White/ivory/beige devices are more likely to be mass-pigmented. For a residential apartment (vs. a high-traffic commercial space), the speaker states either type is durable enough not to matter.

## Numeric Data (EUR, unverified single-account figures — no year/region confidence beyond "as of this video")

- Good-quality device mechanism: ~€45 (cited as the price/quality sweet spot).
- Standard/base frames: roughly the same as or slightly less than the mechanism cost; designer/exclusive frames: €50–€100+ each.
- Full finish-electrical package for a 100 m² apartment: **all-base-frame ≈ €800**; **some designer frames ≈ €1,000**; **all designer frames ≈ €2,000** (roughly double the base cost) — cited to illustrate that frame markup, not mechanism cost, drives most of the price spread, and retailers are financially incentivized to upsell frames.
- Recommendation: only use designer/expensive frames on "visible" points (in open sightlines); use base frames on "invisible" points (behind TV/furniture/desks, on window reveals under curtains) to control cost without a visible quality tradeoff.
