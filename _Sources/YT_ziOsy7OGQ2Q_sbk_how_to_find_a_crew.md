## Mistakes / Warnings — First-Meeting Vetting Checklist for an Individual Tradesperson

- **⚠️ Punctuality at the first meeting, stated as a direct professionalism signal**: a tradesperson arriving on time signals he values his own time (professional self-respect) and the client's time; being late is framed directly as disrespect toward the client and a leading indicator of how the relationship will go.
- **⚠️ Personal tidiness/appearance as an indirect quality signal, explicitly hedged as "not 100% reliable, but worth noting"**: a neatly dressed tradesperson who takes care not to track dirt (removes muddy boots, uses shoe covers) is more likely to be careful in his general work habits, including workmanship — contrasted with a named negative real-world example (a utility-company plumber who tracked mud through an entire apartment and building stairwell without removing boots). The speaker explicitly notes this heuristic isn't guaranteed, but correlates with respect for the client's property and, by extension, for the work itself.
- **⚠️ Tool inventory quantity over brand, an explicit anti-heuristic correction**: the speaker explicitly rejects "red-tool-brand snobbery" (a real in-industry stereotype that a specific tool brand color signals professionalism) — instead, judge by the **breadth/quantity** of tools brought: a tradesperson who shows up with only one tool (e.g. a single rotary hammer) for a job that requires several distinct tools is a red flag; a professional typically owns and brings a wide range of task-specific tools because he knows different jobs need different tools.
- **⚠️ Tool condition as a provenance/ownership signal**: clean, well-maintained, properly stored/packed tools suggest the tradesperson treats his tools as his livelihood and cares for them; tools that look freshly "borrowed from a nearby construction site" or salvaged from a scrapyard are a reason to question whether the person is a genuine tradesperson at all versus someone who scavenged tools to take a one-off side job ("шабашка").
- **⚠️ Smell of alcohol or visible hangover at the first meeting or on-site — an explicit hard pass, not just a caution**: framed directly as disqualifying, since a person in this state cannot execute precision work (straight lines, accurate measurement/cutting) — explicitly contrasted with an older, looser industry norm ("used to be OK to have two beers and still lay tile") the speaker says no longer meets current quality expectations.
- **⚠️ Named long-term-alcohol-abuse indicator: visibly poor dental condition ("gnilye zuby")** — offered as a genuinely observable-without-expertise signal, reasoned through three stated mechanisms: chronic drinkers tend to eat poorly, neglect dental care (cost/priority), and a good tradesperson who earns adequately would otherwise maintain basic dental health. Explicitly not a demand for a "Hollywood smile" — the bar stated is merely "not badly rotted."
- **⚠️ A same-day/first-day advance-payment request, a named red flag with an explicit legitimate-exception carve-out**: a tradesperson requesting an advance before any work has started (the video's own example: asking for 1,000-2,000 RUB) is flagged as a sign of financial instability/unreliability — reasoned as: a professional who values each client as his livelihood keeps his own finances in order and doesn't need to solicit small sums; if there's a genuine emergency requiring money, it would realistically be "tens of thousands of RUB," not a trivial sum. **Explicit legitimate exception carved out**: a standard, appropriate deposit is **~30% of the total contracted price**, requested **after** a written contract is signed — this is a normal, expected practice distinct from an ad hoc small-cash request before any agreement exists. A second legitimate variant noted: a tradesperson who has been burned by a non-paying client before may reasonably ask for a small deposit specifically to confirm the client's own ability/willingness to pay — distinguished from the disqualifying pattern by context and small scale.
- **⚠️ A named "disappearing act" pattern, described as a strong, repeatable red flag**: a tradesperson who fails to show up as scheduled, becomes unreachable by phone for an extended period (hours to a full day), then resurfaces with an implausible excuse (the video's own recurring joke example: repeatedly claiming a family member's funeral) — explicitly linked back to the alcohol-related concerns above as the speaker's suspected underlying cause, and framed as highly likely to recur ("he'll disappear again in another two or three days") rather than a one-off excusable incident.
- **Explicit methodological note on how to apply this checklist**: no single item alone is a disqualifying signal (the speaker specifically warns against rejecting someone over teeth alone, or an advance request alone) — the pattern is meant to be read holistically across several observed points together, not as a rigid points system.
