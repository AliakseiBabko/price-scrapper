## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Design storage/scope to the household's actual stated priorities, not
  a generic default** — stated examples: families with young children
  need more concealed/child-safe storage plus soft decor (rugs); avid
  cooks/entertainers need large built-in appliances and a spacious prep

  Worth weighing actual entertaining frequency against a table sized for
  a rare best-case scenario.

## Electrical / Lighting — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Outlet-count planning by zone, with specific figures**: kitchen zone
  needs roughly **8-10 outlets** (fridge, hood, dishwasher, microwave,
  stove, kettle, other small appliances, plus a dedicated outlet under
  the sink for a garbage disposal unit); living zone needs roughly **5-6
  outlets** (TV, game consoles, decorative lighting, etc.). **General
  principle: plan more outlets upfront than seems necessary** — avoids
  ugly extension cords and hunting for a phone charging in a corner.
- **In-wall cable-routing technique**: run a cable channel/conduit inside
  the wall with blank cover plates on both ends, to route cabling (e.g.
  TV to AV equipment) invisibly rather than along the surface.
- **Major appliances (fridge, dishwasher, oven) should each get a
  dedicated electrical circuit** — improves safety and prevents voltage
  drops when multiple devices switch on simultaneously. These outlets are
  typically placed in the kitchen cabinet plinth/toe-kick — not
  inconvenient in practice, since everything can be switched off from the
  breaker panel; **label every breaker correctly** so this stays usable.
- **Ventilation regulatory-style constraint, stated as a general rule for
  new-build apartments (region unconfirmed)**: a kitchen hood should not
  be connected to a shared building ventilation stack — the stated reason
  is that without this separation, cooking odors spread between
  neighboring units through the shared duct. Mitigation: plan a
  carbon-filter recirculating hood system, **and** install a standard
  vent fitted with a backdraft/check valve, specifically to block a
  neighbor's odors from coming back through the shared system if they
  improperly tap into it.
- **Even with a strong hood, combining kitchen and living means cooking
  smells will still spread through the whole combined space to some
  degree** — a realistic expectation-setting point, not a defect to
  design away entirely.
- **Hood airflow-capacity sizing formula, explicit and checkable**:
  **kitchen floor area (m²) × ceiling height (m) × 12 = required hood
  airflow capacity (m³/h)** — a standard air-exchange-multiplier
  calculation.
- **Hood mounting height**: 60-75cm above a gas cooktop; 50-60cm above an
  electric cooktop. Choose a genuinely quiet hood — a loud one disrupts
  the atmosphere, especially with guests present.
- **Acoustic comfort in a combined kitchen-living space benefits from
  sliding partitions, wood flooring, wood surfaces generally, rugs, and
  other soft textiles** — a general material-based sound-comfort
  heuristic, not a single fix.
- **Lighting should be explicitly multi-layered, three distinct layers**:
  (1) **Primary/general light** — bright enough for genuine cleaning,
  either powerful fixtures or a lamp centered per functional zone;
  chandeliers are now mostly decorative rather than the actual main light
  source, but still fine to include; (2) **Accent light** — task lighting
  such as under-cabinet/backsplash lighting or shelf lighting, aimed at
  illuminating a specific work surface; (3) **Decorative light** — wall
  sconces or floor lamps, which double as a legitimate stand-in for main
  lighting with soft warm light for coziness. **General rule: the more
  such light sources in a kitchen-living zone, the better** for creating
  atmosphere.

## Furniture / Built-ins & Zoning Techniques — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Furniture zoning**: a sofa with its back facing the kitchen clearly
  separates the living zone from the kitchen. **Real client-pattern
  caution**: many clients specifically request a sliding/pull-out sofa
  bed to accommodate occasional overnight guests, but most pull-out
  sofas look genuinely unattractive once extended — weigh how relevant
  that overnight-guest need actually is before committing to this
  trade-off (this **corroborates** the sofa-only trade-off already
  recorded in this batch from `YT_qHuFie5U65g`, from an independent
  practitioner). In small spaces, a bar counter can divide zones while
  doubling as extra work surface; in larger spaces, cabinets, bookshelves,
  or a large dining table with chairs can serve as the zoning element.
- **Arches, shelving, partitions, and false walls in various
  configurations** are a standard zoning method, but the designer
  explicitly cautions against chasing specific partition-shape trends
  seen on moodboards, since they age quickly — prefer natural materials
  that complement the kitchen's other textures. If space allows, a more
  functional partition can host a bio-fireplace or display shelving —
  **with an explicit safety/practical warning: never place a fireplace
  under a TV.**
- **Flooring zoning, with a concrete structural reason for material
  choice, not just aesthetics**: put practical, hard flooring (tile)
  specifically under the kitchen cabinetry, because heavy cabinetry can
  dent/compress a softer material (quartz-vinyl or laminate) over time —
  a dent in one spot forces the compressed material to bulge somewhere
  else. The living zone can prioritize comfort/coziness (carpet,
  wood-look) instead. **If a shared dining table spans the flooring
  transition line between kitchen and living zones, position the chairs
  so they don't sit directly on the material seam.**
- **Ceiling/lighting zoning**: each functional zone can have its own
  ceiling treatment and its own light — but make sure each zone gets its
  own light switch, not a single shared one.

## Furniture / Built-ins — Storage Planning Rules

- **Size concealed storage to actual usage, not a maximalist default**:
  don't build cabinets all the way to the ceiling if the household
  doesn't own enough dishware/small appliances to fill that space — the
  wasted volume costs money and typically becomes a dumping ground for
  unrelated clutter instead.
- **Vary opening mechanisms by cabinet position** (e.g. lift-up fronts
  for upper cabinets, pull-out drawers lower down) and **never economize
  on mechanism quality** — cheap mechanisms wear out quickly.
- **Use internal drawer/cabinet dividers/organizers** (e.g. dedicated
  compartments for kitchen gadgets or spices) so concealed storage stays
  organized while remaining accessible; position all drawers/compartments
  at a comfortable working height.
- **Ensure concealed kitchen storage specifically is well-lit and
  ventilated**, not just enclosed.
