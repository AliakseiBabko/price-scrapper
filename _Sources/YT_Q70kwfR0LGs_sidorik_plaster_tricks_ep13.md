## Walls / Ceilings — Ten Named Plastering Tricks

1. **Chase-filling-in-finished-plaster technique, with a named wrong way**:
   filling a chase flush immediately (the common approach) causes the
   plaster to draw in/shrink into a visible groove later — invisible until
   raking/side light after painting, at which point it's a real defect
   requiring one or two extra passes of putty to fix. **Correct technique**:
   overfill the chase with a slight excess bump, wait ~40 minutes until
   it firms enough to hold shape but still cuts with a spatula edge, then
   trim flush with the existing wall — at that hardness it won't sink
   further.
2. **Digital cable/chase-location documentation upgrade**: photograph
   the wall, then use a photo-annotation measuring app (named: Image
   Metr, described as outperforming Bosch's own equivalent app) to place
   dimension callouts directly on the photo instead of hand-notes; the app
   can pair via Bluetooth with a laser distance meter so a real
   measurement auto-populates the callout instead of needing a tape
   measure read manually — cited as the specific reason the speaker
   upgraded his laser-distance-meter model to one with Bluetooth.
3. **Material distribution on the slab**: spread plaster bags in even
   small piles across the whole apartment rather than one stacked pile, to
   avoid excess point-load on the floor slab. Real quantity cited: 40 bags
   total for this apartment's plastering job.
4. **Named plaster product and its working properties**: Волма слой
   (Volma Layer) gypsum plaster — pliable, applicable up to 60mm in a
   single layer, sticky enough to hold on a ceiling application.
5. **Consistent-mix discipline**: measure mixing water precisely with a
   graduated container every batch (not by eye) so every batch has the
   same consistency; open bags with a knife/scissors rather than tearing
   (less mess); pour an initial "cushion" of dry plaster into the water
   first to prevent clumping at the bucket bottom before adding the rest.
6. **Scaffold/trestle-protection trick**: wrap scaffolding/trestles in
   plastic film + cardboard before starting plaster work — cheaper in
   time than scraping dried plaster off them afterward.
7. **Adhesion-layer trick for tricky substrates**: trowel a thin
   "bonding coat" of the same plaster onto the wall before throwing dabs
   — dabs thrown directly sometimes fail to bond or trap air pockets; the
   thin pre-coat fixes this.
8. **Floor protection**: canvas/tarp preferred over plastic sheeting,
   since dried plaster shakes off canvas cleanly; cardboard is an
   acceptable fallback. Framed as worth the setup time even though any
   single dropped dab seems trivial to just scrape up — at whole-apartment
   scale the cumulative time saved is real.
9. **Tool-storage-on-the-wet-wall trick**: stick hand tools (float, etc.)
   directly onto the wet plastered wall itself while working (high or low
   depending on scaffold position) instead of setting them down — keeps
   both hands free without bending/reaching, especially useful when
   working from a raised platform.
10. **Batch size matched to personal working pace**: mix only as much
    plaster as can be worked before it sets — fast workers can mix 2 bags
    at once, slower workers should mix 1 bag or half a bag.
11. **Top-down default application order**: plastering from the top of a
    wall downward means any plaster that falls doesn't damage an
    already-leveled lower strip; speaker states personal indifference to
    this order in practice, applying it as a default rather than a strict
    rule.
12. **Trim (подрезка) timing and its real importance**: check with a
    fingertip ~40-50 minutes after application — ready to trim once
    firm but still slightly yielding; scrape flush against the beacons
    with the straightedge repeatedly *until the straightedge stops
    removing material* — that's the signal the plane is actually flat.
    Skipping or mistiming this step leaves raking-light-visible sag/bulges
    invisible under normal lighting.
13. **Beacons are single-use**: pulled beacons bend during removal
    (even carefully) and can't be reliably reused, contrary to a
    recurring viewer suggestion.
14. **Speaker's own stated opinion/practice: skips the traditional
    float/smooth-polish finish step ("замыливание"/"глянцевание")
    entirely on his own projects**, `single-account`, opinion — reasoning
    given: (a) it takes as long or longer than simple puttying anyway; (b)
    workers rushing it often over-wet the plaster into a "wet-on-wet"
    condition that later delaminates; (c) even a careful hand-floated
    finish still shows "orange peel" texture under raking light and needs
    puttying regardless, so the extra step doesn't actually skip the
    putty stage. His stated philosophy: a plasterer's job is to deliver a
    flat *plane*; smoothness is putty's job, not plaster's. He instead
    machine-sands the set plaster (120-grit, ~10 min/wall) — states this
    gives a flatter result in his subjective judgment despite exposing
    filled voids/craters that were invisible before sanding. Explicitly
    flagged as his own opinion — "many float it and get excellent
    results."
15. **Physically-lighter half-strip technique**: for a beginner or
    lower-strength worker, throw and level only half a plaster strip at a
    time, then throw the second half onto the now-leveled first half
    (easier to pull since one edge reference already exists) — roughly
    halves the physical pulling effort per strip compared to throwing a
    full strip at once.
16. **Solo-worker high-reach mixing trick**: to avoid lifting a fully
    mixed bucket (>50kg once a bag of dry mix is added to water) up to
    scaffold/platform height alone, carry the empty mixing bucket and a
    dry bag of plaster up separately, then mix directly on the platform —
    avoids ever lifting the combined heavy wet mass. Also notes a
    two-paddle mixer attachment mixes faster and better than a
    single-paddle one (speaker's own comparison), and the mixer paddle
    should be rinsed in a water bucket immediately after each batch or
    dried plaster later needs to be hammered off.
17. **Inside-corner-forming sequencing**: plaster the two walls meeting
    at an inside corner on separate days — parallel walls first, the
    perpendicular wall the next day — so one side of the eventual corner
    is already flat and hardened when the corner itself is formed;
    sharpen the corner and trim overlap with a plain flat spatula, not a
    specialized corner trowel, which the speaker states gives a worse
    result in his own experience.
18. **Outside-corner-forming technique with a temporary reference rail**:
    find the wall's highest point via laser or bubble level (this becomes
    the reference plane); verify true 90° at that point with a carpenter's
    square (since the laser's own angle to the wall may not itself be
    exactly 90°); temporarily fasten a straightedge/rail to the adjacent
    wall at that verified reference (screwed with slightly oversized
    holes so it can be nudged before final tightening, or "frozen" on with
    a fast-setting gypsum/cement-plaster mix if no gypsum is on hand);
    plaster the corner using the rail as one edge reference and the flat
    wall as the other, screeding with a carpenter's square (not a
    straightedge) — one leg riding the flat wall, the other along the
    rail; trim once set and remove the temporary rail.
