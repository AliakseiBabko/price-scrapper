## Materials — the named marbles, and that tile copies specific ones

- **⚠️ Tile designs imitate *specific named* marbles, not "marble" generically** — she flags this as something she had not consciously registered despite specifying tile constantly: the design traces a real quarried stone. Quarried mostly in **Italy, Brazil and Turkey**.
- **Statuario** — the most expensive white marble in Italy; **contrasting and sparse veining.**
- **Calacatta** — **more diffuse figure with more veins**; from the same Carrara quarry as Statuario. The default expectation in a classical bathroom.
- **Arabescato** — **more contrasting than Calacatta**, with a distinct structure: the veins enclose rounded areas, which she describes as *"looking down on a grey ocean with rounded islands floating in it."*
- **Emperador** and **Sahara Noir** named as other common imitations.
- **Invisible Grey** — her own preference, a chaotic and graphic figure, a strong design. **Naming warning: makers label it inconsistently** — Invisible White, Invisible Pearl, "Invisible-something" — so the name does not identify the stone.

## Materials — Group 1: strict neoclassic

Signals she uses to place a client here: a spacious room with high ceilings, strict forms, a limited palette, mouldings, mirrors, candelabra-style pendants. Scoped to *strict* neoclassic — not coloured neoclassic, and not neoclassic drifting toward eclecticism.

- **Default: marble-effect tile**, typically Calacatta.
- **⚠️ Do not combine two marbles, especially at the same pattern scale.** Exception: a deliberate artistic panel or geometric layout.
- **⚠️ Do not mix marble-effect with semi-precious stone effects (onyx, agate).** Worst case is kitsch — "всё лучшее сразу"; **best case is a saturated luxury that reads Art Deco rather than neoclassic**, i.e. you land in a different style than intended.
- **Wood-effect porcelain is a common partner**: light marble on the walls, wood-effect on the floor.
  - **⚠️ The quality test for wood-effect porcelain is "many faces" («много лиц») — you should not be able to see where the pattern repeats.** A directly usable purchasing check.
  - **Choose dark and mid wood tones.** Avoid bleached or over-light wood, and unnatural colours (green, blue). Her analogy: a man in a dinner jacket with green hair — the result is eclecticism, not restrained classic.
- **Geometric layouts**, whole-floor or as a panel. **Chequerboard** from two marble or stone effects, often laid diagonally — **and its success depends on the ratio of the chequer size to the room's scale**, not on the tiles themselves.
- **Octagon** (square tile with cut corners) — an attribute of **English** style, popular in 19th-century Victorian England. **The inserts need not be black.** Suits neoclassic and pronounced eclecticism alike.
- **Herringbone, chevron and hexagon**: on a kitchen backsplash a neutral tile laid herringbone works, and these also go on floors. **⚠️ The interest comes from the laying pattern rather than from the tile's own pattern** — a way to get character from a plain tile.
- **⚠️ Floor borders («бордюр») are a supporting element in neoclassic, and easy to overdo.** A border is laconic and echoes wall mouldings — **but do not make it too wide, and do not place it too close to the centre of a panel, or the result reads Art Deco.**

## Materials — Group 2: country, farmhouse, Provence, shabby chic

Signals: a country-house atmosphere, closeness to nature, aged and textured surfaces, open storage, woven baskets and rugs. Homely rather than formal.

- **Her distinctions between the four**, which are finer than the usual lumping: **country** — frank roughness, barn doors, chests; **farmhouse** (American) — more laconic, often with contemporary elements; **Provence** (French) — built on classical forms with painted furniture; **shabby chic** — barely different from Provence, but the name means exactly what it says, "шикарное обшарпэ," wear and traces of time. All are classical-leaning; **farmhouse is the one that can go either classical or contemporary.**
- **⚠️ What separates this group from neoclassic, stated as a rule: a love of texture and ornament, with less tonal contrast and less gloss.** Wear and matte finishes are supposed to lower the pomp — though she adds the honest qualifier that **the pomp is not absent, just of a different kind** ("про пафос неправда, просто он другой").
- **The tile repertoire is the same as neoclassic, but less polished and more alive.** Specifically:
  - **Wood-effect: simpler species — oak, no exotics — and floor only. Do not run it up the walls.**
  - **Stone-effect: not marble.** Use simpler stone — **granite, sandstone, quartzite**. Stone, but not *noble* stone.
  - **Deliberately aged tile** — new, but manufactured to look old, with worn edges or a worn pattern.
  - **⚠️ Factory tile made to look handmade** — an irregular edge, which **deliberately widens the grout joints**, or an uneven surface. **Reads best on small plain tiles.**
  - **Terracotta** (fired clay), traditionally rectangular, square or hexagonal. **Demanding to maintain — though not impossibly so, and an imitation exists if the upkeep is unwelcome.**
  - **Ornament is welcome here, including floral**, especially on small formats, on floors or backsplashes; whole walls more rarely.
- **⚠️ Zellige («зелик») — and the mechanism is what makes it identifiable.** Small pieces are **hand-dipped in glaze and fired in a clay kiln where the temperature is deliberately uneven**, so **each tile takes a slightly different colour and no two are identical.** That variance is precisely what distinguishes genuine artisanal zellige from its uniform serial imitations — the same authenticity-marker logic as the ikat white stripe in `7Kk_TTxoq4g`.

## Materials — Group 3: contemporary and modernism

Signals: laconic, plenty of empty space, flat fronts, integrated handles, minimal and carefully chosen decor. These styles reject historical styles but permit 20th-century objects; either minimalist and contrasting, or textured and cosy; a neutral palette with natural materials and accents carried by accessories.

- **⚠️ They favour large monolithic surfaces — which is why plaster and paint are more popular than wallpaper here.** A useful causal link rather than a taste claim.
- **Large-format porcelain**: the pattern does not break and the eye glides across it.
  - **⚠️ Large-format wood-effect is the genuinely interesting case: a real alternative to timber wall panels.** It does not warp, it can be cut freely, **the grain figure is pre-drawn and therefore predictable**, and **it is cheaper than actual wall panels** — especially against something like American walnut.
- **Metro tile** — a laconic glossy rectangle. Origin: the **New York subway, early 20th century**, chosen because it was cheap, easy to clean, and its format could clad curved surfaces; adopted by the Paris and London metros, then factories, then homes. Now available across formats, mixing smooth and relief faces and new colours. **"Like brushstrokes on a painting" — even a single type introduces movement.**
  - **⚠️ The distinction people get wrong: metro tile *with a bevel* («фацет») is «кабанчик», and a bevelled tile suits only classic or retro — not contemporary. For a contemporary scheme, specify bevel-free.**
- **Terrazzo** — originally poured concrete with marble, stone and glass chips; the technique runs from ancient Greece to Venice and then to the USSR. **⚠️ Practical trick: take one of the chip colours and reuse it as the paint colour or the companion tile in the same room** — a ready-made palette source.
- **Concrete-effect tile** forms a single unbroken surface but **reads quite rough**. **⚠️ Pair it with more refined materials — gloss, glass, thin edges. Add anything excessively rough and you land in Loft instead of contemporary.**
- **Ornament is possible but should be graphic and geometric, so the rhythm reads easily.**

## Design Concept — her closing principle, which outranks the taxonomy

- **⚠️ She calls the three-group division deliberately approximate**, and says the real rule is different: **tile must relate to every room in the home, including the rooms it is not laid in.** "Воспринимайте дом как единый организм, как единое пространство."
- **⚠️ And the consequence, stated bluntly**: if you want concrete-effect tile on one side of the house and mouldings on the other, **do not pretend the result is neoclassic — do eclecticism properly, throughout, instead.** Choosing a coherent mixed direction beats mislabelling an incoherent one.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- Two marbles together, especially at the same pattern scale.
- Marble-effect with onyx or agate effects.
- Bleached, over-light or unnaturally coloured wood-effect in a classical scheme.
- Wood-effect climbing the walls in a country/farmhouse scheme.
- A neoclassic floor border made too wide or set too close to a panel's centre — it becomes Art Deco.
- **Bevelled («кабанчик») metro tile in a contemporary interior.**
- Concrete-effect tile paired with further rough materials — the result is Loft.
- Specifying a marble-effect by trade name alone, given makers label the same stone inconsistently.
