## Plumbing — Rough Installation Checklist

- **Sink/basin drain pipe: use 40mm sewage pipe, not 50mm** — modern faucets aerate water and use much less flow than older fixtures, so 40mm is more than sufficient; using 40mm instead of 50mm needs a meaningfully narrower wall channel, easier for DIY chiseling.
- **Bathtub drain pipe: use 50mm sewage pipe, never 40mm** — a bathtub drains a much larger volume; a narrower pipe drains too slowly and clogs more often given the different debris load versus a sink.
- **Never chase (штробить) a load-bearing wall for pipe routing, not even vertically.** When a pipe must run along a load-bearing wall, build out with 50mm-thick aerated-concrete blocks screwed directly to the wall, or a drywall stud wall along it — do not bury the pipe under a thick plaster layer alone (it will delaminate, especially once later chased into for maintenance).
- **Rough electrical and plumbing stub-outs must be installed only after rough room geometry is fully formed AND after plastering is complete** — installing before plastering buries stub-outs under the plaster layer, forcing extension splices later (extra joints = extra leak risk) when finish fixtures go in.
- **Never chase into plaster only days after application, before it's fully cured** — chiseling before cure causes an entire plaster section to delaminate later (often only discovered after tiling, when a whole slab of plaster+tile detaches) — a very expensive rework. Cure time depends on plaster brand/type; follow manufacturer specs with a margin.
- **Sink placement relative to a side wall**: if not flush in a corner, must have a **minimum 100mm offset** from the side wall — less looks cramped and makes it physically impossible to reach a hand in to wash the sink.
- **Mark rough plumbing/electrical stub-out heights from the level of the *future finished floor*, not the current raw subfloor** — since supply pipes are typically embedded in the screed before it's poured. Method: calculate planned screed thickness, add 15mm for tile+adhesive in a wet room, mark that reference height with a laser level on all walls, and do all further layout from that mark. Getting this wrong can force cutting a vanity's bottom drawer or extending flexible supply hoses (extra joints, extra leak risk).
- **Sink-drain stub-out height standard: 600mm from finished floor to the drain outlet's center**, for two reasons: (1) sewage-pipe slope requirement (30mm per running meter for 40 or 50mm pipe) means 600mm ensures correct drainage even up to 5m from the stack; (2) at 600mm, the stub-out lands precisely on a tile joint line for the two most common/practical tile sizes (60×60 or 30×30, both multiples of 30cm) — producing two clean half-circle notches rather than an off-joint full-circle cutout with a fragile, breakage-prone tile "tail." `confirmed`, explicit numeric mechanism.
- **Never install rough plumbing stub-outs before the design project is finalized/approved** — sink stub-out centering depends on the sink's exact width, which only a proper designer can correctly determine (accounting for clearances to the toilet/tub and ergonomics).
- **Space hot/cold supply and drain stub-outs as compactly as possible under a vanity** — the cabinet's cutout is sized for closely-grouped stub-outs; overly spread stub-outs (real bad examples: 15-20cm apart) force cutting into the cabinet's back panel, often destroying the drawer's rear support/fixation and requiring the drawer to be permanently glued shut to hide the modification.
- **Correct mounting-plate spec for sink supply stub-outs: 100mm center-to-center spacing** for flexible-hose connections — **not** the 150mm center-to-center plate (that spacing is for a standard mixer/faucet's own fixed hot/cold inlet spacing, a different fitting purpose).
- **Chase all three lines (hot, cold, sewage) for a sink together in one single wide channel (120-140mm wide)**, never as three separate narrow channels — thin wall material between separate parallel channels reliably breaks/crumbles during chiseling.
- **A wall carrying embedded plumbing lines must be at least 100mm thick, ideally ~120mm** — thinner risks accidentally breaking all the way through the wall while chasing, compromising both structural continuity and sound isolation.
- **Chase-cutting sequence/safety rule**: mark all pipe routes first, cut **all** channels with the wall saw/angle grinder first, **then** clean out **all** channels with the hammer drill afterward — never cut-and-clean one channel at a time. Reasoning: sequential cut-and-clean means repeatedly walking across large loose rubble mid-task — a real stated injury risk (sprained ankle, or falling with a running hammer drill).
- **Rough plumbing installation sequence: mounting plate first → sewage pipe → hot/cold water supply pipes, in that exact order, never any other order.** Reasoning: mounting-plate installation is the messiest step (requires chasing to the plate's own thickness with an oscillating multi-tool so it sits flush with finished plaster), best done before other pipes are in place to avoid re-dirtying them; and it's much easier to route supply pipes around an already-fixed large sewage pipe than the reverse.
- **Sink drain/branch routing direction: always route starting from the lowest point (the stack) working upward/outward to the sink**, not from the sink down to the stack — starting from the sink risks a vertical-drop miscalculation that misses the stack's own branch fitting; starting from the stack lets the correct rise to the sink's drain-center height be calculated cleanly once the horizontal run's slope is set.
- **Never use a single 90° elbow fitting for a sewage-pipe direction change — always use two 45° elbow fittings joined together instead** (technically forming a ~135° fitting pair per the transcript's own correction, still called a "45° elbow" as a part name). Reasoning: smoother interior profile, better flow-through, easier to snake/clean, faster water flow (less wall buildup), fewer clogs. **Exception: a single 90° elbow is acceptable specifically where a horizontal run transitions to vertical at a wall stub-out** — everywhere else, always the two-45°-elbow pair.
- **Always use proper sewage-pipe lubricant — aerosol/spray type, not tube — when assembling push-fit pipe joints** — dry assembly requires much more force and reliably rolls/damages the rubber seal ring, a defect only discovered later once the system leaks. (Corrected 2026-08-18: an earlier draft of this note had the aerosol-vs-tube preference backwards; re-checked against the transcript — the source prefers aerosol, matching this store's existing `Rough_Plumbing_Sequencing.md` guidance from an independent source, now a second corroboration.)
- **Water-supply pipe insulation sleeve spec: 18mm inner diameter, 6mm wall thickness** — avoid the cheapest sleeve type often bundled/upsold by pipe sellers, which tears during installation and becomes purely decorative.
- **Hot/cold supply orientation convention: hot on the left, cold on the right** (mnemonic: "left is where the heart is, warm").
- **Critical final QC step, explicit numeric protocol: pressure-test the assembled system before covering/burying it, using proper equipment (5-10 atmospheres), not just a base household water-pressure/flow check.** A base-pressure "open the valve and see if it leaks" test often fails to reveal an imperfect joint. Method: fill the system with water, connect a pressure-testing pump, pressurize to 2 atmospheres, then 5, then 10, hold ~10 minutes, and confirm pressure drop is no more than 0.5 atmosphere over that time. Recommends renting a pressure-test pump for a day if you don't own one — trivial cost relative to the risk of a slow leak soaking the screed, damaging a downstairs neighbor's unit, and causing hard-to-remove mold/damp. `confirmed`, explicit, genuinely important safety-relevant QC protocol not previously recorded this precisely in this store.
