## Genuinely New Content

- **⚠️ Hard structural constraint: buildings with wooden inter-floor slabs can essentially never use cement-based screed (wet or semi-dry) — only a dry screed (GVL sheet-based) is viable there**, to avoid overloading a wood structure and flood risk to the unit below. **Real building-type nuance**: not every historic "Stalinka" building has wood floor slabs — some do, particularly older city-center buildings under monument protection, where the facade is preserved but the interior (including floor slabs) has been rebuilt over time to accommodate modern additions like elevator shafts or underground parking excavation.
- **Dry screed's three real downsides, one counter-intuitive**: (1) cannot tolerate uneven point loading from furniture/appliances/cabinetry — the loose aggregate fill inside shifts over time under such loading, deforming the floor; (2) a flood requires full demolition and rebuild (trapped moisture under the screed grows mold); (3) **⚠️ despite being weaker/less durable than wet or semi-dry screed, dry screed is actually the *more expensive* of the three options** — a real cost-inversion the source calls out explicitly.
- **Company's own stated preference and reasoning: semi-dry over wet screed, for level accuracy** — wet screed's much higher water content causes significantly more shrinkage/settling as it cures, making it harder to hit an exact target floor level; semi-dry's lower water content settles less, giving more accurate leveling control.
- **⚠️ Wet-screed thickness rule and a keramzit-buildup technique for exceeding it**: minimum 3cm, and the actual mortar layer itself should never exceed 6cm regardless of total desired thickness (overloading the structural slab is the reason). For any total thickness beyond 6cm, build up the excess with expanded-clay (keramzit) fill consolidated with cement milk first, then finish with a mortar layer up to 6cm on top. **Worked example**: a 10cm total screed = 4-5cm keramzit + cement-milk consolidation + 5cm mortar screed on top.
- **⚠️ Semi-dry screed thickness rule, a distinct threshold from wet screed**: minimum 4cm (1cm more than wet screed's minimum); up to 8cm achievable without keramzit; beyond 8cm requires the same keramzit-fill-plus-cement-milk buildup as wet screed, **plus mandatory mesh reinforcement** at that point.
- **⚠️ Three-tier drying schedule with concrete numbers for both screed types, first full comparison for this store**:
  - **Walkable**: wet screed 2-3 days (special stepping boards needed before then); semi-dry screed 12-24 hours.
  - **Tile-layable**: wet screed requires *full* cure before tile can go down at all (tiling onto a still-damp wet screed is explicitly forbidden) — **28 calendar days, or a formula of 7 days per cm of mortar thickness** (a 5cm layer needs 35 days). Semi-dry screed, by contrast, only needs **10-14 days** before tile — most moisture is already out and strength is sufficient, even though the screed isn't fully cured yet.
  - **Final flooring** (laminate, quartz-vinyl, parquet board, etc.): only after full cure for either screed type. Semi-dry's own full cure is slightly faster than wet's — **≈20-21 days (3 weeks)** vs. wet screed's 28 days.
- **⚠️ No universal drying-time standard, an explicit caveat against blindly applying the 28-day figure**: humid or cold rooms can extend full cure meaningfully, up to **35-40 days**, and must be judged per-room condition rather than assuming a fixed calendar number.
- **Top-3 named crack causes, a structured framework**: (1) **bad mix composition** — too much water or inadequate mixing produces an inhomogeneous mix that dries unevenly and cracks; (2) **installation defects** — an overly thick layer poured without reinforcement, or without mandatory fiber additive and plasticizer added to *both* wet and semi-dry screed (this company adds both to every screed regardless of type); (3) **missing deformation joints** — called out as the single most important of the three causes.
- **⚠️ Perimeter damper-tape minimum thickness spec, first numeric figure for this store**: perimeter damper tape must be at least **8mm thick**, in addition to deformation cuts at door openings, every 3-4m in long/narrow rooms, and a diagonal "T"-shaped relief cut at every external corner (this last detail corroborates this page's existing Петришин-Строй content almost exactly, not re-recorded as new).
- **⚠️ Radiator-adjacent extra-watering technique during cure, a genuinely new practical nuance**: if an installed radiator can't be switched off during cold weather while the screed cures, the area immediately around it needs closer monitoring and more frequent/heavier watering than the rest of the room — the radiator's heat accelerates local moisture loss, risking a weaker zone right at that spot if not compensated for.
