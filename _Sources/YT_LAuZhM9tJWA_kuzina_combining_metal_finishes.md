## Mistakes / Warnings — the cascade failure she opens with

- **⚠️ A worked procurement cascade, and it is the most useful part of the source because it is how the problem actually arises** — nobody sets out to mix six metals:
  1. You buy a **gold pendant** for the kitchen before choosing the kitchen.
  2. You then pick facades with a **gold profile** to match it.
  3. Standard hardware has no gold, so you buy **gold handles separately**.
  4. But the built-in appliances have **silver handles** — and appliances with gold handles are very hard to find, so you settle for **black**.
  5. Then you find a mixer roughly matching the facade handles.
  6. **Assembled, nothing matches**: in daylight the "gold" pendant reads as pale gold, almost silver; the mixer is matte; the facade handles are glossy and read "как будто его раскапывали у инков."
- **⚠️ And the obvious fix has its own failure mode**: giving up and using chrome handles, a chrome mixer and matching door handles, leaving the pendant alone — **left to itself that reads as a random set.** Neither forcing a match nor abandoning the attempt works; the answer is hierarchy.
- **Her named negative example**: a kitchen deliberately mixing matte chrome (hood, fridge), matte gold (mixer, base handles), bright glossy gold (bottle pull-out), bright gold (small upper handles) and blackened gold (chairs). **Why it fails: no metal repeats twice and none dominates** — "просто разрозненный набор."
- **The bathroom version is subtler and worth knowing**: matching a mixer to a towel warmer, **two chromes turn out to be visibly different shades.**

## Materials — the metal colour taxonomy

She is explicit this is **about colour only**, not the actual alloy, hardness or chemistry.

- **Warm**: gold, brass («латунь»), bronze, copper — **and black, which she classes as warm by default.**
  - **⚠️ Warm metals are easier to work with because the differences between them are larger** — you will not confuse reddish copper with bright gold. **Gold is brighter; brass paler and calmer** — though a satin finish shifts this.
- **Cold**: **chrome is the coldest** (from its white cast), silver slightly warmer, then **nickel and titanium warmer still** (titanium slightly darker than nickel).
- **⚠️ Nickel, titanium and "platinum" sit in the middle and behave relatively**: grouped with colder metals they read warm; grouped with warm metals they read cold. **Nickel combines with warm metals better than silver or chrome do.**
- **"Platinum" here means a fashionable gold/silver mix**, which Italians call *argento antico* / aged silver — **despite there being no actual ageing; it is simply a pale-gold shade.**
- **⚠️ Naming warning, and it is a real purchasing trap: manufacturers use the same words for different finishes.** A maker with brass but no gold **will very likely label the brass "gold,"** because it is the yellow metal in their range. **Brass is often sold as "champagne" or "champagne gold."** It depends entirely on the supplier — so the name on the spec sheet is not the finish.
- **Surface treatments, which matter as much as colour**: full gloss; matte (highlights do not play, light reflects evenly, **you cannot see your reflection — it is not a mirror**); artificially blackened or aged (oxidation applied over the top, reading even more matte and muted); or tinted — black, ivory, white, imitation rust, **or a greenish oxidised patina, which adds volume.**
- **⚠️ The mix includes anything with metallic sheen, not only metal objects**: textiles, wallpaper with sheen, metallised-plastic worktops ("не дай бог"), metallised inserts on doors, **and tile imitating rust** — leaning bronze or silver, it enters your metal count whether you intended it or not.
- Symbolism she offers in passing: gold ↔ sun ↔ masculine, silver ↔ moon ↔ feminine.

## Design Concept — hierarchy, which is the core rule

- **⚠️ Decide the goal first**: is metal merely a purchasing simplification (not a design-forming element), or is the design deliberately built on a metal mix? Her examples mostly illustrate the second.
- **⚠️ The dominant metal should occupy roughly 70% — two-thirds — of all metallised items; the accent metal takes the rest.**
- **⚠️ Her contrast pair makes the threshold concrete.** A bathroom with **only two metals** — matte brass ~60%, glossy chrome ~40% — **still does not work, because near-half-and-half has no dominant.** The successful comparison: silvery-sheen wallpaper + matte-nickel sconces + matte-silver mixer + the visible waste, **70–80% in one family**, with **a rose-gold mirror at ~20–30% as the accent** — which is the first thing you see.
- **⚠️ Count by room size: two metals in small rooms, up to four in large ones** (a combined kitchen-dining-living). **The reason is object count**: in a small room there are not enough objects, so each ends up carrying its own finish.
- **⚠️ Several objects must share each finish** — at minimum several in the dominant metal and several in the accent; at most, several dominant + several accent + one or two others.

## Design Concept — which colours actually combine

- **⚠️ The governing finding: metals combine well when they are *different*.** Different in colour (warm + cold), different in texture (glossy + matte), **or anything plus black.**
- **Warm + cold works regardless of finish** — glossy with glossy, glossy with matte, matte with matte. **What matters is that one is warm and one is cold.**
- **Different warm metals combine well** because they differ enough in colour to be told apart — e.g. bright gold as the accent (a pendant) with quieter brass on table and chair legs, which do not compete for attention.
- **The same works in cold metals** — silver plus a matte silver (silver foil, say) is a calm combination, **well suited where the floors or wallpaper are already active.**
- **⚠️ Black is the universal joiner: "чёрный сочетается с любым металлом."** If one element is black, everything else will go with it — the result reads graphic. Her example: a black cast-iron staircase at ~70%, chrome chair bases, gold handles on classical furniture.
- **⚠️ Two *similar* colours fail — "два похожих цвета будет выглядеть просто как ошибка,"** reading as a failed attempt to match everything to gold. **Worse in cold metals: silver + nickel, silver + chrome, or chrome + nickel will very likely fail if they share the same surface treatment.** This is the reason two chromes clash in the bathroom example above.
- **A composition note she makes twice**: where the object mix is complex, **set it against a very simple background** so the eye examines the objects rather than competing with the wallpaper. Conversely, where gold is the lead, keep the background deliberately quiet so it comes forward.

## Design Concept — how to distribute them

- **⚠️ Distribute the dominant metal evenly around the room.** In her example gold is not a large quantity in total, **but it appears on the central table, on accessories on the chest, on cushions, and on the visible curtain bracket** — so it is unmistakably the lead. Two supporting metals (white on the pendant, black on an armchair) complete it. **"Основной металл распределён по всему пространству, собирает пространство вместе."**
- **⚠️ Distribute by height — a rule she offers while doubting it.** Working top to bottom: ceiling fixtures, curtain brackets, sconces, table lamps → door handles, window and cabinet hardware → side tables and furniture legs. Assign finishes by band — all pendants black, all sconces silver, all legs brass. **If splitting into two groups rather than three, assign the middle band arbitrarily to the top or the bottom.** **She notes candidly that most examples she found did not follow this**, which is why it is recorded as a suggestion rather than a rule.
- **⚠️ There must be something in common between objects in different finishes — and this is the most usable of the three.** Shared style is one route, but she gives two stronger devices:
  - **Several fixtures of the same form in different sizes and different finishes** work well together, and then make it easy to introduce further silver and copper items, **because those fixtures bind them.**
  - **A chandelier that itself contains several finishes** (silver + gold + crystals + perhaps chrome) means **gold side tables and silver side tables both work**, and any other fixture in the zone can be any of those colours — **the chandelier ties them together.**
- **Origin note**: the mixed-metals idea came into interiors from jewellery, where wearing a red-gold ring with a yellow-gold chain was once considered bad form and is now fashionable.
