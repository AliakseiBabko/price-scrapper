## Quantities / Measurements — what "low ceiling" actually means, and where the centimetres go

- **⚠️ The problem is mostly self-inflicted by the build-up, and she quantifies it.** The highest new-build ceiling she has personally heard of is **320 cm** — and **~20 cm of it goes to screed and plasterboard ceilings**, with a ceiling-void ventilation system taking more. **"Потолок неожиданно из 3,20 превращается в 2,90 или меньше."** She notes drily that developers already advertise 3 m as "super-high."
- **⚠️ Ceiling-void cost by construction type — the three figures that decide the lighting strategy**:
  - **Recessed fixtures: the ceiling drops a minimum of 8 cm.**
  - **Plasterboard ceiling with no recessed fixtures: ~5 cm.**
  - **A good-brand stretch ceiling (she names Clipso): ~3 cm** — conditional on the slab above being reasonably flat.
- **Cornice height: anything under 10 cm she considers small, and never go below 5 cm.** A 4 cm ceiling cornice "выглядит очень смешно." **If a proper cornice will not fit, fit none at all** rather than a token one.
- **Cove lighting needs a minimum 5 cm from the cornice to the ceiling** for the light to escape — which is exactly what makes the standard implementation fail (below).

## Walls / Ceilings — colour and finish

- **⚠️ White does not make a ceiling look highest — she names this as a misconception.** The highest-reading ceiling is one **painted the wall colour**, and better still **half a tone to a tone lighter** than the walls.
- **The mechanism, which is why the difference must be small**: the ceiling is almost always in shadow while the walls may be lit, **so it needs to be only imperceptibly lighter.** Demonstrated on paint swatches — the pair with the *smaller* tonal gap beats the more contrasting pair.
- **Gloss or semi-gloss ceiling paint helps** — light reflects in attractive drifts across it. **She would choose semi-gloss.**
- **⚠️ But not a glossy stretch ceiling — "это выглядит просто как пластик." If stretch, matte only.** The gloss recommendation is specifically about paint.
- **⚠️ Glossy floors also raise the perceived ceiling** — the wall appears to continue downward. **Works best where fixtures come close to the floor and reflect in it, or where low windowsills let the window reflect.** The same effect runs upward: a window close to the ceiling reflects better in it.
- **Do not**: paint a mural on the ceiling (**including perspective/3D sky-and-clouds — "этот потолок лежит практически уже на вас"**), add beams, add abundant stucco, or paint the ceiling in several colours. All pull attention upward.

## Walls / Ceilings — hiding the junction, which is the core principle

- **⚠️ The governing idea: the eye must not be able to locate the exact wall/ceiling line, because that line is what defines the wall's height.** Everything below follows from it. What is needed is a radius at that junction.
- **Options for the radius**: a furniture cornice, or a plaster/moulding cornice covering the junction. **Choose the simplest profiles.**
  - **⚠️ Two selection criteria, both about minimising the visible step**: the shallower the cornice's lowest point the better, and the smaller its height where it meets the ceiling the better — the transition then reads smoother.
  - **⚠️ Cornice geometry rule: pick one that extends further onto the ceiling than it occupies on the wall** — low height, large depth. **Some cornices can be inverted** to convert height into depth.
- **A plaster radius with no cornice at all.** The radius is small but effective. **Historical note she offers as reassurance: this was standard in cheap pre-revolutionary flats precisely because there was no money for a cornice** — and it looks good, so it is worth using deliberately.
- **⚠️ A consequence that is easy to get wrong: with a radius you physically cannot paint wall and ceiling different colours.** Doing so re-establishes the line you just erased, and looks messy because there is no depth change to cut the paint against. **Default: with a radius, ceiling and wall are one colour.**
- **The alternative if you want two colours: a gradient — with the top of the gradient, on the wall, matching the ceiling colour.**
- **⚠️ A subtler version that needs no construction at all**: the junction goes unnoticed if **the lower part of the wall is more colourful** — the eye is drawn there and spends its time hunting for the colour transition, which is deliberately **not** at the wall/ceiling junction.
- **"Flying ceiling" («летящий потолок»)** — detach the ceiling from the walls with a shadow gap, with or without perimeter light, creating a dark recess the eye cannot read. **Shadow profiles are made for this; she tried one and it failed on quality** ("отвратного качества") and expects the market to improve. A modernist effect.
- **Cornice plus a ceiling moulding in tandem** also blurs the junction — **provided the moulding sits close enough to the cornice to read as part of it.**
- **Do not**: leave wall and ceiling in different colours with no cornice (it accentuates the junction), or use a **coloured cornice** — that points at exactly the place you want unnoticed. **The cornice should match the wall.**

## Lighting — and a strong caution on cove lighting

- **⚠️ Cove / behind-cornice lighting («закарнизная подсветка») is largely self-defeating, and she gives both an observation and a mechanism.** Her observation: **the more expensive the project, the less cove lighting it contains** — she sees almost none in leading European designers' work.
- **The mechanism**: the standard product is a hollow cornice with an LED strip, needing ≥5 cm to the ceiling for light to escape — **so it illuminates precisely the wall/ceiling junction you were trying to hide.** "Такой вариант не подходит."
- **⚠️ Two working alternatives**: a cornice already shaped with a bend that conceals the strip (effectively a double cornice), **or buy the cornice and a separate light moulding and put the light moulding *under* the cornice — so the light washes the cornice, not the junction.**
- **⚠️ The chandelier question: the common move — find a lower chandelier so it does not hit your head, and centre it — is wrong. Better to drop the chandelier entirely and use technical light.**
- **Track lighting, with a rebuttal she volunteers**: she has read many comments calling tracks "office style" or inherently contemporary. **"Немножко не так" — tracks fit essentially any style, including classical.** She particularly likes **tracks dropped away from the ceiling** — a thin thread running the perimeter of a large room, unifying several zones and lighting all of them. Tracks that run the ceiling and then descend the wall exist but are a very committing contemporary choice.
- **⚠️ Always specify a diffuser with an LED profile. Never buy a profile without one** — even in a niche where the strip is not directly in view — **because the strip's joints and its corner joints show.**
- **Where the ceiling void is unavailable: surface-mounted fixtures** (surface cylinders). **Caveat: they come mostly in white and black, sometimes silver** — so with non-white walls or ceilings, check they will not draw attention.
- **LED strip without a light cornice**: profiles made to sit between ceiling and wall throwing light down the wall; or a perimeter run bright enough to **replace the chandelier entirely.**
- **Ceiling niches with concealed indirect light as a chandelier replacement** — costs perhaps 10 cm, but no chandelier is only 10 cm tall, so the trade is favourable. The sophisticated version throws light sideways from under a lip and reflects it down. **Needs genuinely skilled builders.**
- **Replacing the chandelier's decorative role, since technical light alone can read as bare**: pendants along the walls (conventionally in bedrooms over nightstands, but equally over living-room coffee tables); **a full chandelier hung in a corner** rather than centrally, with proportions calculated against what it hangs over; and **large wall fixtures — too big to call sconces — which pair well with ceiling technical light and make a chandelier redundant.**
- **⚠️ Do not use a chandelier with a downward-facing opaque shade** — it casts a shadow line around the room's perimeter, **adding another horizontal line**, which is the opposite of the goal. **Prefer an upward-facing shade, and sconces that throw light onto the ceiling.**

## Furniture / Built-ins

- **The task is the same as with walls: add verticals and distract from the ceiling.**
- **⚠️ The counter-intuitive furniture-height rule, and it is the most transferable item here.** It seems that low furniture suits a low room. **What actually matters is the proportion between the furniture's height and the strip of wall above it — the two must be noticeably different.** Either the furniture is much taller with a band of wall above, or much lower with more wall above. **"Пополам" — half and half — is the worst possible case.**
- **⚠️ And do not use low furniture in a *small* room** — a low ceiling is not the same problem as a small room. In a 100 m² room with ribbon windows and a view, low furniture is excellent because it emphasises the horizontal; **she cites Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie House as the deliberate version of this.** In an ordinary ~20 m² room it fails.
  - **Her analogy for why**: low furniture in a small room is like cropped trousers — superb on the few people built for them, and on everyone else they simply read as ill-fitting or outgrown. **"Я бы ставила скорее обычную мебель, чем специально искала низкую."**
- **Verticals that work**: mirror inserts (she flags the behind-the-nightstand version as common and rather banal); **elongated niches with fixtures or shelves inside**; and a projecting wall section built into the ceiling with a light niche around its contour — effectively a risalit with perimeter ceiling light, which she suggests using behind a bed's headboard.
- **Niches with shelving dividing a wall into three vertical segments** — shown in combination with same-colour wall and ceiling and recessed lighting instead of a pendant.
- **Bedroom: a headboard running to the ceiling, or soft wall panels acting as one — and composed of several vertical segments** so it carries upward movement rather than reading as one block.
- **Furniture on invisible legs (clear acrylic) adds airiness.** It does not stretch the room upward, but it lightens it — she cites Starck's Ghost chair and notes transparent furniture and "airiness" as a current direction.

## Doors / Trim

- **⚠️ Use the door to stretch the wall — a door is already a vertical element, so take it further.** Either specify a genuinely tall door (**expensive**), **or fit a standard-height door and pay for an over-panel** finished to match the door leaf or the architrave, carrying the opening up to the ceiling.
- **⚠️ Hard incompatibility worth flagging: this only works where there is no ceiling cornice.** It presupposes a straight wall/ceiling junction, so **it is mutually exclusive with the cornice-radius approach above** — pick one strategy per room and use the other tools (ceiling colour, lighting) alongside whichever you choose.

## Curtains and Textiles

- **Choose simple textiles** in a low room.
- **⚠️ Hide the curtains in a curtain niche.** Where a cornice exists, use its niche. **Where there is no ceiling cornice, build a cornice only at the window where the niche is needed** — still better than an exposed rod, however attractive the rod.
- **If a rod is unavoidable, push it as close to the ceiling as possible**, even where there is a lot of wall above the window — what matters is raising the curtains' top edge.
- **Better still: a ceiling-mounted curtain track**, so the curtains begin at the ceiling. Usually unattractive, which is why niches exist, but tidier versions can be found — **or hide the track behind the fabric's folds.** This is the right answer in the no-cornice case.
- **⚠️ Do not use a pelmet («ламбрекен»), and probably not tie-backs («подхваты»)** — emphasise falling fabric with **vertical folds** instead, which adds vertical lines.

## Design Concept — the worked example that combines the techniques

She closes on a room stacking five of the above at once, and it is worth recording as a pattern rather than as separate rules: **ceiling and wall in one colour; the ceiling glossy; a small distance above the window so the window visually continues into the ceiling; curtains and their fixing hidden in a niche so the fabric reads unbroken to the ceiling and reflects upward** — "комната кажется просто в два раза выше, чем она есть на самом деле" — **plus bright orange colour spots distributed around the room to pull attention away from the junction.**

**She also notes all of these work as pure decorative devices in rooms with adequate ceilings**, to add air — they are not only remedial.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- Assuming a white ceiling reads highest.
- A glossy **stretch** ceiling; ceiling murals, beams, heavy stucco, multi-colour ceilings.
- Standard cove lighting, which lights the junction it was meant to hide.
- A cornice under 5 cm; a coloured cornice; different wall and ceiling colours with no cornice.
- An LED profile with no diffuser.
- A downward-facing opaque chandelier shade.
- Furniture whose height splits the wall roughly in half.
- Deliberately hunting for low furniture in a small room.
- Pelmets and exposed curtain rods.
- Combining a ceiling-height door with a ceiling cornice.
