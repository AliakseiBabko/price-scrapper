## Plumbing / HVAC — In-Floor Convector Risks

- **Incompatibility with central heating, mechanism-explained**: an in-floor convector's plumbing routes through a longer, more convoluted path (more joints, more narrowing) than a standard radiator, producing higher hydraulic resistance. In a shared central-heating riser, hot water preferentially flows through whichever path offers least resistance — so it bypasses a convector with a complex routing and flows to neighboring units' simpler, lower-resistance radiators instead, leaving the convector cold regardless of the overall system being functional. On a one-pipe scheme, capping the bypass to force water through the convector produces the same outcome as installing water underfloor heating illegally: your unit gets warm, neighbors' units go cold, followed by neighbor complaints, an inspection, and a mandated 3-day restoration order.
- **Legal status matches water underfloor heating exactly, independent of the hydraulic problem above**: installing an in-floor convector is legally equivalent to installing water underfloor heating (both route heating medium through the floor slab) — categorically prohibited on a standard central-heating connection, same enforcement consequences (inspection, fines, mandated restoration) as described elsewhere in this store for illegal loggia radiator relocation.
- **Even where technically routable (modern push-fit plastic piping, correctly sized), only the most powerful fan-assisted convector models work reliably in a central-Russia climate** — installation depth matters directly: 80mm and 100mm depth convectors are stated to not work adequately in this climate at all (window condensation, ambient room temperature capped around 17°C, resulting damp/mold on walls, floor, and ceiling); only 120mm-depth units with a supplementary fan perform adequately.
- **Fan-assisted units carry their own cost and reliability problems**: adds electrical-wiring complexity (power supply, switching) and a genuinely noisy operating fan (not silent, contrary to marketing claims for "quiet" fan models); a quiet-fan convector model starts around **50,000 RUB and reaches 200,000 RUB**, versus **under 5,000 RUB for a comparable-output bimetal radiator** — 2019 Moscow pricing, `single-account`, `unverified`. Fan components themselves have short rated lifespans (no manufacturer offers more than a 1-year warranty on the fan specifically) and are typically not sold as standalone replacement parts, forcing a full convector replacement on fan failure.
- **Screed-depth/weight cost mechanism**: fitting a 120mm-deep convector box into a typical ~5cm baseline screed plan requires adding roughly 3 bags of screed mix per m² just to reach the needed depth — worked example: ~320 bags (~13 tonnes) of additional screed material for a 100m² apartment, several full truck-loads beyond what a standard renovation would otherwise need — while also lowering the finished ceiling height by roughly 7cm. **Compounding constraint**: the apartment's entrance-level floor must still match the building corridor's floor level (see this store's existing zero-reference system content), and the source estimates roughly 70% of apartments simply don't have enough spare screed-height budget to accommodate a 120mm convector box at all without violating that entrance-level constraint.
- **Leak-detection delay, a distinct risk from a standard radiator**: a standard radiator leak is visible immediately (a growing puddle on the visible floor surface); an in-floor convector leak is invisible until water has already saturated the underlayment beneath the finish flooring and risen above the convector's own installed level — by which point flooding of the unit below is effectively guaranteed, versus a standard radiator leak that's typically caught and stopped well before reaching that point.
- **Leak-repair difficulty**: a standard radiator leak can be addressed with an ordinary wrench on visible, accessible fittings; an in-floor convector's fittings sit inside the recessed box, submerged in hot, opaque water during a leak, with sharp edges — described as a genuinely unpleasant, difficult repair versus the equivalent radiator scenario. Combined with the fan's mains electrical connection sitting in the same recessed, potentially flooded box, the source states this is functionally equivalent to submerging live household wiring in water.
- **Maintenance/cleaning**: dirt and dust accumulate inside the recessed grille housing and are awkward to clean out.
- **Laminate-expansion incompatibility, same underlying mechanism as this store's existing floor-radiator content** (`yt_WBaKEl5HIzU`, `yt_-Kh9JZ34zRc`, same chunk), with a genuinely new failure detail: a convector's decorative grille trim is only ~5mm wide, far narrower than laminate's typical expansion allowance — on roughly half of real installations the source has serviced, the laminate shrinks enough over time that a visible gap opens around the housing (a cosmetic defect); on the other half, the laminate expands enough to crush against the housing and jam the decorative grille solid — this second failure mode is the direct precondition for the safety incident below.
- **Documented physical-injury risk, escalating severity**: exposed convector edges snag and tear socks/stockings, and can cut bare feet; stepping directly on a convector grille (especially a metal one) can break it and cause the foot to fall through, a genuine injury risk; small pets (the source's example: small dogs) can catch a leg in the grille slats and injure themselves attempting to keep moving. **The most serious documented case, described by the source as an active, ongoing lawsuit against the convector's manufacturer at time of filming**: a convector grille jammed solid by laminate expansion (per the mechanism above) trapped a dropped chain/necklace; a young child attempting to help retrieve it got their fingers wedged and permanently stuck in the jammed grille; multiple household attempts to free the fingers (including soaking with water and vegetable oil) and a neighbor's help all failed; emergency responders were called and ultimately had to cut/dismantle the grille in sections — the process took long enough that the child's fingers were lost. `single-account`, described by the source as a real, specific, still-litigated case (not a hypothetical), but not independently verified by this extraction beyond the source's own account. This is the most severe safety-relevant fact recorded in this store to date and should be treated as a genuine, high-priority Mistakes/Warnings entry, not a minor caution.
- **Bottom-line recommendation, stated at the close**: avoid in-floor convectors wherever a normal wall-mounted radiator (concealed behind a false wall if desired) is feasible instead.
