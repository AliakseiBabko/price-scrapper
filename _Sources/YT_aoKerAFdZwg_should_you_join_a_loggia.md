## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's core physics point**: glazing and insulation alone, without an active heat source, do not make a loggia warm — any cladding only radiates heat if literally set on fire; otherwise it only slows heat loss. Without a heat source, a well-glazed/insulated loggia will still equalize toward outdoor temperature over time, the same way an apartment with its radiators removed would.
- **Zemskov's rule against moving a central-heating radiator onto a loggia**: illegal, carries real fines and a mandated 3-day restoration order — use a standalone electric heat source instead.
- **Zemskov's floor-heating recommendation for a separately-kept (not joined) loggia**: a convector or floor-standing radiator only heats the air directly above itself, leaving the floor — the coldest surface — untouched; **his recommended fix is thin-film electric underfloor heating specifically for the loggia**, since it's the one heat source that actually addresses the coldest zone.
- **Zemskov explicitly debunks a common belief**: "my downstairs neighbor already insulated their loggia, so my floor slab benefits from their heat" is false — a neighbor's insulation sits on **their own ceiling**, retaining **their own** heat; it does nothing to warm the slab above them. Your loggia floor stays cold regardless of what a downstairs neighbor has done.
- **Zemskov's three real drawbacks of running loggia floor heating only intermittently (not continuously, given Russia needs ~7 months/year of heating and running it constantly is expensive even at film-heating's relatively low draw)**: (1) minimum ~1 hour from cold start to comfortable temperature; (2) opening the loggia door causes instant condensation on the cold glazing, requiring venting with cold outside air to clear it — which further delays warm-up; (3) users tend to crank the thermostat to maximum to speed things up, which burns out both the thermostat and the heating elements faster than normal use would.
- **Zemskov's case FOR joining (removing the door/wall between loggia and room)**: uses air already heated by central heating (already paid for) instead of adding a separate electric heating cost; merges two small windowsills into one larger usable countertop/surface; increases natural light, since both the balcony door/window unit's own frame and its central post (where door meets window) block some light when left in place — removing them opens the room and lets in noticeably more light. Framed as making a small room feel bigger, brighter, and more open.
- **Zemskov's real degradation case against leaving the loggia unjoined but propping the door open for warmth instead**: (a) a permanently propped-open door blocks kitchen fridge access, bedroom curtain access, and the room's light switch (typically mounted on the door's hinge side — the window occupies the other side, leaving no room for a switch there); (b) **a glazed loggia door left permanently open will physically deform within roughly 6 months and stop closing at all.** Mechanism: when closed, the door's full weight (real weight, given the glass unit) rests on the frame; left open, that same weight instead pulls continuously on the hinges, gradually deforming the sash geometry until the lower corner starts catching/dragging on the frame. Hinge-adjustment screws have finite range to compensate — once exhausted (~6 months in), the door can no longer close and is often removed entirely, wasting the money spent on it and leaving exposed frame/hardware edges as a trip hazard.
- **Zemskov's real drawbacks of joining, to weigh against the case for it**: 
  1. **Joining cannot realistically be legally permitted.** Proper registration would require heat-loss calculations, engineering surveys, and paperwork on the scale of an entire building's construction permit — nobody actually completes this in practice, so joining is always technically an unregistered risk. Zemskov calls this a justified risk if you don't plan to sell, but flags a real workaround if you do: install the cheapest available plastic frame back into the opening before a sale or inspection — "formal glazing" is present, satisfying a surface-level inspection, without genuinely undoing the joined space behind it.
  2. **A joined room is always ~3-4°C colder than the rest of the apartment** — more glazed area unavoidably means more heat loss. Framed as a non-issue in a kitchen (often overheated anyway) but a **real problem in a bedroom or kids' room**, especially for someone cold-sensitive or a child with weaker immunity — Zemskov compares the effect to a persistent draft or AC cold-air blast, not merely "slightly less cozy." **His explicit rule: if you're personally cold-sensitive, never join a loggia to a room you occupy regularly.**
  3. **Joining brings noticeably more street noise into the adjoining room** — a real tradeoff if quiet matters to the household.
  4. **A loggia turned into a home office adjoining a bedroom, once joined, transmits noise (typing, gaming, voice chat) that disrupts a sleeping partner** — his explicit recommendation: don't join a loggia intended as an office if it's adjacent to a room someone sleeps in.
- **Zemskov's explicit "never join these loggia types" list**: (1) a long, narrow loggia whose post-cladding usable width comes out under 75cm — useless as a joined space (can't fit a desk, and even hanging laundry needs ~60cm for the rack plus clearance to approach it, leaving only 10-15cm to spare); (2) a small corner loggia — nothing meaningfully fits, so joining only adds cost/heat-loss/noise for no real benefit; (3) a complex/irregular ("broken") shaped loggia that can't be furnished or used rationally even if its total area is large; (4) **most important — a loggia with continuous facade-glazing shared across the whole building's height (floor 1 through the top floor as one unified system)**. This type is made of cold aluminum profile, is shared building infrastructure that can't be cut apart to insulate just one unit's section, can't even have its individual glazing units swapped for warmer ones, and its junction to the floor slab can't be insulated at all since the glazing doesn't rest on the slab — it hangs external to the building with roughly a 10cm gap to the slab. Practically impossible to insulate within any reasonable budget — **an unconditional "don't join" case**, independent of the size/shape considerations above.
