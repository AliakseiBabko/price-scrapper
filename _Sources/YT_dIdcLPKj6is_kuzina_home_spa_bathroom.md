## Design Concept — the programme, and why partial application fails

- **⚠️ The load-bearing claim: advice on "making your bathroom a spa" is superficial, and applying one or two items produces no spa effect at all. Most of the recommendations must be executed together.** An all-or-nothing programme rather than a list of nice touches — this is what distinguishes the source from generic content.
- **The three groups she organises everything into: (1) tactile comfort, (2) sound and smell, (3) visual comfort.** A usable checklist structure for specifying a bathroom.
- **Historical framing she uses to justify the material emphasis**: Greek baths and Roman thermae — mosaic and marble, with sports halls, libraries and gardens attached. **What survives into modern spas is two things: an emphasis on material, and the assumption that a person spends a long time inside.** The second is the design consequence — a bathroom programmed for a long stay is a different room from one programmed for a quick one.

## Design Concept — tactile comfort (group 1)

- **⚠️ The disqualifying detail, stated as a hard gate: if you stand barefoot on porcelain or stone, the floor must be heated. Cold floor → "вы не в спа, а просто в санузле"** and you hurry into the shower. **Underfloor heating is a precondition, not an upgrade, if the spa effect is the goal.**
- Everything touching the body counts — towels fresh, soft, natural.
- **Her own material preference, given as preference**: a **velvety semi-matte porcelain**, and **composite bath materials with a matte, velvety-to-the-touch surface.** She explicitly allows the opposite taste (porous or fine-grained floors with a smooth bath).
- **⚠️ The transferable technique: tactile contrast, including on surfaces you never touch.** Spas play surfaces against each other; the *perceived* smoothness of the bath comes from the roughness of what surrounds it.
  - Her worked examples: **very rough stone or formwork-marked concrete walls against wood floors and a smooth bath** — the roughness is what makes the bath read as perfectly smooth; a bath appearing cut from a single rock with the roughness left on its outer contour; **pebble surfacing behind the bath rather than on the floor** — hard to maintain, but far worse underfoot, and placing it out of contact is precisely what creates the effect of imagining stepping on it.

## Design Concept — steam rooms, materials and types

- **Two families: stone (hammam, thermae) and timber (Finnish sauna, Russian banya).**
- **Timber lining suits traditional steam rooms: aspen (осина), lime (липа), cedar (кедр). ⚠️ Cedar is particularly valued because on heating it releases natural essential oils**, and it has a wide colour range.
- **⚠️ Timber is unsuitable for a hammam because of the high humidity.** Traditional curved forms are clad in tile or mosaic; traditional ornament strengthens the effect.

**Humidity determines the ventilation problem — her ranking, which is the most useful engineering content here:**

| Type | Humidity | Temperature | Ventilation consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Infrared cabin** | **no steam at all** | **up to 60 °C** | **⚠️ Safest option.** Heats by infrared energy |
| **Finnish sauna** | **up to 15 %** | **up to 100 °C** | **Natural ventilation may still suffice.** But usually larger than an IR cabin |
| **Hammam / Turkish** | **up to 100 %** | **optimal 45–65 °C** | **⚠️ Forced ventilation and natural air supply are mandatory** |

- **⚠️ The general rule: the higher the humidity, the bigger the ventilation problem — and getting it wrong produces smell, fungus or mould.**
- **The Turkish steam room has the fewest contraindications** at 45–65 °C: relaxes muscles, deep-cleans pores, good for joints, normalises sleep, reduces stress.
- **⚠️ Cost and space note (directional, not costed): full hammam equipment is expensive and can take a lot of space in a flat.** Her named alternative: **a shower cabin with a hammam function — necessarily closed top and bottom — with a separately-purchased steam generator**, giving either a hammam or a hotter steam bath depending on the unit chosen.

- **Permitted in a flat: hammams, infrared cabins and individual saunas — factory-assembled units only.**

## Fixtures / Equipment — massage and water

- **Built-in shower jet systems** — choose cabins with nozzle systems directing water at parts of the body.
- **Whirlpool baths: ⚠️ harder to install and heavier to maintain than ordinary ones, and must be used at least weekly to keep the system working.** She asks viewers who own one how often they actually use it — clearly sceptical that they do.
- Indications she cites for hydromassage: nervous-system conditions, oedema, chronic fatigue syndrome, low tone; also improves skin condition.
- **⚠️ The cheap substitute if the bath is already installed: a multi-mode shower head.** Her reasoning is specific — **many heads mix water with air, and they do it differently**; she rates **Hansgrohe Raindance** highly on that basis. Some have intense massage jets, some move, some change temperature for a contrast shower.
- **A foot bath** — vibration, hydromassage or built-in rollers; relieves fatigue, stimulates circulation.

## Fixtures / Equipment — sound and smell (group 2)

- **Radio/MP3 can be built into any sanitaryware and even into the bathroom mirror** — **⚠️ she is openly dismissive: "хорошо подходит для тех, кто любит успешно решать самостоятельно созданные проблемы."**
- **The more traditional approach is speakers in the ceiling.** Portable waterproof speakers exist, including floating round ones with backlighting; some work to 2 m depth, which she notes is of no use in a bathroom.
- **⚠️ Her real point, and the one with a construction consequence: the absence of unwanted sound matters more than the presence of music.** Her examples — the washing machine starting its spin, a TV audible through the door. **She explicitly does not propose soundproofing; she proposes scheduling and agreement with the household.** Recorded as stated, because it is a cheaper answer than the one a designer could bill for.
- Because hearing, smell and touch work together, therapeutic equipment often carries extra options — **aroma-oil compartments in whirlpool baths and steam generators**, sound effects and lighting in smart showers.
- **⚠️ Smell caveats, given as practical limits**: most scent is processed associatively via the brain, so **a pleasant, beneficial scent with negative associations should not be used.** Allergic reactions to watch. **Essential oils must be diluted.** **An aroma bath: no more than 15 minutes, water no hotter than 36 °C.**

## Lighting — visual comfort (group 3)

- **⚠️ Lighting must be planned in advance** — the strongest lighting claim in the source.
- **Bright, cool light for waking** (imitating sunlight, especially important in the dark season); **soft, warm light for relaxing.** **⚠️ The conclusion she draws: you must control both brightness and colour temperature in a spa zone** — via a dimmer.
- **⚠️ «Dim to warm» named as the specific feature to look for** in modern fittings, especially recessed spots and LED strip: bright output is cool, dimmed output becomes warmer. A directly actionable specification item.
- **Alternative: keep the main ceiling light, and add warmer diffuse perimeter lighting.**
- **On colour/chromotherapy she is candid: she does not believe the therapeutic claims** (green relaxes, red stimulates) — *"это всё очень относительно"* — **but allows that watching one shade fade into another under the shower may simply be pleasant.**
- **⚠️ The finish consequence, which is the actionable part of the colour section: some finishes look very bad under coloured light. If you plan to use RGB, specify plain cream walls and grey floors.**
- **A solution she rates**: a stretch ceiling backlit by LED strip through the membrane, giving an even wash, switchable between colour and white. Seen in a showroom; she found it very aesthetic.

## Design Concept — what you actually look at (group 3 continued)

- **The bath should look clean and tidy but not sterile.**
- **⚠️ Her rebuttal of the standard advice** (plants in the bathroom, scrubs and creams on open shelves, towels beautifully stacked in the open): **that works in a salon because trained staff spend the whole day re-folding the towels.** *"Если вы согласны тоже так делать — тогда окей."* Otherwise store as in an ordinary bathroom.
- **⚠️ The storage rule she does give: in the open, leave only what is needed every five minutes. Everything else goes behind closed doors** — a calm, relaxing atmosphere cannot survive scattered small objects.
- **⚠️ Plan the position of decor in advance.** Her worked example: **decorative candles sit badly on the bath rim; a wider horizontal surface next to the bath is needed — one that does not obstruct getting into the bath.**
- **If the dimensions allow, include things atypical of a bathroom**: an armchair, a small sofa, a small table — **useful when applying body cream or doing a wrap.** Also a picture, a vase, a sculpture or other artwork.
- **⚠️ The point of all of it, stated as a status change: "вы повышаете статус ванны от обыкновенного санузла до комнаты."** Programming the bathroom as a *room* rather than as sanitary space is the actual design move; the fittings follow from it.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- Applying one or two spa touches and expecting the effect.
- An unheated stone or porcelain floor in a room programmed for barefoot use.
- Timber lining in a hammam.
- A high-humidity steam room without forced ventilation — smell, fungus, mould.
- A whirlpool bath used less than weekly.
- RGB lighting over finishes that read badly under coloured light.
- Open storage of everything, on the assumption a salon's presentation is maintainable at home.
- **Treating any of the regulatory content as applicable outside Russia.**
