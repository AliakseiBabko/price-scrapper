## Genuinely New Content

1. **Three-function framework for baseboards, first structured framing for this store**: (1) cover the gap where flooring meets the wall — though some flooring types leave no such gap at all; (2) protect that wall-floor junction zone from mechanical damage during ordinary use (foot scuffs, vacuum-cleaner impacts, dragged furniture/rugs) — otherwise the fastest-damaged part of any wall; (3) **for floating flooring specifically**, the baseboard's own weight/pressure holds the floating floor's edge down against the substrate, working with the floor's thermal-expansion/contraction cycle. A fourth, purely visual function: creates a perceived boundary between floor and wall plane, easier for the eye to parse — explicitly called secondary/non-essential.
2. **⚠️ Seven-baseboard-type taxonomy, first structured version for this store**: (1) PVC/plastic (often with an integrated cable channel); (2) rigid pre-finished baseboard family — MDF, veneer, duropolymer, polyurethane foam, either ready-finished or paint-ready (grouped as one family, ~90% of all baseboards installed per the source); (3) tile baseboard (cut from the same floor tile); (4) concealed-mount baseboard (flush with the wall, effectively invisible, but still functionally a baseboard); (5) shadow baseboard/profile (a channel recessed into the wall, finished with joint compound/paint/wallpaper — **the source states this does not actually perform the baseboard function at all**, functioning instead as a stylistic reveal/gap); (6) aluminum baseboard (rare); (7) micro-baseboard/corner strip (a simple L-angle profile).
3. **⚠️ Installation-cost ranking across all seven types**: cheapest — PVC (simplest, fastest install, dowel-nailed). Mid-cost — aluminum (glued to a finished wall), the rigid pre-finished family (clip-mounted or dowel-nailed, with holes to fill afterward), micro-baseboard/corner (no special substrate prep needed). Next tier — tile baseboard (needs a factory-mitred edge joint at the top, more skill/labor, though not dramatically pricier than PVC per linear meter). **Most expensive install of all seven, by a wide margin: shadow baseboard and concealed-mount baseboard** — both require cutting a niche/channel into the wall first, installing internal support structure, and finishing (fiberglass mesh tape, joint compound) to a perfectly straight edge along the entire room perimeter; concealed-mount additionally needs its internal insert painted before final mounting — described as needing dramatically more skill, time, and labor than even tile baseboard.
4. **⚠️ Material-cost ranking across all seven types**: cheapest — PVC (costs almost nothing). Mid-tier — tile baseboard (extra tile material plus more waste from factory-mitred cuts, worse with large-format tile), the rigid pre-finished family (MDF/duropolymer/veneer/polyurethane — cheap and by far the most popular), micro-baseboard/corner (positioned between low and mid cost, "definitely pricier than PVC"). Most expensive material — concealed-mount, shadow, and aluminum baseboard, simply because those materials themselves cost more to manufacture.
5. **⚠️ Concealed (flush-mount) door compatibility, a genuinely new criterion linking directly to this project's existing Concealed-Door content**: a baseboard compatible with a concealed door must be either very thin or itself flush with the wall, since a concealed door's frame sits flush with no protruding casing to hide a thicker baseboard's clash. **Compatible types**: shadow baseboard and concealed-mount baseboard (both already flush/recessed), aluminum (only a few mm proud of the wall), micro-baseboard/corner (thin angle profile). **Incompatible types**: PVC, the rigid pre-finished family, and tile baseboard — these protrude far enough to catch/obstruct the door leaf during opening; they can be notched/hacked around but never allow full door swing — described as a "wrong, unappealing junction detail," directly corroborating and extending this project's existing shadow-gap-baseboard structural-dependency content on `Concealed_Door_Considerations.md`.
6. **⚠️ Wall-prep-quality-needed ranking across all seven types**: least demanding — tile baseboard (mounted on tile adhesive, forgiving) and plastic/PVC (flexible enough to follow even a semicircular curve, fully hiding wall waviness). Middle — the rigid pre-finished family and micro-baseboard/corner (a visible angle profile will show wall waviness, though without fanaticism required). Also demanding — shadow baseboard and concealed-mount baseboard: their profile is a long, straight, rigid (usually aluminum) bar roughly 3m long, so any wall waviness shows through however carefully the joint is puttied. **Most demanding of all — aluminum baseboard specifically**: an 8-10cm-tall flat plate glued directly to the wall over its full 3m length, with no recess to hide behind — any wall irregularity shows through and can never be puttied away afterward, since it's glued straight to the finished wall surface, not recessed into it.
7. **⚠️ Wear-resistance ranking across all seven types, first structured comparison for this project's baseboard content**: most durable — plastic/PVC (provided quality corner fittings are used; cheap corner caps can pop off from a single kick, a real weak point) and tile baseboard (both effectively indestructible over decades). Mid-tier — the rigid pre-finished family (can show wear/dulling after roughly 10-15 years, typically beyond a renovation's expected lifespan anyway), micro-baseboard/corner (plain metal angle, durable). Least durable — concealed-mount and aluminum baseboard: aluminum scratches easily from the slightest contact with anything sharp (an unavoidable material property); concealed-mount's exposed insert is painted the same color as the wall, and even light repeated contact visibly wears/dulls that paint in a way that can't be fully cleaned back to original — genuinely lower durability than the rest despite the premium cost. **Shadow profile scores as not really applicable to this criterion** — the source states it isn't a functioning baseboard at all technically, just a reveal/gap cut into the wall, so "wear resistance" doesn't meaningfully apply to it.
8. **⚠️ Ease-of-cleaning criterion, exists specifically because of one baseboard type**: shadow profile/baseboard is the sole real outlier here — no robot vacuum, ordinary vacuum, or hand can effectively reach into its narrow recessed gap; dust accumulates heavily and is extremely difficult to remove even by hand (described as very tiring/impractical) — a real, concrete downside unique to this type among all seven compared.
9. **⚠️ Presenter's own final ranking/recommendation, extends this project's existing shadow-gap-baseboard skepticism with a second, independent critique angle**: personal top pick is the rigid pre-finished (MDF/duropolymer) family for a genuinely modern quality renovation — "nothing beats it practically." PVC recommended only for a rental/flip or an extremely tight budget. Tile baseboard called "outdated." Aluminum "not needed — scratches too easily, needs a perfect wall." Concealed-mount baseboard "costs like an airplane wing" and still scratches/wears quickly despite the price. Shadow profile draws the harshest framing of all seven — the source poses a rhetorical challenge to viewers ("tell me in the comments what it's actually for, besides style") — corroborating and extending this project's existing `Flooring_Guide.md` shadow-profile-incompatibility content (this round's video 2) with a second, independent low-functional-value critique.
