## Quantities / Measurements — Ergonomic Clearances

- **⚠️ Dining table to wall: 75 cm, and she decomposes it** — **45 cm for a seated person + 30 cm for someone to pass sideways**. She attributes the 75 cm to ergonomic regulation. The decomposition is what makes it usable, because it lets you re-derive the figure for a different situation.
- **⚠️ Dining table to a *kitchen run* (not a wall): minimum 110 cm** — **50 cm for the seated person + 60 cm for a cabinet front to open.** A different and larger number than the wall case, which she says people miss because the plan "looks fine."
- **⚠️ With a dishwasher door open plus a bulky armchair-style dining chair that doesn't tuck under: minimum 145 cm.**
- **⚠️ Measure to the chairs, not the table, whenever the chairs don't tuck under.**
- **⚠️ A dishwasher door opens outward and occupies a full 80 cm.** Named failure: planning exactly 80 cm — or less — between the kitchen run and an island or table, having checked only the ordinary 40–60 cm cabinet fronts.
- **⚠️ Three scenarios to walk through on the plan**, not one: (1) pushing a chair back and standing up; (2) one person seated while another passes; (3) one person seated while another uses the kitchen fronts.
- **⚠️ Fridge needs a 7–8 cm filler strip against a wall** so the door opens past 90° and the crisper drawers can be pulled out (model-dependent). **Their practice: assume 7 cm worst case, or better, put a narrow cabinet or bottle pull-out there instead.** Named downstream consequence: the strip shifts the whole run, and if the electrics are already installed a socket can end up over the hob or hidden behind a cabinet.
- **⚠️ A radiator closer than 60 cm to the kitchen run blocks the fronts from opening** — relocate it if it's being replaced, or plan a blind filler panel.
- **⚠️ Hob setback from the worktop edge: stone fabricators require a minimum 10–15 cm** or the stone cracks, **and they will not warranty the worktop otherwise.** Separate from the ergonomic objection (your elbow hits the wall or a tall unit, and pan handles can't be turned).

## Mistakes / Warnings — Non-Dimensional

- **⚠️ Worktop overhang with a drip groove ("капельник") does not keep water off the fronts — her third documented experiment on this channel.** She poured water onto an overhanging worktop with a drip groove, fast and slow. **The stream separates from the surface, and surface tension gives it an angle in the air, so it still reaches both the fronts and the space behind them.** Conclusion: the overhang-plus-drip-groove detail has no functional purpose and only spoils the kitchen visually — "ровно так же, как подоконник с навесом." **This extends her Round 1 experiment in `0WXiKNXPD_0`**, which tested a plain overhang; this one tests the supposedly-improved version and finds it fails too.
- **⚠️ Push-to-open / Tip-on on kitchen fronts, now with the mechanism**: a showroom trial of two or three opens misleads. **In use it is two motions, not one — press, then pull — because the self-opening speed is not the speed you want**, so you end up pulling anyway. A real handle is one motion at your own speed. On a surface used every day the difference compounds. Corroborates `avRNMkNdOBs` (same channel) and supplies the reasoning that note lacked.
- **Integrated groove handles**: fingers slip, nails break. If specifying remotely without a showroom trial, choose fronts with **no cut-out at all** — pull on the front edge.
- **⚠️ Acrylic sinks — avoid. Stainless steel is what designers actually specify**, undermounted: hygienic, wear-resistant, tolerant of chemicals, heat and staining agents. She notes the common assumption that designers pick a stone sink matching the worktop, and says the opposite is true in her practice.
- **⚠️ Plan sockets inside cabinets, not only above the worktop** — for countertop appliances (toaster, coffee machine, kettle, multicooker), especially in a small kitchen. With pull-out shelves the appliance can be used without being lifted out. **Her method: list every appliance you own first**, then decide which live in a cabinet.

## Electrical / Lighting

- **⚠️ Lighting placed behind you, using spots, throws your own shadow onto the worktop.** Stated mechanism: most spots on the market have a **narrow beam of about 60°**. She calls hard shadows on a kitchen worktop a safety issue, not just a comfort one.
  - **Best: a luminaire between the worktop edge and the upper cabinet fronts.**
  - **If it must go behind you: use diffuse fixtures with a wider spread (~90–110°)** so at least there are no hard shadows.
  - **Don't skip backsplash/under-cabinet lighting in any case** — it's what rescues the shadow problem.

## Regulations — ⚠️ RUSSIAN LAW, NOT BELARUS

- **She states that venting a cooker hood into the apartment's ventilation shaft is now illegal in Russia**, including the partial variant that blocks only half the vent opening to preserve natural ventilation, and that this now applies to **all** apartments rather than only gas ones. Stated exception: apartments with their own individual ventilation ducts. Her prescribed alternative is a **recirculating (filter) hood**, which she says are now widely available and inexpensive — filter for "рециркуляция" when buying. She adds a personal view that the change is correct regardless, because shaft-vented hoods degraded the building's ventilation for everyone.
