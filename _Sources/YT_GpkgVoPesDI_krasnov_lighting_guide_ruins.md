## Rules / Heuristics — the ten numbered mistakes

- **⚠️ Three-layer lighting is mandatory**: general/ambient (ceiling fixture or downlight array), decorative/accent (highlighting a feature), and functional/task (worktop light, reading sconces, desk lamps) — a single ceiling source alone is the base mistake.
- **Evening-only living with just accent/task light (no general light on) is presented as normal and desirable**, not a compromise — he states roughly 40% of people he knows (including himself) live this way in the evening and recommends viewers who do the same to comment/like, treating it as a legitimate lighting scenario to design for explicitly rather than an afterthought.
- **Spot/downlight-only ceilings fail to substitute for diffuse general light**: an array of point spots/cylinders across the ceiling leaves the upper part of the room visibly darker — most people find this unsatisfying even if a minority is fine with it. General ceiling light should be maximally diffuse and even, filling the whole room, not just point-illuminating specific spots.
- **⚠️ Avoid cold-white light entirely in residential spaces** — reserved for offices in his framing; use neutral or warm only.
- **Never mix multiple colour temperatures within one room** — pick one light-temperature strategy per space and apply it consistently; a room with several different colour temperatures simultaneously reads as a design error, not a stylistic choice.
- **⚠️ Illuminance targets by room, three figures**: **living room 100–150 lux/m²**; **kitchen 200–300 lux/m²**; **bedroom ~50 lux/m²** (deliberately low, since the room's primary function is sleep, not task work).
- **Larger homes need a proper photometric/lighting calculation at the design stage** (fixture count, placement, wattage) — described as not strictly necessary for compact flats but increasingly important as floor area grows, since the risk and cost of an over-lit or under-lit zone rises with scale, and correcting it after finishes are complete is very difficult or expensive. States this is why his studio moved to running its own in-house construction crew — coordinating the lighting calculation between designer, architect, and installer under one team avoids the handoff failures ("одни строители понимают, другие нет") he experienced when outsourcing construction separately.
- **⚠️ CRI (colour rendering index) should be ≥90** in rooms where people dress, apply makeup, or cook: dressing room/wardrobe, bathroom, kitchen, living room. States most people don't know the metric exists, despite its practical importance for how skin tone/colours actually render under the fixture.
- **Switch-zoning balance**: avoid one single master switch controlling every fixture in a room (loses all scene flexibility) but also avoid over-fragmenting into one switch per individual fixture (too many switches to manage sanely) — group fixtures into a small number of sensible switch zones per room, decided at the design stage alongside the lighting layout itself.
- **⚠️ Chandelier/pendant sizing rule**: match the fixture to its manufacturer-rated coverage area — rule of thumb, the room should be **roughly ≥4× the fixture's rated area** (his example: a fixture rated for 50 m² is oversized for a 20 m² room). Also physically measure the fixture's stated height dimension against the room's actual headroom before buying (his example: a 50 cm-tall fixture occupies real vertical space once installed that a spec sheet alone doesn't make intuitive).
- **⚠️ Lighting must be planned during design, before renovation begins, not added afterward** — the single sequencing rule tying everything above together: once the renovation is complete, correcting a poorly-planned lighting scheme is difficult or impossible. Minimum viable version for someone not working with a designer: sketch a basic floor plan, mark planned fixture positions, decide desired colour temperature per room, before starting construction.
