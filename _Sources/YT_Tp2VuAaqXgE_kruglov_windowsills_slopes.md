## Genuinely New Content

1. **Windowsill-vs-slope functional/decorative zone distinction, first explicit framing for this store**: the windowsill (подоконник, the horizontal surface below the window) is the genuinely **functional** zone — usable as a shelf, desk-surface extension, kitchen-counter continuation, or seating nook. The three slopes (side and top reveals, "откосы") are **purely decorative**, with the single narrow functional exception of integrating 1-3 electrical outlets into a slope (already documented on the existing page's electrical-points-in-slopes content).
2. **⚠️ Windowsill use-case examples, genuinely new practical ideas for this store**: (a) a voice-assistant smart speaker placed on the windowsill with a dedicated outlet built into the adjacent slope — the source's own home setup; (b) widening the windowsill's depth to double as a desk/workspace surface; (c) windowsill as a literal continuation of an adjacent kitchen countertop; (d) widening the windowsill (via a drywall false-wall build-out, or simply because the facade wall is already thick) to create a seating nook. **⚠️ A genuinely new architectural fact**: in brick buildings specifically, the facade wall is often thick enough on its own that the windowsill zone is naturally wide enough to sit on without any build-out at all — distinct from a drywall-false-wall widening technique that works regardless of the building's own wall thickness.
3. **⚠️ Four windowsill material types, entirely new comparison content for this store (the existing page covers slope materials only, no windowsill-material comparison)**: **plastic** — cheapest, most accessible (buyable same-day at any hardware store), but a limited color/texture palette. **Tile** — either flush with the wall (no overhang past the slope) or with factory-made 90°-mitred corner joints on all sides (a genuine product category some tile companies mass-produce), giving a near-factory-finished look; cons: expensive, cold to sit on without a cushion, and needs extra structural reinforcement (angle brackets or similar) if it overhangs significantly. **Artificial stone (acrylic)** — the widest color/shape range of all four (round, oval, curved or sharp corners, fully custom via a template), fabricated off-site to that custom shape and installed in pieces like a countertop, sanded to a seamless/monolithic finish; cons: expensive, a real **10-20 calendar/working-day production lead time** to plan for, and cold to sit on. **Wood** — cheaper and warmer than the other three, but limited to a rectangular shape (no curves or rounded corners achievable).
4. **⚠️ Windowsill cost ranking (material + install combined), first for this store**: cheapest — plastic (cost scales directly with the specific plastic product's own price tier, install labor is the same regardless). Next — wood. Next — tile, **split into two sub-tiers**: flush-mounted tile is cheap, arguably even cheaper than wood; factory-mitred-corner tile costs more than both wood and plastic. **Most expensive of all four — custom-fabricated artificial stone**, the priciest in both material and install/labor.
5. **Plaster-transition trim-free slope-finish variant, extends this page's existing sandwich-panel/trim construction content**: instead of the standard plastic corner-trim strip between a sandwich-panel slope and the wall, some installations now finish the transition with careful plaster work so the wall visually flows into the slope without a visible trim strip — described as a rare but real, growing alternative to the standard trim-strip finish.
6. **⚠️ A practitioner's own risk-tolerance opinion on the plastered-slope crack risk, a distinct personal stance worth recording alongside this page's existing "never appropriate" framing**: the source personally states that if a hairline crack eventually appears at the plastic-frame-to-plaster junction (which this page's existing content already documents as essentially inevitable), it wouldn't bother him at all — "I don't look at it every day" — framing a painted/plastered slope's crack risk as an acceptable tradeoff for its superior look/feel, a real practitioner's own risk tolerance rather than a technical rebuttal of the existing crack-mechanism content.
7. **Tile-slope-plus-windowsill continuous same-material treatment**: some installations use identical tile across the windowsill and all three slopes, framing the entire window opening in one continuous material — an aesthetic option distinct from finishing the sill and slopes in two different materials.
8. **⚠️ Slope-material cost ranking (four types), first structured comparison for this store**: cheapest — plastic sandwich-panel with a standard corner-trim strip. Next — the plaster-transition trim-free plastic variant (slightly pricier than the standard trim version). Next — plaster-plus-panel (MDF/wood/gypsum-based panel) and plaster-plus-tile, priced roughly equal to each other. **Priciest of all four — painted/plastered slope**, due to the sheer number of painting-preparation steps required — though the source states the cost gap between painted, tiled, and panel-finished slopes is practically negligible in absolute terms, unlike the much larger gap down to plastic.
