## Design Concept, Mistakes / Warnings, and Materials

- **⚠️ Marble trend-saturation critique**: Carrara, Calacatta, Statuario named specifically as overused patterns now seen everywhere from showrooms to new-builds — the current premium direction is moving toward calmer stone with a more subtle/muted pattern rather than dramatic veining. If using marble at all, recommends using it sparingly in accent zones with a matte finish rather than as the dominant material.
- **⚠️ Unlacquered ("living") brass vs. PVD-coated brass — a genuinely new named material-science distinction**: unlacquered brass reacts to water/fingerprints/humidity and develops an UNEVEN patina (some areas darken faster than others, creating a "blotchy" rather than uniformly aged look) requiring regular hand-polishing to maintain shine — only recommend it to a client who genuinely wants and will maintain a living-patina aesthetic. **PVD-coated brass or a factory-sealed alloy** gives a stable, even golden color with minimal maintenance and better scratch/moisture resistance — the practical recommendation for anyone who doesn't want the patina-maintenance commitment.
- **⚠️ Monochrome kitchen critique** — a specific critique of the "make cabinets, island AND wall all one identical color" design move sometimes proposed as a "monolithic" look: argued to actually flatten depth/texture perception (wood grain, stone depth, and light play all become indistinguishable when everything is one uniform color/material), making the kitchen read as "a giant featureless box" rather than an architectural statement. Recommends nuance/contrast/material variation instead of full uniformity.
- **⚠️ Kickplate/toe-kick (цоколь) ergonomics — the round's most valuable single new mechanism**: the popular "floating"/no-toe-kick cabinet look (fronts running almost to the floor) sacrifices real ergonomics. **The ~80cm toe-kick recess lets a person's feet tuck slightly under the cabinet while standing close to the counter, which keeps the body's center of gravity stable and reduces lower-back strain during extended standing/food-prep** — losing this recess forces standing further back and leaning forward more, causing fatigue within 5-10 minutes of cooking. **A second, distinct problem**: with no toe-kick, the lower front edge is directly exposed to splashes/moisture/foot impact (staining, swelling, chipping over months). **A third**: a toe-kick area commonly hides electrical/plumbing runs (outlets, dishwasher drain hose) and built-in-appliance ventilation clearance, which becomes harder to access/service without it. **Recommended compromise**: a recessed toe-kick 5-10cm high, painted to match the floor or cabinet front (for the visual lightness of "floating" while keeping the ergonomic recess), or in premium projects a **flush recessed kickplate set back a few cm from the cabinet face** for the best of both. Russian «цоколь» quoted inline on first use.
- **⚠️ Glossy cabinet fronts — a specific critique**: visual "noise" from constant reflections, reads as dated (explicitly compared to early-2000s "high-tech" kitchens, now considered fatigued/outdated), collects fingerprints and grime heavily. Recommends using gloss only as a tiny accent (a single display case or module), never full-surface.
- **⚠️ Invisible/hidden induction cooktop technology — genuinely new, flagged as an emerging/experimental technology, not an established recommendation**: a cooktop with induction modules mounted BENEATH a porcelain/ceramic countertop slab, fully invisible when off (no visible cooktop outline, cookware heats directly through the stone). **Named brands: "Invisible Cook" (likely "InvisaCook", ASR-garbled — flag as ASR-uncertain), Infinity, ASC** — even in Europe/US this is still considered a niche high-end/demonstration technology, not mainstream. **Technical risks explicitly named**: heating uniformity depends critically on matching the slab's thickness/composition/thermal conductivity to the induction module (a poor match causes uneven cooking or module overheating); if one module fails, the ENTIRE countertop slab must be disassembled to access/repair it (no simple swap); thermal shock risk — pouring cold water onto a still-hot cooking zone can crack the slab; burn risk since the heated zone has no visible marking/outline once the cooktop is off. His explicit recommendation is to wait and observe this technology maturing (cooling/reliability/modularity/repairability) rather than adopt it now — if the same "invisible cooktop" visual effect is wanted today, use a **thin-glass induction cooktop with a concealed/hidden frame** instead for a similar clean look with predictable, established reliability.
