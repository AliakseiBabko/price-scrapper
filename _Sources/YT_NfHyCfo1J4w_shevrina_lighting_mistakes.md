## Electrical / Lighting — Seven Named Mistakes

- **⚠️ 1. LED strip run round the whole perimeter of a non-rectangular room.** Produces an ugly broken line following every jog. Her stated principle: **minimalist and contemporary interiors use simple geometric forms** — so if a room has a projection formed by two walls (e.g. a wardrobe boxed out of a bedroom), **light only that external corner, or run one single line along the headboard wall under the ceiling, or use a cornice-concealed strip** rather than tracing the whole perimeter.
- **⚠️ 2. A strip you can see into glares.** The named case is a strip above a bed headboard that shines into your eyes once you lean back against the headboard — and she notes it is uncomfortably bright even when not looked at directly. **Fix: mount the strip so it throws light upward rather than downward, on a small standoff, preferably in a corner profile**, so the light is soft and diffuse.
- **⚠️ 3. Voltage drop over a long strip run — the most concrete electrical item here.** **A reel is 5 m. If the total run exceeds one reel and it is fed from one end only, the far end will be visibly dimmer** — the classic case being a bedroom perimeter fed from a single corner, leaving the opposite corner dark, because the summed side lengths exceed 5 m. **Fix: run feeds to at least two opposite corners.** Same principle for multiple strips: three vertical wall strips on one switch should **each get their own individual cable**, not be chained off one wire — "угасание будет довольно заметно глазу." She notes the fitter may not warn you.
- **⚠️ 4. Choosing warm 3000 K as the *main* light for the wrong reason — the best physics point in the source.** People compare two showroom fixtures of **identical lumen output**, one at 3000 K and one at 4000 K, and conclude the 4000 K one is brighter. **It is not: luminous flux is independent of colour temperature, and both will illuminate a tabletop identically. They are confusing "warm" with "soft."**
  - **Her prescription: if 4000 K feels too bright, take 4000 K with fewer lumens** rather than dropping to 3000 K.
  - **Two further reasons to prefer neutral for the main light**: warm light is strongly relaxing and sleep-inducing, which is wrong if you want to work or get things done in the evening; and **neutral light does not distort the flat's colours**, which makes colour selection easier — it will not push already-yellow walls further yellow when the main light goes on in the evening.
  - **A useful counterweight to the colour pages**: she states the influence of a cornice LED strip on wall-colour choice is **overstated** — people over-weight it when picking paint.
- **⚠️ 5. Switches with an indicator lamp.** Two named symptoms: ceiling spots glow dimly all night, **or switch on spontaneously at random moments** — because current accumulates until it is sufficient to trigger the driver. **Fix: a shunt resistor in the circuit, and possibly more than one.** Otherwise you live with dimly glowing spots.
- **⚠️ 6. Not checking the depth of recessed fixtures — with a specific allowance.** **Add 1 cm to the fixture's stated depth when calculating the ceiling drop**: a 6 cm fixture needs a 7 cm drop; a 10 cm fixture forces an 11 cm drop. Her named failure: buying an attractive showroom spot on the strength of its light quality and colour temperature without checking its depth, then discovering the ceiling must come down 11 cm.
- **⚠️ 7. Sizing a magnetic track's power supply exactly to the initial fixture count.** The whole appeal of a magnetic track is being able to add fixtures later — which the power supply then blocks. **Fix: specify headroom at purchase; she suggests telling the supplier you plan roughly three additional fixtures.**
