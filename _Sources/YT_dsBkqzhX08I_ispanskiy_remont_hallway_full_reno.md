## Demolition / Walls / Ceilings — Durable Facts & Rules

- **A non-load-bearing partition wall (plus its door frame/box) between
  the hallway and living room was demolished to combine the spaces** —
  explicit reasoning given: "if a wall serves no function, it's not
  needed." A DIY-made pry bar (welded from a car bumper by an acquaintance
  welder) was used to break the door frame before removing the wall
  itself. Framed as fast, low-noise, low-dust when the wall is genuinely
  non-structural.
- **A suspended/dropped ceiling (~20 cm lower than structural) was
  demolished** — identified as typical 1960s-era Spanish residential
  construction: the suspended ceiling substrate was **straw soaked in
  gypsum, laid over a structural deck of concrete joists and brick**. The
  source frames this explicitly as a historical workaround (lowering the
  ceiling to conceal electrical wiring runs rather than properly routing
  them) — a genuine regional-construction-history data point, Spain,
  1960s vintage buildings specifically.
- **A section of ceiling near the door, where existing wiring (including
  a shared/neighboring apartment's cabling running through an electrical
  shaft) ran, was deliberately NOT rebuilt/leveled** — reasoning: fully
  fixing that section would require much more demolition and rerouting
  shared-building cabling, judged not worth it; a small ceiling "step" was
  accepted at the door instead. **Generalizable planning principle**: a
  ceiling/wall defect adjacent to shared-building infrastructure (wiring
  shafts, risers) may be cheaper to leave as a visible compromise than to
  fully correct — evaluate the blast radius of a "proper" fix before
  committing to it.
- **Ceiling holes/gaps in the exposed brick substrate were filled, and
  plastic corner beads were installed at all ceiling corners/edges before
  plastering** — the ceiling was plastered (not just skimmed) specifically
  to avoid losing more height and to bring it level with the adjoining
  living-room ceiling.
- **Wiring for new recessed spotlights was run before the plastering
  stage**, not after — standard sequencing but explicitly confirmed here.

- **Plaster guide rails/beacons ("маяки")** — the type used to guide a
  straight, even plaster application — **are described as practically
  impossible to find in Spain** at the time of filming (2021); the source
  knew of a supplier but it required a long trip to a specific building-
  supply yard. **Many Spanish plasterers he encountered did not know this
  tool/method existed at all** — plastering there is described as
  typically done freehand, and **Spanish residential walls are commonly
  wavy/uneven, which is a locally accepted norm, not a defect anyone
  complains about**. As a substitute, the source used plastic corner
  beads/guides instead of proper beacons.
- **A "правило" (a long aluminum straightedge/darby screed tool, standard

  begun stocking this tool** — the source attributes this market shift
  explicitly to an influx of builders from the former USSR/CIS region
  changing local Spanish construction technique/practice for the better.
  A genuinely specific, dated, first-hand claim about cross-border trade-

## Walls / Ceilings — Plastering, Priming, and Painting Technique (sequential detail)

- **Ceiling finishing sequence**: light sanding of the base plaster →
  prime → let fully dry → apply one coat of ready-mixed filler/putty
  (шпаклёвка, bought pre-mixed, not powder) → let dry → sand again →
  apply a second coat. **Two coats of finish putty were sufficient** to
  bring this ceiling to a smooth, paint-ready surface.
- **Deliberate wall-texture decision, with explicit reasoning**: for this
  narrow, light-painted corridor specifically, the source decided *against*
  a smooth wall finish — reasoning stated directly: "in a narrow corridor,
  leaving walls perfectly smooth is not very sensible — any small scratch
  will show immediately," especially with a light/bright paint color
  planned. **Technique used**: a large long-nap roller loaded with finish
  putty, rolled to create an even, irregular "cobweb" ("паутинка") texture,
  applied in two coats, then very lightly hand-sanded to knock down any
  protruding high points once dry. **Directly generalizable rule**: match
  wall-finish smoothness/texture decisions to the corridor's own light
  level and paint darkness — a bright, light-painted narrow hallway is
  exactly the case where texture (not glass-smooth) hides everyday wear
  better.
- **Priming before paint**: uses a concentrated primer, diluted according
  to substrate porosity — his gypsum-plaster surface was highly absorbent,
  requiring a **deep-penetration concentrate** specifically to reduce paint
  consumption and bind dust particles left from sanding. **Typical
  dilution ratio for this surface: approximately 60-70% water** (varies by
  concentrate brand/strength) — a concrete, checkable mixing ratio.
- **Painting sequence and roller-width tradeoff, stated explicitly**:
  paint corners/edges by brush first, then roll the field — a
  **narrower roller was deliberately chosen over the widest available**
  for better control and less overspray/spatter, at the cost of slower
  coverage speed ("the wider the roller, the faster it goes, but I wanted
  to do this carefully, and I had the time"). Ceiling: white; walls: gray,
  deep-matte vinyl paint; 2 coats each, full dry between coats.
  Recessed spotlights were installed only after ceiling paint fully dried,
  and immediately and noticeably increased perceived brightness.

## Furniture / Built-ins — Durable Facts & Rules (real refinishing case)

- **Decision to repaint existing hallway furniture rather than replace
  it**: furniture was structurally sound but the wrong color for the new
  scheme — repainting (primer + 2 coats, first coat brushed, second
  coat rolled to avoid streaking) was chosen over buying new.
- **A shoe cabinet's horizontal top surface was deliberately left in its
  natural wood-texture finish, not painted white**, even though the rest
  of the piece was repainted — explicit reasoning: a horizontal surface
  that gets used for setting down keys and other items (and sometimes
  sitting on) shows scuffs/discoloration quickly under white paint; the
  natural wood grain conceals this kind of everyday wear far better.
  **Directly reusable furniture-material rule for any entryway/hallway
  storage piece**: keep a heavily-contacted horizontal surface in a
  wear-resistant natural/patterned finish rather than a flat light color,
  independent of the rest of the piece's finish.
- **A mirror frame was masked with painter's tape at the glass edge and
  repainted with the same door/furniture-grade paint** as the rest of the
  project (primer + 2 coats) — reused an existing mirror rather than
  buying new, matching frame color to the new scheme.

## Flooring / Doors-Trim — Durable Facts & Rules (real floor/baseboard decision)

- **Key structural constraint driving the floor decision, explicitly
  named**: the apartment's own front entry door opens *inward* into this
  corridor — meaning any floor buildup (new flooring layered on top of the
  existing tile) risks the door no longer clearing the finished floor.
  **Directly reusable hallway-planning principle**: check entry-door swing
  and floor-to-door clearance *before* committing to any renovation option
  that raises floor height in a hallway/entryway, not just for aesthetic
  reasons.
- **Existing tile flooring (1.5 cm thick, on a loose cement bed over a
  thick sand layer) was deliberately kept rather than removed**, in
  addition to the door-clearance constraint above — removal was assessed
  as generating a very large volume of debris and requiring a full
  new thick screed pour, a materially larger labor/time/cost commitment
  than the alternative chosen: painting directly over the existing tile.
- **Existing tile-pattern baseboards (matching the floor tile, not wood)
  were also kept and repainted rather than replaced** — assessed as
  structurally sound ("very strong, no issues with them") with no reason
  to remove functioning trim just to change its appearance.
- **Specialty water/impact-resistant floor paint for tile, real EUR
  price**: **~25 EUR per 700 g** ("хорошая краска для плитки дорогая, 700
  грамм краски стоит около 25 евро") — **≈$30** (manual EUR→USD estimate
  at the approximate contemporaneous ~1.20-1.21 EUR/USD rate for
  Feb 2021; **not run through this project's own `currency_converter.py`
  tool, which currently supports only USD/RUB and USD/BYN pairs** — flagged
  as a rough estimate, not a tool-verified conversion). Result described
  as looking like genuine white tile after painting.
  Manufacturer guidance for this specific paint was to apply **directly to
  the tile with no separate floor primer**, despite tile primers being
  commercially available — followed as given. Application: brush for
  edges/corners, large-nap roller for the field, **2 coats with ~24 hours
  drying between coats**; water-based, odorless, dries to the touch within
  a couple hours but reaches full hardness/cure only after **2 days**.
- **Stated design rule**: baseboards should always match the color of the
  door/trim casings ("наличники"), not be treated as an independent color
  choice.
- **Closing principle, explicitly generalized by the source**: a common
  belief that repainted doors or repainted tile "always look bad" is
  false — **the deciding factor is choosing the correct paint type for the
  substrate and use-zone** (e.g., a shower-zone tile needs shower-rated
  tile paint specifically; doors need dedicated door/furniture paint, not
  generic wall emulsion) — a durable, brand-agnostic, directly reusable
  material-selection principle, not tied to this specific project.
