## Ceilings — Getting the Single-Finish "Box" Effect

**The problem she starts from: porcelain is too heavy to fix to a ceiling**, so you cannot simply wrap the room in one material. **Two solutions:**

1. **Colour-match paint to the porcelain** and paint the ceiling in it.
2. **Switch the walls to microcement instead of porcelain**, so walls and ceiling can both be microcement.

## ⚠️ Microcement in Wet Areas — a Composition-Based Defence

Addressing the fear of using microcement in a shower: **microcement is not paint.** Its composition is **cement + polymers + quartz sand in various fractions** — and quartz is highly wear-resistant, which she analogises to quartz worktops being the most durable worktop type. It is used in **public high-traffic spaces on both walls and floors**.

Her conclusion: it is reliable in a bathroom's dry, damp *and* wettest zones, **and it can be applied to the ceiling**, which is what delivers the single-finish box effect. **And the finish is controllable** — different application methods and aggregate fractions give either a mottled look or something that reads like plain paint.

Complements the Round 2 material on microcement fractions (`0WXiKNXPD_0`, coarse fraction for traffic zones) and on microcement over a heated wall (`z4G-ocStu9o`).

## ⚠️ Tile Format — a Perceptual Argument for Rectangles

**Prefer rectangular tile formats over square on walls.** Her stated mechanism: **people find horizontal/elongated forms more agreeable than squares** — she points to screens, phones and televisions all being rectangular. **Square porcelain is fine on floors**, because you see it in perspective rather than face-on, and because you look at walls far more than at floors. Orientation of the rectangles — horizontal or vertical — she leaves as free choice.

This is the same underlying claim she applies to pillow shape and to device screens elsewhere in her catalogue; **three distinct applications of one asserted perceptual preference**, which is worth noting as a consistent position rather than three separate opinions.

## Materials — The Flush-Junction Rule, Restated With a Better Analogy

**Don't finish a wall and then glue porcelain onto part of it** — you get an ugly exposed edge. **And even if you reject metal trims and dress the edge neatly, the mere existence of that small step makes the whole bathroom read unconsidered.** Her analogy is the best statement of this rule in her catalogue: **it is like a specialist hanging wallpaper with the seams misaligned between two runs — nobody accepts that, yet people accept a badly-terminated porcelain edge.**

**On cabinet fronts in the same material as the walls**: porcelain is too heavy for large fronts (e.g. over a WC installation). Options are **quartz** (limited choice — it is usually too thick) or, most commonly, **thin wide-format ceramic**. **She is explicit that this is not a cheap route** — especially if you also want the walls in the same wide-format ceramic, which "может влететь в копеечку."

## ⚠️ Two Cheap, Useful Details

- **A heated wall behind the mirror**, to prevent condensation on what is normally a cold mirror surface. She frames it as an extension of using a heated wall instead of a towel radiator — a technique already on record from Round 2, now with a second application.
- **Hide what irritates you.** A narrow vertical niche cabinet with the **toilet brush mounted to the inside of the door**; the **paper holder likewise**, with a perforation in the front for the paper end to emerge. **To recess a paper holder into the wall you need a genuinely thick wall** — and she gives a real method: **go and measure how much depth your paper plus holder occupies, and add that to the wall thickness**, since it must not pass through.

## ⚠️ Mixer and Shower Placement — Walk Your Own Scenarios

Her most transferable reasoning here, and explicitly against copying reference images: **don't put the spout, hand shower, overhead and mixers all on one wall just because Pinterest does.**

Worked example, a bath with a glass screen and a child or dog in the household:

- **Hand shower and its mixer on the LONG wall, centred on the bath** — otherwise you reach across the glass screen to the far end.
- **Overhead shower on the short wall**, where the height works.
- **⚠️ But put the overhead's mixer on the long wall**, so you can turn it on before stepping in and let the water warm up.
- **The counter-consideration she raises against her own suggestion**: adjusting temperature while already standing under the overhead then means bending and reaching. So the answer depends on which discomfort you'd rather have.

**Her practice: split the components across walls** — mixers often on the long wall, overhead and head on the short wall, sometimes the hand shower with the mixers.

## ⚠️ Concealed-Profile Glass Screens — the Procurement Reality

The most actionable item in the source:

- **If a glass-partition showroom says they can't do a concealed profile, leave and go to another** — it means they have no experience of it and no profiles for it.
- **It is not the builders' job.** "Не мучайте их, не пытайтесь сами там что-то колхозить."
- **⚠️ Critical timing: raise it BEFORE the tiling goes on.** Installation may be two-stage — **the profile fitted at rough stage onto the screed, and the glass only after finishing works.**
- **On a bath**: nobody cuts a niche into the tub. **The glass sits on the rim through a sealing tape** (or, less well, sealant) — **never bare glass onto the tub.**

## Design Concept — Shelf Placement by Sightline

Identify **where you actually stand and what you actually look at** — at the basin looking one way; in the shower looking another — and place shelves relative to those viewpoints. Her worked example: orient a vertical shelf so that from the main viewpoint it is seen **edge-on**, and its contents aren't visible at all.
