- **Room count is constrained by window count** (a balcony door counts as a quasi-window for this purpose) before client preference is even considered — more rooms than there are usable light sources tends to produce impractically small, poorly-lit rooms. - **Relocating a kitchen/sink must account for drainage slope, not just supply lines**: pressurized water-supply lines can be hidden in screed with little constraint, but sewage/drain lines are gravity-fed and need a consistent slope toward the riser — the further a sink is from the riser, the more screed depth/space is needed to conceal the sloped pipe. Practical recommendation: place the sink along a wall so the drain can run inside the wall to the riser without losing slope, rather than mid-room. `confirmed`, genuinely useful, non-obvious technical constraint.

- **Interior door leaf width standard has shifted**: older Soviet-era construction commonly used 60–70 cm door leaves (including narrow rooms where this made sense); modern practice commonly uses 80 cm leaves even for bathroom doors, specifically so large items (washing machines, bathtubs) can be moved through without needing to chip door frames. Rough opening should be ~10 cm wider than the leaf (5 cm clearance per side for frame/jamb installation) — e.g. an 80 cm leaf needs a ~90 cm rough opening. `confirmed`, a concrete, broadly-applicable planning number.
- **Door swing-path conflicts should be checked before finalizing leaf width** — e.g. an 80 cm door swinging into a room might strike furniture (a bed corner in this project's case); recommendation is to drop to a 70 cm leaf specifically where a genuine swing conflict exists, rather than defaulting to 80 cm everywhere regardless of layout. 60 cm is described as the historical economy minimum, rarely used today.
