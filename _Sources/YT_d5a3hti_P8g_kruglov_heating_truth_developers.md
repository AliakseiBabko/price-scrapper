## Heating — Radiator vs. In-Floor Convector Physics (genuinely new mechanism explanation)

- **⚠️ Convector and radiator heat a room via two different, only-partially-overlapping physical mechanisms — a clear explanatory model not previously captured in this store.** A convector heats almost entirely by **convection**: a thin metal tube carries hot water/fluid, aluminum or copper fins on the tube greatly increase contact surface area with the air, cold air enters low, warms between the fins, and rises — pushing cooler air down to be drawn in again (natural convection loop). A fan-assisted convector speeds this cycle but **introduces a real, audible noise cost** — a plain (non-fan) convector doesn't disturb sleep, a fan-assisted one is genuinely noticeable at night. A radiator works via **both convection (heating the air beside it, same rising/falling cycle) and radiant heat** — it also warms the furniture, walls, floor, and other mass around it directly. **Bottom line: a radiator heats everything around it including the walls; a convector only heats the air directly above itself.** Radiators are more massive, heat up slower, but hold heat significantly longer once warm. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
- **Designer/architect practice: supplementing in-floor convectors with an additional vertical wall radiator, with a concrete stated reason.** At extreme cold (the source's own example: -30°C), a convector only warms the air directly above itself and doesn't project heat further into the room — a bed positioned against the far wall from the window may not receive adequate warmth. Adding a slim vertical wall radiator (small footprint) increases in-room air convection and closes this gap. `single-account`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]

## Heating — Distribution System Taxonomy (extends existing radial-vs-sequential note)

- **Vertical riser distribution ("вертикальная разводка")**: an older, largely obsolete-for-Moscow system (the source states it's now essentially absent from Moscow's market, mostly found in economy-segment older buildings) where heating pipes run vertically floor-to-floor and radiators connect in series along the riser. **⚠️ Boxing in an exposed vertical riser is permitted, but only if a revision/access hatch is included** for the management company to reach it if needed. Practical guidance: check the building's own communal-systems major-repair (капремонт) schedule — if that's 5-10+ years away, boxing the riser in with no immediate access point is reasonable; if access is later genuinely needed, the box can be cut open and rebuilt (**tip: keep one spare roll of the same wallpaper, or one can of the same paint, specifically for this eventual patch-up** — the source frames this as making the eventual repair cosmetically painless). Precondition before boxing in: confirm the riser's internal welds are sound and that shutoff valves exist on both supply and return sides. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
- **Horizontal/sequential ("последовательная") distribution — most common current new-build default, with a specific quantified risk.** Two supply lines enter the apartment and run sequentially to each radiator, with a tee fitting on each line at every radiator. **⚠️ Worked risk figure: 6 radiators × 2 tees each = 12 under-screed tee joints — any one of which failing floods the neighbor below**, since all are buried under the screed with no individual access. The source states this is why it recommends switching every apartment to radial distribution instead, regardless of what the developer installed. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
- **Radial ("лучевая") distribution — recommended default, mechanism restated with added benefit detail.** Two supply lines connect to a manifold cabinet with two headers (one for supply, one for return); each radiator gets its own dedicated pair of lines run directly from the manifold, with **zero connections under the screed**. Stated benefits beyond leak-safety: fully serviceable (any single connection can be replaced without disturbing others — both the radiator-side and manifold-side ends of each run are accessible), and **any individual radiator can be isolated/shut off independently** for service without affecting the rest of the system. **If buying pre-owned/developer-finished and the screed is already poured with sequential distribution in place**, the source's own recommendation is to break out the screed and convert to radial regardless of the disruption — framed as "always break it out" rather than accepting the risk, though the decision is left to the owner if they choose not to. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]

## Heating — Manifold Cabinet Composition (genuinely new detail, distinct from the plumbing-manifold content already in `Water_Inlet_Node_Components.md`)

**⚠️ A heating manifold cabinet's minimum required component list, stated explicitly**: [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]

1. **The cabinet itself** — either surface-mounted (attached to the wall face) or fully recessed into the wall with only an access door visible; can be located anywhere convenient (a closet, a walk-in wardrobe, a bathroom, a hallway) as long as it fits the interior. **The single incoming line from the building's shared riser to this cabinet should have zero joints under the screed** — only the distribution side (cabinet-to-radiator) is meant to be joint-free under the floor; the riser-to-cabinet feed is a separate consideration.
2. **A ball valve isolating both the supply and return lines** at the cabinet.
3. **The manifold/header unit itself ("гребёнка")** — two headers, one per line (supply, return), each capable of shutting off any individual radiator's line independently.
4. Optional additions mounted on the manifold: **flow meters**, and **thermostatic actuator heads (servo-driven)** controllable via a smart-home system or directly from a smartphone.
5. **A mandatory drainage group**: an automatic air bleed valve plus a drain valve — stated as a non-optional minimum component.

## Heating — Radiator Connection Side (genuinely new)

- **⚠️ Bottom-through-the-floor radiator connection (two pipes protruding from the finished floor) is explicitly discouraged**: described as unattractive, inconvenient, obstructing normal cleaning (neither a robot vacuum nor manual cleaning can work properly around the protruding pipes), and requires cutting into the finished floor covering for the penetration. **Through-the-wall connection is recommended instead** — doesn't compromise the floor covering's integrity at all and doesn't interfere with cleaning or a robot vacuum. This guidance applies to horizontal/sequential and radial distribution; a vertical-riser system has no such choice (side connection only, dictated by the riser's own layout). ## Materials — Radiator Comparison by Construction Material (genuinely new, table-ready)

| Type | Pros | Cons | Recommended use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bimetallic** (steel tube core carrying water, aluminum fins for heat transfer) | Withstands up to 30 atm; compatible with any distribution scheme (vertical or horizontal); strong heat output; resistant to water hammer; modern styling; comparatively simple to install | More expensive than aluminum or steel; frequently counterfeited (labeled "bimetal" but actually pure aluminum) | **Best fit for this market's central-heating apartments** |
| **Aluminum** (aluminum, sometimes with added silicon) | Very high heat output; light and easy to install; cheaper than bimetallic; wide range of shapes/colors | Poor tolerance of excess pressure; corrodes in aggressive water chemistry; vulnerable to water hammer | Not well suited to central apartment heating — recommended for private houses or multi-unit buildings with individual (non-central) heating |
| **Cast iron** | Very high thermal mass — slow to heat, slow to cool, holds warmth long after the heat source stops; up to 50-year service life; indifferent to the specific heat-transfer fluid used; doesn't corrode | Very heavy, difficult to install; slow to warm a cold room; collects dust inside its construction, hard to clean; limited design/color/shape range (though some retro models exist) | Traditional/legacy default; still viable where its thermal-mass behavior and durability are wanted |
| **Steel** (includes both flat-panel radiators and ~95%-of-the-time-steel tubular/designer radiators) | Stylish, modern appearance (tubular type); fast to heat up and fast to cool down | Vulnerable to water hammer; corrosion-prone, especially where risers are periodically drained (accelerates failure); short working life on a standard central system | Recommended for cottages/private houses, or new-build apartments specifically with reduced pressure and horizontal (not vertical/riser) distribution only |

**⚠️ Rules for safely installing a stylish steel tubular radiator on a central system anyway** (if the owner wants the look despite the material caution above): buy only from verified/reputable manufacturers; confirm the specific model has been tested to withstand at least 10 atm; prefer a model with internal anti-corrosion treatment; always install through a ball valve, filter, and automatic air bleed valve; **never install a steel tubular radiator on a vertical-riser system — radial distribution only.** [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]

## Materials — Radiator Construction and Sizing (genuinely new)

- **Radiators also differ by build type — sectional (add/remove individual sections to hit a target output) vs. panel (fixed at a manufactured output rating)** — and by mounting orientation (vertical vs. horizontal), covered above. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
- **Center-to-center ("inter-axial") spacing standard**: **500mm is by far the most common** dimension between a radiator's top and bottom pipe connections; less common alternatives exist at 300mm, 350mm, 600mm, and 800mm. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
- **⚠️ Radiator sizing formula, stated directly with a worked example**: **100W of output per m² of floor area**, valid for a ceiling height up to 2.7m. Worked example: a 20m² room needs 2,000W total; if a single radiator section outputs 200W, that room needs 10 sections. [source: [[_Sources/YT_d5a3hti_P8g_kruglov_heating_truth_developers|d5a3hti_P8g]]]
