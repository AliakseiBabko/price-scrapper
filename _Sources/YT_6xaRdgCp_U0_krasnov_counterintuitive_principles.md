## Design Concept

**⚠️ Five named artists, each with a stated design principle — flag all 5 names per the cultural-reference filter — recorded as a structured set, this is the core content:**

1. **⚠️ Olafur Eliasson** ("The Weather Project" cited by name) — principle: atmosphere is created through light/reflection/air/scale rather than object density; a strong space can be visually restrained yet emotionally rich, built through a STATE rather than a count of objects. Practical translation: sometimes atmosphere comes from a single lighting scenario or reflective plane changing a room's perceived depth, not from adding more decor objects.
2. **⚠️ James Turrell** — principle: light as material/architecture itself, not merely illumination. Practical translation: design a SEQUENCE of light states across the day's real-life scenarios (arrival, quiet pause, dinner, contemplation, socializing) rather than a fixed single switch scheme. **Cross-reference: this closely reinforces this vault's existing multi-scene-lighting content (Round 9's `OWIBkORaQX4`, and the archetype video's Environment/Sensation-archetype lighting-scene requirement)** — recorded as corroborating/extending, with this specific artistic framing as the new element.
3. **⚠️ Yayoi Kusama** (Infinity Mirror Rooms cited by name) — explicitly critiques the naive takeaway (literally copying mirrors/polka-dot patterns) as a near-guaranteed failure. The real lesson: discipline of repetition, precise control of viewpoint/optics, ONE well-executed contained micro-scene rather than smearing a motif across an entire apartment. Explicitly states the effect's strength is NOT tied to budget — precision matters more than cost.
4. **⚠️ Ilya Kabakov** — principle: a room as narrative/story rather than a container of objects. Explicitly critiques the naive takeaway (filling a home with random nostalgic/vintage objects, posters, souvenirs = visual noise, not narrative). The real lesson, quoted closely: if a space is meant to tell a story, it must be RIGOROUSLY EDITED, with nothing random or accidental in it, because personal meaning does not automatically equal a strong result — personal narrative still requires curatorial structure/discipline. **Cross-reference: sharpens and extends this vault's existing meaning-based-art-curation content (Rounds 8-9)** with this specific "personal ≠ automatically strong, still needs editing discipline" reasoning.
5. **⚠️ teamLab** (named collective) — principle: a reactive/responsive environment. Critiques the naive takeaway (installing flashing sensors/gadgets/reactive tech everywhere as a "wow" gimmick). The real lesson: the "interior of the future" should softly ADAPT to a resident's established life rhythm (concrete example: the entryway-to-kitchen lighting path behaves differently morning vs. evening based on established habit, staying muted/quiet at night) rather than being a flashy attraction.

**⚠️ Explicit general methodology given for applying any art-derived principle to a real home, record as a distinct practical checklist:**

(a) Identify exactly what specifically appeals to you in a given artwork — light? repetition? tension of form? materiality? narrative? environmental response? — and select ONE main principle, not a vague overall impression.
(b) Scale that ONE principle down to a domestic level — apply it to a single local scene/niche/lighting sequence rather than attempting to make the entire apartment "the art piece." Explicitly states a small, precisely-executed micro-installation in a contained space works far better than spreading the concept thin across an entire home.
(c) Use real architectural material analogs instead of museum-grade special-effect technology for its own sake — frosted/matte glass, textile, concealed lighting, mineral plaster, wood with genuine texture, calm reflective surfaces rather than what the video calls "hysterical gloss."
(d) The artistic principle must never compromise basic function — light should be beautiful but not blinding, material should feel alive but not fragile/high-maintenance, a narrative concept should organize the space, not create clutter/chaos.
