## Doors / Trim & Planning Rules — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Living-space doors (bedroom, living room, study/office, kids' room, library, kitchen) should open INWARD, into the room** — this is the speaker's stated standing rule for his own projects, given for five stated reasons:
  1. **Safety**: a person is far more likely to be walking through the shared corridor (used by the whole household) than standing just inside a room's own doorway at the moment a door swings — an inward-opening door reduces the odds of hitting a family member, since the risk is concentrated inside the room being entered, not the shared corridor.
  2. **Light-switch ergonomics**: interior light switches and the door handle are conventionally on the same (inside) wall/side. Pushing a door open while entering lets the entrant keep one hand on the handle for orientation while immediately reaching for the switch with the other hand — even in the dark. An outward-opening door forces the opposite, awkward sequence when entering: step back to let the door open toward you, release it, step into a dark room, and search for the switch with no point of reference.
  3. **Open-position convenience**: interior doors spend most of their time open. An inward-opening door parks flat against an interior wall, out of the way. An outward-opening door either juts into the corridor (commonly only ~1 m wide, with multiple household members passing through) or otherwise obstructs it.
  4. **Floor-mounted door stopper placement**: an inward door's floor stopper sits inside the room, near a wall, out of the main walking path. An outward door's stopper sits in the corridor itself — a stubbed-toe/pinky-toe hazard in a heavily trafficked space.
  5. **Closing convenience**: interior doors are typically offset toward a side wall (not centered) and swing ~95°; an inward-opening door can be closed by simply reaching in from the corridor. An outward-opening door forces the person inside the room to walk most of the way back to the opening to grab the handle and close it.
  `confirmed` as the speaker's own consistently-applied design rule and stated reasoning — a coherent, internally consistent five-point rationale, not a vague preference.
- **WC/bathroom doors (and small utility rooms/closets/storage) should open OUTWARD, into the corridor** — opposite convention from living spaces, for two stated reasons:
  1. Most WCs/bathrooms have less interior floor area than the corridor, so an inward-opening door is even more cramped inside a small wet room than a corridor-swinging door would be in the hallway.
  2. **Safety-critical reason**: if an occupant collapses or becomes incapacitated inside and their body blocks the door, an inward-opening door cannot be pushed open without directly striking/injuring the person lying against it; an outward-opening door avoids this failure mode entirely. `confirmed`, and flagged as the single most safety-relevant fact in this extraction note.
  - **Speaker's own stated exception rate**: fewer than ~1% of his own projects use an inward-opening WC/bathroom door, for reasons he states would take ~90 minutes to fully explain and which he explicitly declines to cover in this video — **left as an acknowledged, unexplained edge case, not resolved here.**
- **General quick heuristic given for any room where the correct swing direction isn't obvious**: "if the light switch is inside the room, the door should open inward; if the switch is outside the room, the door should open outward." `confirmed` as the speaker's own simplified rule of thumb — note this is presented as a memory aid derived from the switch-ergonomics reasoning above (point 2), not an independently justified rule.
