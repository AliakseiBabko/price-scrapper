## Rooms / Zones — Living and Dining Room

- **Historical framing**: the dining room was historically the "white"
  (formal, guest-facing) counterpart to the kitchen's historically hidden
  "black" role — used for formal dinners and receiving guests. Dedicated
  serving/staging rooms (сервировочная, later "раздаточная" in Soviet
  usage) existed alongside historical dining rooms but have essentially
  disappeared from private-house design today (surviving mainly in
  hotels/restaurants that serve large volumes of dishes at once),
  attributed to the same kitchen-zone evolution covered in the kitchen
  theory video.
- **Neufert minimum per-person dining footprint: 60cm × 40cm rectangle**
  — the space one seated diner needs in front of them with dishes/food
  laid out; unchanged since Neufert's mid-20th-century figure.
- **⚠️ Face-to-face table width derivation, worked formula**: two facing
  40cm-deep place settings (40+40=80cm) plus a 20cm center serving strip
  (for platters/bottles/centerpieces) = **100cm minimum table width** for
  people seated facing each other. **Recommend increasing to 110cm** for
  a genuinely comfortable table, or **120-130cm** for a premium/larger
  table (a commonly available furniture-market width tier).
- **⚠️ 8-person table sizing, standard default for this source's own
  practice**: length = 3 × 60cm (the per-seat footprint's long side) =
  180cm, **usually rounded up to 190cm**; width per the derivation above,
  **110-120cm**. Framed as the standard table size drawn for houses
  roughly ≥200m² (family of 5-6 plus regularly-visiting guests/relatives);
  smaller households would scale the seat count down using the same
  60cm-per-seat unit.
- **⚠️ Table perimeter clearance rule, two tiers**: **minimum 0.6m (60cm)**
  clearance around the table's non-service sides — enough to push a chair
  back and stand comfortably, and to pass behind a seated diner without
  disturbing them. **On the side food is actually served from** (i.e.
  facing the kitchen/serving point), increase clearance to **0.9-1.0m**
  (source explicitly prefers the full 1.0m) — carrying dishes needs more
  room than just standing/sitting clearance.
- **⚠️ Round-table diameter figures, by seat count**: 5 seats →
  **95-100cm**; 6 seats → **115-120cm**; 8 seats (the source's own
  standard default) → **140-150cm**. **Formula given explicitly**:
  diameter = (seat count × per-seat width) ÷ π (3.14) — then **round the
  computed result up by 10-15cm** rather than using the raw formula
  output, since the bare formula tends to land at or slightly under the
  cited minimums. Oval tables are noted as combinatorially similar to
  rectangular ones and not covered separately.
- **⚠️ Open-plan sightline/zoning framework — a genuinely reusable
  planning lens, not house-specific**: in a shared kitchen-living-dining
  space, the dining table is often placed near the room's geometric
  center, functioning as the actual divider between the kitchen zone and
  the living/TV zone (three sub-zones sharing one open footprint). Many
  clients want the TV visible from every sub-zone (cooking, eating,
  lounging) simultaneously — achievable at a moderate open-plan size
  (~200-250m² cited, but the *principle* — check TV sightlines from every
  planned activity zone before finalizing furniture placement — applies
  at any scale). In a more elongated/narrow open-plan footprint, a
  **shelving unit placed behind the sofa** (decorated with books/plants)
  can visually screen the dining zone from the living zone while keeping
  the kitchen open behind it — described as "already closer to an
  apartment-style" solution, i.e. explicitly flagged by the source as
  the technique most relevant to a smaller/apartment-scale open-plan
  space rather than a sprawling house layout.
- **⚠️ Kitchen island/breakfast-bar as a *separate* zone from the formal
  dining table, a genuinely reusable functional distinction**: a formal
  dining table implies full table service (seating arrangement, serving
  dishes) and in this source's stated practice is realistically used
  mainly on **weekends/holidays/guest occasions**; a raised bar-height
  section of a kitchen island is the **everyday quick-breakfast zone**
  instead, saving the service overhead for routine weekday meals. **Bar
  height derivation**: standard kitchen counter height (~80-100cm, per the
  companion kitchen video) **+ 20cm** = the raised bar-counter height,
  producing a stepped island profile (lower prep side / raised eating
  side).
- **Table height, cited to a named 1985 Soviet furniture standard (ГОСТ
  13025-85)**: minimum underside-of-tabletop clearance **0.65m** from
  floor; top-surface height range up to **0.70-0.78m** (transcript reads
  "70 278" — ASR-garbled; most plausible reading is a ~0.70-0.78m range,
  flagged `ASR-uncertain`). Source notes modern furniture manufacturers
  don't strictly follow this old GOST anymore, and that a formula relating
  table height to a diner's own height exists (mentioned but the specific
  formula itself was not stated in this transcript — gap, not
  extractable). One example given: for an average Soviet-reference height
  of 1.65m, tabletop height ≈ 0.75m.
- **⚠️ Open-plan vs. enclosed-dining-room floor-area tradeoff, a genuinely
  reusable planning argument**: enclosing a dining zone behind partition
  walls means its own clearance/circulation buffer (the 0.6-1.0m rules
  above) can no longer be shared with an adjacent zone's own buffer (e.g.
  the living room's soft-seating circulation space) — each enclosed room
  needs its *own* full buffer, increasing total required floor area
  compared to an open-plan layout where two adjacent zones' buffers can
  overlap. **Practical recommendation for anyone considering a future
  partition**: reserve/plan the space for a future dividing wall from the
  start, even while keeping the space open initially — enables converting
  to an enclosed dining room later without a full replan.
