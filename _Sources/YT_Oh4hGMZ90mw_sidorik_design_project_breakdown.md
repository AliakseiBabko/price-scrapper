## Design Concept — Professional Design-Project Deliverable Structure (20-page breakdown)

**A second, independent studio's design-project page structure**,
corroborating and materially expanding this store's existing 28-item
Zemstandart/zemspro checklist (`web_zemspro_about_development`,
`Durable_Facts.md` "Design-Project Process & Deliverable Structure"):

1. Cover page (studio name/address).
2. Sheet list / table of contents.
3. Dimensioned as-built (pre-replanning) measuring plan — the raw
   developer-handover state, with all wall dimensions and per-room areas.
4. Demolition plan — walls to be removed marked in red, with demolished
   area calculated.
5. New-construction plan — new block walls (blue) and drywall/GKL
   partitions (green) distinguished by color, with area calculated;
   includes notes on door-opening and installation nuances.
6. Post-replanning as-built plan — final room dimensions/areas after new
   walls are built.
7. **Furniture layout plan** — furniture and appliances with exact
   dimensions; the speaker states explicitly that **room design begins
   from the furniture layout plan specifically**, not from finishes.
8. **Furniture specification** — a full itemized list (22 items in this
   example) with the specific store/source for each. Genuinely new
   practical point: sourcing 22 individual furniture items yourself
   (visiting/comparing each store) realistically costs about **one full
   week of time** by the speaker's own estimate; buying a complete
   ready-made interior set from a single retailer (his own example:
   traveling to Kyiv to buy everything from one supplier) is presented as
   a legitimate lower-budget alternative when a design fee isn't
   affordable.
9. Door/window opening schedule — exact door dimensions plus door model
   and purchase location (windows excluded in this project since they're
   kept from the developer).
10. **Flooring layout plan** — exact flooring material names, plank-laying
    starting point/direction, furniture contours shown as dashed lines,
    baseboard shown as a green line. Genuinely new mechanism: furniture
    contours are included specifically so the installer can see where a
    flooring run meets a built-in closet/kitchen edge — the worked example
    given is a wardrobe (гардеробная) where, without the contour reference,
    the flooring could end up laid parallel to the closet face and leave a
    visibly thin, awkward strip cut at the joint.
11. **Underfloor-heating layout plan** — heating-loop boundaries (red
    line) and furniture footprint (dashed orange line) shown together
    specifically so heating cable is never routed under furniture (where
    it would be blocked/trapped by cabinets or nightstands); thermostat
    installation locations also marked here.
12. Ceiling plan — material, paint color/type, drop distance from the
    slab, and **locations requiring extra structural backing/reinforcement
    for later fixture mounting** (e.g. for lighting).
13. **Lighting layout plan** — every fixture and LED strip with exact
    position/dimensions on the ceiling, a fixture table, and cable-exit
    routing notes for LED-strip power runs.
14. **Switch-to-fixture wiring/numbering scheme** — a numbered-pairing
    system (e.g. switch #12 controls fixtures numbered 12; switch #1
    controls fixtures numbered 1 and 9) so the electrician has an
    unambiguous mapping between switches and the specific fixtures they
    control, with the switch count matching the controlled-fixture-group
    count exactly.
15. Fixture specification sheet — every lighting fixture model and
    purchase source.
16. **Socket/switch placement plan, positioned relative to furniture** —
    every outlet and switch with position and mounting height, drawn with
    standardized symbols specifically chosen to be readable by non-
    specialists (not just electricians), plus a full outlet/switch
    specification table.
17. Plumbing/sanitary equipment layout — water-supply-point locations plus
    fixture specification and purchase sources.
18. **Room finishing schedule** — every wall numbered and color-coded on a
    plan, with a table specifying the exact finish for each numbered wall
    (worked example: "wall #2 = 3D panels," a wall with an accent
    geometric pattern called out separately for the bedroom).
19. Wet-room (bathroom) wall elevations with tile specification — called
    out by the speaker as **specifically important reference material for
    the tile installer**.
20. Kitchen wall elevation, including socket/switch placement tied to the
    kitchen cabinetry layout.
21. Visualization pages (3D renders) — used both for client sign-off and
    as supplementary reference for the construction crew.

## Design Concept — Practitioner's Own Critique/Additions to This Studio's Package

- **Missing explicit 90°-corner call-outs**: the speaker notes this
  particular project doesn't mark which corners must be squared to 90°,
  speculating this studio's own in-house construction crews already know
  which walls need it without it being documented — a **gap** if the same
  design project were handed to an independent contractor instead of the
  studio's own crew.
- **Recommended improvement**: order/bind the design-project sheets in the
  actual construction work sequence, not the studio's default
  presentation order — a practical usability suggestion from the
  contractor's-eye view, not a criticism of content completeness.
- **Recommended addition**: include cross-section drawings of complex wall/
  ceiling junction details for the construction crew — the speaker notes
  it isn't always obvious how to execute a complex ceiling detail without
  a section view. **This independently corroborates, word-for-word in
  substance, this project's own existing `Budgeting_Guide.md` §1.2
  recommendation** ("node/section drawings for junction details") sourced
  from `yt_BMmPLHVmnqw` — now a second, independent practitioner making
  the identical recommendation.

## Numeric Data — Design-Project Pricing Tiers (Belarus-likely, level 2, 2020)

- **Beginner designers**: $10-15/m² for the design project.
- **Established/professional designers**: $20-30/m² for the design
  project.
- **Named/reputation studios**: $30/m² and up, "to infinity" (no stated
  ceiling).
- **Speaker's own real example** (his own 61 m² apartment, same object as
  video 1): a design project with 3D visualization would cost
  approximately **$1,500** — $1,500 for 61 m², so ≈$25/m² (`arithmetic-exact`), falling inside the "established
  professional" tier above) — the speaker notes price varies by scope: a
  schemes/drawings-only package (no visualization) is markedly cheaper
  than a full visualized package, and price rises further if ongoing
  author's-supervision (авторский надзор) is added as a service.
- These figures were **not converted via `currency_converter.py`** since
  the source states them directly in USD; no RUB/BYN intermediate figure
  exists to convert.

## Design Concept — Why/Whether to Commission a Design Project

- **Speaker's personal disclosure**: he does not use a formal design
  project for his own current apartment (the video-1 object) — his own
  stated reasoning is 10+ years of trade experience means the planning
  already happens in his head, making a paid design project
  uneconomical for himself specifically; he does keep informal sketches/
  schemes, not a full package. Flagged as a personal exception, not a
  general recommendation to skip a design project.
- **Reasons for a design project (speaker's own list)**: (1) early
  visibility into the finished result via 3D visualization, correctable
  before construction; (2) a designer's specialized knowledge of
  color/material/furniture combinations exceeds a typical client's; (3) a
  dimensioned design project lets both materials and labor be quantified
  accurately for costing; (4) a design project measurably reduces
  rework/redo time during construction; (5) a designer functions as a
  neutral "arbiter" between a couple who disagree on choices (e.g.
  wallpaper or flooring) — offered half-humorously but treated as a real,
  recurring practical benefit.
- **Stated single con**: cost — this is presented as the only real
  argument against commissioning a design project, conditional on the
  project actually being professionally executed (dimensions, client
  wishes, and budget all reconciled).
