## Design Concept, Furniture/Built-ins, Mistakes/Warnings

- **Style-consistency self-diagnosis method**: assemble a personal collection of liked reference images (Pinterest or phone photos), then explicitly analyze what colors/forms/materials repeat across them to derive your own "base" style, without needing formal design vocabulary — a specific self-diagnosis technique for non-professionals.
- **⚠️ Color/material "rhyme" cohesion technique, genuinely useful, distinct from general palette-consistency content already logged**: repeat the same accent color or material at least twice across DIFFERENT zones/rooms even without a strict formal style (his examples: a stainless-steel kitchen detail echoed in a living-room lamp/accessory; a green sofa accent echoed via posters/pillows in other rooms) — this repetition creates a subtle sense of coherence even when mixing pieces from different eras/styles. A specific "echo, don't match exactly" cohesion method.
- **⚠️ 3-part "timelessness" framework, genuinely reusable even though parts restate existing content**: (1) natural materials (wood, cotton, linen, natural stone) age well and read as tactilely rich vs. plastic/MDF (restates); (2) patterns, if used at all, should be genuinely personal/individual, classic, or exclusive — NOT patterns found in every mass-market retailer — recommends unusual fabrics or vintage finds specifically to avoid a mass-market pattern signature (a genuinely specific "avoid the mass-market pattern signature" point); (3) trends should be applied only in easily-changeable elements (wall color, small decor, small furniture), never as the base/structural material — explicitly names wood-slat walls as a real example of a trend that read stylish ~5 years ago and now reads as poor taste even where structurally justified, and states he'd avoid using marble as a full base surface material for the same reason (fine as an accent, not as primary finish) — packages this as a generalized "trend-in-swappable-elements-only" structuring rule.
- **⚠️ "Vignette layering" / zone-within-a-room composition method — the standout content of this video, a genuinely valuable, well-developed, actionable methodology, worth dedicated prominence**: treat a room not as one single composition but as a collection of smaller self-contained "vignettes" (a dresser-top object grouping; a reading corner with a leather chair and two small paintings; an otherwise-empty transitional corner given a console + poster collection; an otherwise-blank bathroom wall given a picture + towel hook + small accessory table). Krasnov's own stated personal breakthrough as a designer: composing a room becomes far easier once you stop trying to design "the whole room at once" and instead build small layered clusters one at a time (an accent chair gets a side table + lamp + maybe art; a hung painting gets something placed beneath it like a chair or plant; a console gets objects added in layers — lamp, vase, small paintings). A specific, transferable, STEP-BY-STEP compositional METHOD, not just a "don't leave things empty" principle — this vault has extensive content on individual mistakes to avoid but comparatively little on a concrete, positive, step-by-step "how to actually compose a room" method.
- **⚠️ Unusual-object selection — two named, distinct methods**: (1) take an ordinary object/form but render it in an unexpected MATERIAL (his examples: a dining table with a massive metal base — ordinary table form, unexpected material choice adding character; a table lamp that looks entirely made of paper) — a "familiar form + unexpected material" technique; (2) alternatively choose an object with an unusual FORM instead (an armchair with pronounced sculptural lines, a lamp that reads as an art object, an unusual stool) — treating furniture as sculpture. Explicitly recommends deliberately "ugly"/odd/borderline-absurd objects as sources of genuine depth and life IF integrated thoughtfully, sourced from designer/author pieces, vintage collections, auctions, or flea/antique markets.
- **Explicit sequencing caveat, his own stated rule**: don't just impulse-buy random flea-market/auction pieces hoping they'll work — always plan/visualize placement in the actual room BEFORE acquiring the piece, then execute — "сначала есть проект, потом реализация" (project first, then execution).
- **⚠️ Contrast-via-scale technique, a deliberate counterpoint/nuance to existing scale-matching content, not a contradiction**: in a small dining room, hang an oversized painting (nearly wall-sized) rather than a small one; conversely, in a spacious room, use a deliberately SMALL/modest art object — the scale MISMATCH relative to the room is what catches the eye. States this same principle works with light fixtures too — a large pendant over a compact table or nightstand, or a minimalist small lamp in a large room, both create deliberate visual "tension"/accent through scale contrast. Flag explicitly as the INVERSE of the "match scale to context" content already logged extensively (e.g. the chandelier-to-room-size rule) — intentional scale CONTRAST used as a deliberate accent technique, requiring good judgment, not a universal rule.
- **Style-mixing contrast** — a painting in a heavy classical/ornate frame above the bed in an otherwise clean/modern bedroom (the era clash itself becomes the individualizing feature), or a vintage writing desk placed as the sole "anchor" object in an otherwise neutral, near-timeless room (becomes a genuine visual anchor precisely because it contrasts with everything else) — restates/extends existing personalization-through-vintage content but frames it explicitly as a CONTRAST technique. States his own personal preference for "mixing old and new" as a universal technique for keeping a space feeling considered rather than dated.
