## Mistakes / Warnings — Sequencing Before Installing the Inlet Node

- **Repeats and reinforces two mistakes already covered in this
  practitioner's earlier videos, both tied specifically to the inlet-node
  install**: (1) soundproof the sewage stack *before* boxing it in with
  drywall/tile — impossible to add after the wall is closed and finished,
  discovered only after move-in; (2) putty and paint the wall surface
  *before* mounting the inlet-node equipment on it — mounting first means
  puttying/painting has to be done working around pipes, the boiler, and
  the installation frame, versus just a small touch-up afterward if the
  wall is finished first.
- **A dedicated "plumbing equipment project" (проект на сантехническое
  оборудование), distinct from a design project**: the speaker had an
  engineer draft a project specifically for what equipment will be
  installed and its installation *sequence*, separate from a designer's
  project (which shows only supply/drain outlet locations and installation
  frame position). Framed explicitly as protection against plumber error —
  "even from an unqualified plumber found through classified ads" — since
  every nuance is worked out and agreed between engineer, homeowner, and
  installer in advance. `single-account`, but a genuinely new, reusable
  planning-process concept not previously captured in this store (distinct
  from the existing design-project checklist content on this channel's
  video 2, Round 1).
- **Ball valve (вводной кран) failure mode from misuse**: never use the
  main shutoff ball valve to partially throttle flow (cracked open rather
  than fully open/closed) — limescale builds up on the ball inside and can
  seize it, leaving you unable to shut off water in an emergency. The
  valve has exactly two valid positions (open/closed); **open and close it
  fully about once a month** to knock accumulated scale off the ball. An
  automated Neptun-brand leak-protection valve does this same monthly
  open/close cycle automatically.
- **A hydronic towel warmer left without its own leak-protection valve is
  "a delayed-action mine"** — a common mistake is installing leak-
  protection valves on the main hot/cold risers but skipping the
  towel-warmer branch. Directly corroborates
  `12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Hygienic_Shower_and_Towel_Warmer.md`'s
  existing point that a hydronic towel warmer often sits downstream of a
  leak-protection system's own valve and isn't covered by it — this source
  frames the fix as simply extending leak-protection coverage to that
  branch too, rather than the existing page's "second, fully separate
  leak-protection branch" framing; treat as two sources describing the
  same coverage gap with slightly different remediation framing, not a
  contradiction.
- **A related towel-warmer plumbing mistake**: soldering a bypass into the
  jumper/loop feeding a hydronic towel warmer in a way that lets water
  take the path of least resistance around (rather than through) the
  towel warmer — defeats its purpose even though water is technically
  still flowing in the loop.
- **Water-hammer arrestor (гаситель гидроударов) mounted on an extension
  fitting creates a stagnant water zone** where bacteria can grow — the
  speaker had to redo this mounting after installing an arrestor this way
  for installation convenience. Lesson: mount the arrestor directly, not
  on an extender, even if direct mounting is less convenient.
- **Riser shutoff window for inlet-node replacement work, a real timing
  data point**: the building's water-supply shutoff for this work lasted
  only 4 hours, in which time the plumber needed to swap the main shutoff
  valves and re-solder the towel-warmer jumper. Useful for scheduling this
  specific stage of work with a building's management company.

## Water Inlet Node — Fuller Component Sequence & Named Specs

Real, granular installation order for this project's own inlet node
(**more granular than this store's existing "mandatory minimum" list**,
which this source's order is consistent with but extends with brand-level
component specs):

вводной кран (main shutoff) → защита от протечки Neptun (leak protection)
→ грязевой фильтр (coarse filter) → счётчик воды (water meter) →
самопромывной фильтр с манометром (self-flushing fine filter + gauge) →
редуктор давления с манометром (pressure reducer + gauge) → обратный
клапан (check valve) → коллектор (manifold) → гаситель гидроударов
(water-hammer arrestor).

- **Main shutoff valve**: 1/2" NPT, both male and female threads; this
  build used Oventrop (FAR brand preferred but out of stock at the
  manufacturer at the time).
- **Neptun leak-protection system**: electric shutoff valve(s) (Italian
  Bugatti-brand valve body, wired to the control unit) + a control unit
  with Wi-Fi and built-in battery backup for power outages; both wired and
  wireless sensors supported in the same install; the control unit's
  housing can be unscrewed and rotated for horizontal or vertical mounting
  in any orientation. Per the speaker, plumber feedback rates Neptun as
  currently the best leak-protection brand on the market. `single-account`,
  `unverified` brand ranking claim.
- **Coarse filter (FAR)**: 300-micron mesh, rated to 25 bar, installed
  with the cleanout access facing down so the mesh can be unscrewed and
  cleaned. Adds a concrete number to this store's existing "commonly 500
  micron (also cited 300/600/800)" mesh-size range.
- **Self-flushing fine filter (FAR)**: 100-micron mesh, rated to 25 bar,
  can be mounted vertically or horizontally (rotatable body). **A specific
  mechanistic reason this filter is placed *before* the pressure reducer,
  not after**: because it can withstand the full 25 atm of line pressure —
  if it couldn't, it would have to go after the reducer instead, which
  would leave the reducer itself unprotected from debris in the water.
  This is a new, more specific mechanism than this store's existing
  "install after the meter, never before" fine-filter rule (which concerns
  a different filter position — post-meter vs. pre/post-reducer).
- **Pressure reducer + gauge**: a genuinely important distinguishing
  feature — **this reducer works in both static and dynamic conditions**
  (i.e. it still regulates pressure with the tap fully closed, not only
  while water is flowing) — cheaper reducers are stated to work *only* in
  dynamic mode (only while a tap is open), meaning they provide zero
  protection from a static overpressure event. **A stated real-world
  reason static protection matters**: a building's water system can be
  pressure-tested with elevated pressure, including at night, and a
  resident would only find out something burst afterward if unprotected.
  Directly extends `Water_Inlet_Node_Components.md`'s existing pressure-
  reducer content (which already covers noise reduction and high-pressure
  "slicing" mitigation) with this static-vs-dynamic distinction.
- **Check valve**: needed because backflow (hot water intruding into a
  cold riser, or vice versa) is "not rare" in practice — cited causes
  include a failed mixer (the speaker names Chinese mixers as a common
  culprit) or simply a missing check valve where one should be. Positioned
  after the reducer, before the hot/cold manifolds.
- **Manifold (FAR)**: comes with pre-printed hot/cold labels plus
  insert tags for naming individual branches/consumers. **Radial
  ("лучевая") distribution through a manifold is stated as better than
  tee/branch ("тройниковая") distribution on every count considered**:
  (1) serviceability — a single branch/valve can be isolated and swapped
  without affecting the rest; (2) fewer embedded fittings in the screed —
  only one fitting plus the wall outlet per branch ends up buried, versus
  more branch fittings under a tee layout; (3) **per-branch flow
  balancing prevents the shared classic complaint of a shower running cold
  or scalding when another fixture (dishwasher, kitchen tap, toilet
  flush) draws water simultaneously** — a concrete mechanism for why
  manifold distribution avoids this, extending this store's and
  `Water_Inlet_Node_Components.md`'s existing serviceability/isolation
  framing of the manifold's purpose with this specific balancing benefit.
  In this apartment, a manifold is used only in the bathroom and toilet;
  the kitchen (a single point) doesn't justify one.
- **Water-hammer arrestor**: should sit under every mixer, or — as done
  here — a single shared arrestor can serve an entire manifold-fed branch
  group at short pipe distances (works fine "on such small distances,"
  per the speaker). In the kitchen, arrestors are instead placed
  individually at the end of the run, right before the hot and cold
  mixer connections.
- **Kitchen cold-water branch outlet count**: 3 separate outlets from the
  manifold/branch — one for the mixer, one for a filter, one for the
  dishwasher — rather than a single outlet split downstream with tees,
  explicitly because routing everything through tees afterward is "not
  very convenient." Complements this store's existing kitchen
  sink+dishwasher rough-in ordering rule
  (`12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Rough_Plumbing_Sequencing.md`)
  with a "why" for the separate-outlets approach.
- **Joint assembly material**: components hand-assembled on a table before
  installation, using flax ("лён") plus a sealing paste on every joint —
  the material the speaker states he trusts most for this. `single-account`,
  `unverified` (a personal material preference, not a mechanism claim).
