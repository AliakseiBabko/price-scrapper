## Plumbing

- **Decision criteria: when to keep vs. replace an existing convector
  radiator.** Keep it if: undamaged (not dented/rusted), heats well, and
  has its own dedicated shutoff valve and control/regulator valve.
  Replace it if: bulky, space-consuming, and **not adjustable/regulated**
  — the deciding factor is functional (regulation capability), not just
  cosmetic condition. `confirmed`.
- **Pre-purchase step**: consult the installing specialist (ideally bring
  them along) before buying a replacement radiator — buy only after all
  materials are confirmed and delivered to site, not before.
- **Critical safety warning: verify the shutoff is on the correct riser
  before cutting.** A worker confirming shutoff from the basement can
  make a mistake and close the wrong riser. If the convector's own
  shutoff valve is missing/unclear, **do not cut with a grinder before
  confirming** — cutting a pressurized water pipe with a corded
  (mains-powered) grinder creates a near-guaranteed electrocution risk if
  water contacts the tool; if uncertain, burn/pierce a test hole first
  with a torch rather than grinding blind. `confirmed`, safety-critical,
  stated from direct practitioner experience with this exact failure mode.
- **Material-matching rule: never mix heating-system pipe materials** —
  match the replacement radiator's connecting pipe material to whatever
  the existing riser system is made of (polypropylene-to-polypropylene,
  metal-to-metal); a metal-to-polypropylene (or reverse) transition is
  called "as unnatural as" reversing it, and **every material transition
  is a threaded joint — a structurally weaker, leak-prone point** vs. a
  continuous run or a welded joint. All-metal welded runs specifically
  avoid internal-diameter narrowing that threaded metal-to-metal
  transitions introduce.
- **Concealment rationale — false-wall-hidden risers/pipes/radiators are
  the "good renovation" standard, with a quantified windowsill-depth
  cost**: the common belief that concealing risers "wastes usable floor
  area" is called a myth — the space above ~1m height (above a window) is
  never functionally used regardless, and the real tradeoff is a
  **~15cm loss of windowsill depth** (worked reasoning: hiding riser +
  supply + return pipes behind a false wall costs roughly 10cm off
  sill width). Stated conclusion: a wider windowsill is more valuable
  than the equivalent depth reclaimed under it — worth the depth loss for
  full pipe/joint access, no soldered-in-wall joints to worry about, and
  no added cost from concealment materials (vs. the explicitly rejected
  in-wall-concealment approach, called out as far more expensive and
  risk-prone due to hidden joints). **A third option (leaving pipes
  fully exposed, boxed only around ceilings) is explicitly called
  unacceptable for a "good, quality renovation."**
- **Weld/joint prep for metal pipe runs**: joints should be formed either
  by pre-heating with hot flanging or via a "glass"/sleeve-style
  connection (ASR-uncertain exact term, `single-account` technique
  description).
- **Bypass/jumper (байпас) requirement**: if closing a radiator's shutoff
  valves, a jumper/bypass **must** be present, or heating-medium
  circulation stops entirely in that branch, causing the floor above or
  below to go cold. `confirmed`, mechanism-explained.
- **Pipe bending must be cold, with a proper pipe bender tool — never
  heat-bent by hand.** A hand/heat-bent pipe flattens at the bend,
  reducing flow cross-section — demonstrated side-by-side comparison
  (cold-bent pipe retains round cross-section; heat-bent pipe visibly
  flattened, worse examples nearly closed). If a installer works without
  a proper pipe-bending tool and heats pipes to bend them by hand, the
  source recommends declining their services. `confirmed`, demonstrated.
- **Threaded-joint sealing material**: flax/tow ("лён") in combination
  with joint compound is the only currently acceptable sealing method for
  durable threaded joints per this source — no other sealing method is
  described as acceptable at time of filming.
- **Riser branch-point rule (50cm rule)**: the transition from a
  standard-spacing riser pipe pair to the radiator's actual connection
  spacing (often ~50cm wider) must happen **immediately at the riser**,
  not partway along the run to the radiator. Branching mid-run at an
  angle instead of at the riser creates unintended low points where air
  bubbles collect instead of rising freely to the top — resulting in
  intermittent airlock and the radiator periodically failing to heat.
  `confirmed`, mechanism-explained, genuinely specific and checkable.
- **Pipe slope rule**: pipes should run with a slight rise from radiator
  outward (return line trending down, supply trending up) so air can
  travel unobstructed through the radiator rather than pooling at a low
  point. Directly related to/reinforces the 50cm branch-point rule above.
- **Pipe corrosion-protection sequence (3 steps, order matters)**: (1)
  sand every joint/pipe surface until a light sheen appears; (2) degrease
  thoroughly — not just wiped with solvent, must be saturated enough to
  remove all sanding residue; (3) only then paint, **by brush only, never
  spray** (aerosol doesn't function as a rust converter and can't coat
  the wall-facing side of the pipe — leaves only a thin dust layer with
  no real protection). **Explicit warning against "paint directly onto
  rust/dirt" product label claims** — painting over rust/dirt without
  prep creates a surface crust that traps moisture and accelerates
  corrosion underneath, faster than leaving it unpainted. Brush
  application should be generous/saturating, not economized — dripped
  paint on a subfloor (to be covered by finish flooring/false wall) is
  explicitly framed as a trivial cost next to a pipe that later rusts
  through and leaks.
- **No reinforcing clamps/straps needed on concealed metal pipe runs**:
  metal pipe is stated as strong enough unsupported behind a false wall;
  a clamped/strapped section is explicitly identified as **the most
  likely point for that pipe to corrode and leak first** (mechanism not
  further explained beyond "that's where it rusts fastest").
