## Mistakes / Warnings — Previous Contractor's Bad XPS Substrate Prep

- **⚠️ Named prior-contractor defect, found and being demolished/redone**: the previous contractor laid XPS insulation board (пеноплекс) directly onto a badly uneven, unlevel substrate across the whole apartment, then laid reinforcing mesh on top in preparation for a screed pour — without first correcting the substrate. **⚠️ Named failure mechanism**: XPS laid over an uneven base doesn't sit flat against the floor; under the weight of a poured screed, unsupported "wave" sections of the board can crush/deform, undermining the whole floor buildup. **Correct sequence, stated directly**: clean/level bumps in the substrate first, achieving a flat plane (not necessarily a perfectly level plane — flatness, not levelness, is the requirement at this stage), pouring a leveling compound where needed, and only then laying XPS onto that corrected substrate.

## Materials / Mistakes — Window Protective Packing Tape, a Named Material Distinction

- **⚠️ Paper packing tape vs. PVC packing tape on new windows, a real material-selection warning**: paper tape (used to protect frames/hardware/glass during construction) looks fine initially but **leaves adhesive residue within about a month and is difficult to remove cleanly** — a real problem regardless of frame material, but described as a genuine "tragedy" specifically on **wood-framed windows**, where residue removal after ~6 months means the wood finish must be stripped, refinished, and repainted. **PVC packing tape (commonly orange or blue) is the correct choice** — the deciding factor is whether the manufacturer specifies how long that specific tape can safely remain on the surface and be removed cleanly afterward; PVC tape of this kind can reportedly stay on for 6 months to a year and come off without residue.

## Engineering / Systems — Floor-Height Compensation With Expanded Clay Under Screed, an Old-Fund Weight Constraint

- **⚠️ Named technique: expanded clay (керамзит) mixed with cement "milk" as a lightweight fill under electrical conduit, used specifically where floor height varies significantly room-to-room in an old building**: this apartment has a real floor-height variance of **≈15cm** across different rooms (old-fund construction, uneven historically). Rather than pour 10-12cm of screed in the lower rooms (adding, per the speaker, "several tons" of extra dead load onto an old structural slab not designed for it), the lower rooms are filled with expanded clay mixed with cement milk (added specifically so the clay aggregate doesn't stay loose/friable) up to grade, conduit is laid on top of that fill, and only a normal-thickness screed is poured over the top — achieving a level final floor without loading the structure with a full-depth screed pour in the low areas.
