## ⚠️ Cost Drivers — a Style-Level Cost Floor, and Where It Bites

The two most transferable claims in the source, and both bear directly on this project's budget-tier framework:

- **⚠️ Minimalism can be executed cheaply and still read correctly; muji cannot.** Her words: minimalism "можно сделать из дешёвых материалов… из имитации каких-то материалов", get the storage right, and it will still read as minimalism. **"А вот в муджи так не прокатит."** The reason is structural rather than aesthetic: **in muji, material quality is not a tier choice within the style — it is the style's defining content.** So the two styles have genuinely different *floors*, not merely different typical spends.
- **⚠️ Scandi is achievable over a developer's base finish** ("вы можете сделать Сканди в ремонте от застройщика"). She presents it as the least renovation-dependent of the group: it asks for a colour range and permits bright accents, and does not require furniture of simple form. **Of the five styles discussed, this is the only one she says survives an unrenovated shell.**

Together these give a **rough ordering of style cost-floors from her account: scandi (lowest, works over a builder finish) → minimalism (cheap execution possible with imitation materials) → japandi / wabi-sabi (unstated) → muji (highest floor, quality is non-negotiable).** ⚠️ **The ordering is hers and is asserted, not costed** — no figures anywhere in the source. Treat it as a shortlist heuristic, not a budget input.

## Design Concept — the Muji Palette

- **Wood, beige, white, and warm grey / warm white.** ⚠️ **Her emphasis is that these are used *together, all at once*, not selected among** — "сочетание всех их сразу в одном интерьере, а не на выбор".
- **All muted, all light. No bright shades and no colour accents at all** — she names artwork specifically as something not used for an accent here.
- **⚠️ Muji has no accent focal point whatsoever**, which she draws as the sharp line against plain minimalism: minimalism will happily take a statement pendant or a signature sofa as a point of attraction; **muji admits none.**

## Design Concept — What Muji Is

- **Origin**: a Japanese retail brand founded **1980**, whose approach became a lifestyle and then an interior style. **Basis is Zen Buddhism** — concentration on the present, non-attachment to material possessions — which she offers as the explanation for the style's content.
- **⚠️ The defining principle is durability as a counter to consumption culture.** Objects are chosen so that quality makes them last, explicitly against brands that build to a season or to a trend cycle ("быстрая мода"). She frames her whole blog as being about "вечно актуальных интерьерах" rather than fast fashion — a consistent position worth noting when weighing her trend content elsewhere.
- **⚠️ The no-author principle.** Pieces are deliberately *not* recognisable — "все предметы будто лишены автора, ноунеймы". Her worked contrast is a Louis Vuitton bag, recognisable even as a fake, versus a muji object with no signature at all. **Practical consequence: much muji furniture is custom-made (на заказ) to the required dimensions** — a plain rectangular console built to your parameters rather than a named designer's piece. **This is a procurement pattern, not just an aesthetic one**, and it happens to suit a market where joinery is made to order anyway.
- **Wood is the dominant finish and appears on walls as well as floors** — by her account more wood than paint by area.
- **Forms are rectangular and low** ("по-японски низкие"); round, oval and spherical forms are rare, lighting excepted.
- **Traditional Japanese materials**: rice-paper / washi lighting, and wood-and-paper blinds.
- MUJI retail leans heavily on **containerised storage** — boxes, jars, baskets — which she reads as part of the style rather than merchandising.

## ⚠️ Style Compatibility — the Directly Usable Part

**Muji combines with scandi, wabi-sabi and mid-century. It does not combine with classic, art deco, or other decorative styles** — she argues the incompatibility is at the level of approach and philosophy, not surface appearance, which is why it cannot be patched over.

**Her own stated preference is muji diluted** — with an interesting light fitting, a sofa, and specifically **mid-century chairs**. ⚠️ **Worth recording that she departs from her own rule here**, and says so openly. Consistent with the mid-century material in `A5G04uPvzL0` (Round 3).

## The Differentiation Set

Compact definitions, useful mainly as vocabulary when reading other sources:

| Style | Her definition |
|---|---|
| **Muji** | Minimalism stripped of designer pieces; only high-quality materials; a fixed narrow palette; wood-dominant; built to last |
| **Wabi-sabi** | Minimalism that accepts imperfection — irregular forms, handmade objects, rough natural finishes such as coarse plaster |
| **Scandi** | The most decorative of the group; permits bright colour accents; no requirement that furniture be simple in form |
| **Japandi** | Scandi with more minimalism — ornament removed, Japanese elements added |
| **Minimalism** | The broad category; muji is a branch of it. Permits authored/designer pieces and accent focal points |

She also claims **Japanese minimalism dates to the 10th century**, likewise grounded in Zen Buddhism, and predates the Western minimalism that began gaining ground in Europe and America **from the 1930s**. ⚠️ **Historical claim, `unverified`, and outside her professional competence** — recorded because it is stated, not because it is evidenced.
