## Durable Facts — Masonry/Insulation Construction Technique

- **Layout marking method**: mark block-course lines on the floor using project-specified offsets — insulation-layer thickness, plus a stated 100 mm setback, plus a stated 1,350 mm dimension (both project-specific figures from this speaker's own drawings, not general constants). Windowsill-level height for the loggia's insulated knee-wall was set at exactly 700 mm from the future screed level in this example, established using a rotary/elevation laser leveled to a fixed reference (99 cm from the finished stairwell floor at the door) and transferred across the room. `confirmed` as the demonstrated technique; the specific dimensions are project-specific, not universal.
- **Mark transfer for durability**: reference marks are always additionally copied onto corners/edges, because a mark on a flat wall surface is likely to get covered in mortar splatter and lost during the build.
- **Paint exposed metal elements (angle brackets, embedded metal) in two coats** to prevent future rust — including painting the cut/exposed ends of blocks near metal, since rust is stated to originate from unpainted edges/ends first.
- **First-course mortar bed should be generously thick, not minimal** — a thick first-row mortar bed lets the mason use it as a leveling "shock absorber" to true up the whole first course against an uneven builder sub-floor (which may be significantly out of level); never economize on mortar for the first row. Getting the first course level and square is described as the single most important step, since all subsequent courses depend on it.
- **Spray foam should be used sparingly** — it expands considerably after application; excess is trimmed off afterward, and over-application is unnecessary and wasteful.
- **Avoid smearing adhesive/mortar excess across surfaces that will later receive gypsum-based plaster** — the two materials can chemically interact/react, and any surface irregularities from smeared adhesive are better left to be corrected by the subsequent plaster coat rather than by trying to perfectly smooth the adhesive itself.
- **Radiators can be recessed into a cut niche within the block knee-wall** rather than requiring a separate false wall built around them for concealment — saves interior floor space and avoids extra framing.
- **A shared countertop/desk surface built over the loggia's insulated parapet** was used in this example to double as a workspace, with a radiator recessed beneath it and vent grilles on both sides to let radiant heat reach both the loggia and the adjoining room.
