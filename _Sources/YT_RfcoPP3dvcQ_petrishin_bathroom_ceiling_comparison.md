## Bathroom Ceiling Type Comparison — Slatted, Cassette, Glass/Mirror, Plastic, Stretch

1. **Material/type taxonomy for a bathroom ceiling**: stretch (натяжной),
   plastic panel (пластиковый), slatted metal (реечный — aluminum or
   steel), cassette (кассетный, French-design variant of slatted), and
   glass/mirror (Armstrong-frame system). Fiberglass/glass-plastic
   composite panels are described as less eco-friendly; metal is
   considered safer/more familiar as a material class.
2. **Slatted-ceiling quality assessment method and named-brand tiers**:
   judge quality by metal thickness, coating, and brand/manufacturer.
   **Албес/Bart** = budget tier — thinner aluminum, visibly dents when
   an installer clips it into place. **Cesal** (marketed as French
   design, actually manufactured in Russia) = mid tier — thicker
   aluminum with a laminated coating, looks more expensive, wide color
   range. **An unnamed "America"-branded line** = premium tier, priciest
   of the three. **Common buyer mistake flagged**: wanting the
   established brand's quality but buying an externally-similar-looking
   rail from a general hardware store (Leroy Merlin, OBI) to save money
   — the substitute often develops rust within ~6 months, and the first
   giveaway at install time is how flimsy/thin the metal feels compared
   to genuine stock (visibly dents on clip-fastening vs. staying rigid).
3. **Real worked cost example, 1.70m × 1.70m bathroom, material only
   (fully fitted incl. corners/hangers/stringers)**: narrow rail with a
   center insert strip (priciest configuration) **≈8,000 RUB (≈$120)**;
   the same manufacturer's wider rail without an insert **≈6,000 RUB
   (≈$90)**; the budget Албес/Bart line **≈6,800 RUB (≈$100,
   `ASR-uncertain` exact framing)**; the premium "America" line **≈9,000
   RUB (≈$140)**. Conversions via trailing-6-month USD/RUB average
   ending 2018-11-21.
4. **Client-provided-dimensions accuracy matters — real installer
   margin practice**: a bathroom can be trapezoidal (non-rectangular)
   even if a client reports the same length on both sides; installers
   deliberately use a 4-meter rail with margin rather than cutting to
   a client-reported exact size, since a field-cut rail is both risky
   and imprecise — professional installers never cut to the exact
   reported dimension.
5. **Buying-tip heuristic**: choose the quality tier you actually want
   and buy that same brand's cheaper finish/configuration to save money,
   rather than switching to a lower-quality brand — "good goods are
   never cheap" framing, paired with the earlier rust/dent failure mode
   as the concrete cost of ignoring it.
6. **Slatted-ceiling drop height by fixture type**: a simple installation
   with only surface-mounted (not recessed) light fixtures drops the
   ceiling roughly **3-4cm**; installing recessed spot lighting needs
   **7-10cm** of drop, because the fixture housing's own mounting "legs"
   need room to be spread out and seated in the rail. **⚠️ Backing
   material (wood blocks or plywood scraps) must be planned in behind
   the ceiling wherever a heavier fixture — a real chandelier/shade
   fixture, not just a recessed spot — will be hung**, sized to that
   fixture's actual weight, decided before installation once the
   fixture itself is chosen.
7. **DIY-feasible slatted-ceiling installation sequence, described as
   genuinely approachable for a self-managed renovation**: mark a level
   line around the room's full perimeter; install a perimeter angle
   track along that line; install suspension combs ("гребенки") at 2-3
   points, or hanging wire suspensions anchored to the base ceiling with
   screws/dowels; snap the rail slats into the track (a "kit/
   constructor" system); peel the manufacturer's protective transport
   film off the finished slats afterward (a mother-of-pearl-looking film
   sometimes mistaken by a first-time installer for part of the actual
   finish). **Basic tools only**: metal shears, hammer drill,
   screwdriver — no specialized skill required, many tutorial videos
   exist.
8. **Cassette-ceiling installation and layout parallel to the classic
   tile-layout rule**: uses a triangular support-rail system instead of
   the slatted system's straight track; grid spacing must be **exactly
   30cm** to match standard 30×30cm cassette size. **Cut cassettes at
   the room's edges should be arranged symmetrically starting from the
   center**, avoiding narrow strips at the edges — the same underlying
   principle as this page's existing tile centered-layout rule, applied
   to a ceiling grid.
9. **Named practical advantage of both slatted and cassette systems**:
   both disassemble non-destructively, letting a section be removed to
   dry out a real leak/flood behind the ceiling and reassembled
   afterward without damage — a genuine serviceability advantage over a
   stretch or drywall ceiling.
10. **Glass/mirror (Armstrong-frame) ceiling**: built on a grid frame
    system using different rail lengths (30cm/60cm/120cm) adjusted via
    hanging suspensions, then glass panels are set into the completed
    frame — same underlying construction principle as slatted/cassette,
    different infill material. Mirror finish visually increases
    perceived room volume; a glass panel with internal backlighting plus
    a sandblasted ("пескоструй") finish produces a "glowing sky" effect.
    **Printed/stained-glass patterned glass ceilings need designer
    coordination** specifically to avoid clashing with a patterned tile
    choice in the same room (two competing patterns read as visual
    clutter). **Real price point: Armstrong glass system from ≈3,000
    RUB/m² (≈$50/m²)**, explicitly stated as not the cheapest option.
11. **Matte vs. glossy finish, condensation visibility**: matte glass/
    plastic/mirror surfaces hide water-droplet marks noticeably better
    than glossy — a real practical reason (not just aesthetic) to
    default to matte for bathroom ceilings.
12. **Plastic-panel ceiling, cheapest tier, a real durability/health
    caution**: cheap-grade plastic exposed to prolonged hot-water
    steam/heat can degrade, releasing an odor and (per the source's own
    framing) potentially unhealthy off-gassing over time — quality
    varies significantly by manufacturer/plastic grade; a genuinely
    good-quality plastic panel doesn't have this problem. **Installation
    note**: can be stapled to a wood-batten frame (if recessed spots are
    needed, same 7-10cm drop logic as slatted) or glued directly to an
    existing ceiling with construction adhesive if no drop/lighting
    change is needed — and can be shaped around an existing ventilation
    duct/pipe to conceal it.
13. **Stretch-ceiling mold/yellowing myths addressed directly by the
    builder, corroborating and extending this page's existing content**:
    mold behind any ceiling type (stretch, slatted, plastered/painted,
    or drywall) is a real risk **only if an actual leak from a neighbor
    above persists** — the ceiling material itself doesn't cause mold,
    it just changes how quickly the problem becomes visible (a
    plastered/painted ceiling shows staining sooner since you can see
    through it; a stretch ceiling hides the same leak longer). A stretch
    ceiling's tight perimeter seal (a compaction/gasket tape at the
    wall-to-ceiling junction) does genuinely resist small ambient
    moisture, but **a real leak volume will still escape through any
    penetration point (a recessed-light cutout, or the perimeter itself
    under enough water)** — it is not leak-proof, only leak-resistant
    for ordinary ambient humidity. **Yellowing**: a stretch ceiling can
    yellow over a ~10-year+ service life, and specifically around a
    light-fixture cutout **if halogen bulbs are used** (halogens run
    hot) — a competent installer avoids halogen with a stretch ceiling
    entirely, or uses only a weak halogen if unavoidable; **LED
    eliminates this risk** since it doesn't generate meaningful heat.
    Sergey's own 3-year personal account with a bathroom stretch ceiling:
    no yellowing, mold, or condensation observed. `single-account` for
    the personal anecdote.
14. **Design guidance from Ekaterina Popova (design blogger, named
    collaborator)**: default to plain white matte (or semi-gloss)
    finishes with **no decorative inserts, gold/silver trim, or
    ornamentation** on a slatted or cassette ceiling — decorative
    add-ons are a visible "budget solution" signal a professional design
    avoids; a plain white matte slatted ceiling is visually
    unremarkable, reading as an ordinary ceiling rather than announcing
    its own material. A wood-look printed slat finish works
    specifically because ceiling height makes it hard to tell printed
    film from real wood at a glance. **A curtain-track niche must be
    communicated to the ceiling installer/measurer before the initial
    measurement visit**, since it requires the stretch-film to be
    specially shaped for it — corroborates and extends this page's
    existing concealed-curtain-track note (Round 6).

**Practical verdict given jointly**: for a tight budget/DIY project,
slatted ceiling is the practical default (cheap, minimal skill needed)
but reads as visually dated; stretch ceiling is the more modern/
practical step-up if budget allows; for a large country-house bathroom
with high ceilings, a drywall ceiling with LED strip lighting is worth
the extra labor cost.
