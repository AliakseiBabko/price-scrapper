## ⚠️ Electrical / Lighting — the Densest Part

- **⚠️ Track quantity matters.** Tracks are her preferred format, **but too many read bulky and draw attention, especially in small areas.** **Prefer simple parallel lines over a square or rectangle of tracks on the ceiling.**
- **⚠️ Standing with your back to a track blocks its light onto the worktop** — hence the need for backsplash lighting. **And a much more specific placement figure than Round 2's version of this rule: where the upper cabinets are shallow, put point spots at about 40–45 cm from the wall** — i.e. between the line of 60-deep cabinets and 30–35-deep ones — **so they light exactly the strip of worktop between you and the shallow fronts.**
- **⚠️ Two pendant zones (island plus dining table) — three valid resolutions.** The common mistake is an active, accent fixture over **both**, so the eye can't tell where the accent is and attention scatters:
  1. **The same restrained pendant over both.**
  2. **An accent fixture over one zone and plain ceiling light over the other.** **Her own usual practice: accent the dining zone, and give the island directional track or spot light.**
  3. **No pendant at all over one of the zones.**
- **⚠️ Island pendant height: it can come down to 150–170 cm from the floor.** You won't hit your head because it sits outside the head zone; it lights better and composes better. **Cross-checks with the 75–80 cm-above-tabletop (~1.5 m) dining figure from `A5G04uPvzL0`** in the same round.

## ⚠️ The Best Statement of Why the Flush-Junction Rule Matters

She has repeated "don't glue a finish onto a finished wall" across five videos now. **This one finally supplies the perceptual mechanism, and it generalises well beyond tiling:**

**A wall material applied as a thin surface-glued overlay reads as an error.** The brain registers the small offset — just the thickness of the panel — as a construction defect: **"вроде попадаешь, но вроде не попадаешь"**, which is an unpleasant in-between.

**⚠️ The fix is a genuine either/or: land it perfectly flush, OR deliberately separate it far enough that alignment clearly wasn't attempted — she suggests setting the material 10–15 cm off the base wall.** What fails is the near-miss.

**And a scoped exception**: the two-materials-in-one-plane rule **does not apply to mirrors**, because "зеркало ломает помещение." A mirror inserted in the same plane as slats is fine and can create the expansion effect in a narrow spot.

## Design Concept — Material and Tone Discipline

- **⚠️ Don't wrap a material around internal corners into L or U configurations.** She doesn't use Г- or П-shaped constructions in minimalism, nor bend a material around internal corners. In the submitted kitchen the marble covered three planes at three different heights, which reads as "шоурум декоров в квартире." **Better: stop the marble at the splashback, and make the tall-unit end panel the same colour as its own fronts.**
- **⚠️ Don't put two materials in one plane** — a tall unit in MDF enamel continuing into marble-look porcelain within the same plane. Either separate them clearly (a niche), or give the whole run one finish.
- **⚠️ Two wood tones — rejected again** (floor vs island; and a niche in a darker wood elsewhere). **Second statement of the one-wood-tone rule in this round**, here carefully scoped to architectural minimalism.
- **⚠️ Wood grain direction should follow the object's proportions**: on an elongated horizontal island, run the grain longitudinally — **rotate the sheet 90° if necessary.** The same "elongated forms are pleasant" argument she applies to tile format and pillow shape.
- **A worktop colour-matched to the fronts is praised as a strong minimalist move**, and she points to her own podcast (`KI2GvB0jzHs`, Round 2) for the method — which is exactly the stone-first-then-enamel sequencing recorded there.
- **Black used sparingly is fine** and she praises the restraint here — consistent with, and the positive counterpart to, the black-accessory glut she criticises in `Bnuim5NjgCU`.

## ⚠️ Undertone Coordination Across Materials — the "Dirt" Effect With a Fix

The concrete/microcement wall was chosen in a **neutral grey** tone that sits badly in an otherwise warm interior. **Her fix: make the concrete a touch warmer — and do the same with the chair fabric**, since the chairs currently match the concrete but the pair matches nothing else in the flat.

**Her general statement of the rule: an active concrete texture plus a neutral or cool undertone, dropped into a warm interior, produces the dirt effect** — the same three-cause analysis as `haM4H-b-bZM` (Round 2), now applied to a live case with a corrective rather than stated abstractly.

## Design Concept — Two Reusable Room Solutions

- **⚠️ The awkward pier between two windows** — a common problem after removing a partition to merge kitchen and living room. Three treatments: **(a) run the curtains continuously across both windows and the pier**, so the textile isn't interrupted; **(b) deliberately accent the pier**, e.g. with panels; **(c) a picture plus a console** if space allows.
- **⚠️ Doors: floor-to-ceiling is the best option in architectural minimalism**, whatever the finish. **If the openings can't be full height and the doors take a finish different from the walls, add a matching transom panel ("антресолька") above the door** — orderable in the same finish as the leaf. It raises the opening visually and harmonises with any adjacent full-height sliding door. She notes the slats and mirror in this hallway run to the ceiling while the doors don't, and wants the doors pulled up toward them.

**Backlighting behind wood panels** to separate the wood decor from a concrete-look base wall is praised as a good move.
