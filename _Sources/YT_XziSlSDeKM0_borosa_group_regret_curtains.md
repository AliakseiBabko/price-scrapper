## Rules / Heuristics — Six Named Window-Treatment Types and Use Cases

1. **Портьеры (drapes)** — the classic, most common type; full panels
   drawn closed to fully cover the window; ideal for full blackout.
2. **Римские шторы (Roman blinds)** — a flat panel that concertina-folds
   upward, fully clearing the window opening; two Roman blinds can be
   layered on one window (one for blackout, one for diffusing), or paired
   with a roller blind for the same effect.
3. **Рулонные шторы (roller blinds)** — used specifically when the window
   should read as fully open with no bulky sheer/drape competing for
   attention (e.g. to showcase an outside view); available in blackout or
   diffusing versions.
4. **Жалюзи (blinds/louvers, horizontal or vertical)** — light is
   adjustable via lamella angle; metal or wood is preferred over the
   "cheap office plastic" stereotype the source explicitly pushes back
   on. Suits large modern spaces (living rooms, dining rooms, spa zones)
   where a curtain would look visually out of place; dark-colored
   louvers read as geometric/structural without drawing extra attention.
5. **Плиссе (pleated shades)** — visually similar to blinds; can be
   diffusing or near-blackout. **Real motorized example**: a penthouse
   rooftop-office renovation used motor-driven pleated shades so the
   owner could fully close off the rooftop office space on demand.
6. **Тюль (sheer curtain), named as an auxiliary sixth type** — doesn't
   block light like the other five, but limits outside visibility into
   the room while still letting inbound light through and preserving
   partial visibility from inside looking out.

## Numeric Data — Material Vocabulary, Formulas, and Real Project Figures

- **⚠️ Blackout vs. dimout material distinction, precise vocabulary**:
  "Blackout" fabric has an acrylic light-reflective coating layer that
  fully blocks sunlight — usable on classic drapes, Roman blinds, or
  roller blinds alike. "Dimout" fabric blocks roughly **70-90%** of light
  (depending on curtain length) and is used when full blackout isn't
  needed. First explicit numeric definition of "dimout" in this batch.
- **⚠️ Curtain-width calculation formula, with named coefficients by
  header type — the only source in this batch giving a full numeric
  formula**: width = (rod length × gather-ratio coefficient) + hem
  allowance. Coefficients: **1.5-2 for curtains on fabric loops/tabs; 2
  for curtains on grommets/eyelets ("люверсы"); 2.5 for pencil-pleat tape
  or triple-pleat headers.**
- **⚠️ Recessed-niche depth formula, more mechanically explicit than
  `YT__F7HqN0-HyQ`'s range-only figure**: niche depth = (radiator/
  windowsill projection from the wall + 2-3cm) + (5-8cm per curtain
  layer/row, depending on type). **Real project range cited: 20-25cm** —
  somewhat deeper than designer-notes' 15-20cm figure; recorded as a
  difference in reference case/fabric assumptions, not treated as a
  contradiction (neither source gives enough detail to reconcile
  precisely).
- **In-floor convector caution**: curtains should not cover an in-floor
  convector — trapping heat behind the fabric keeps it from reaching the
  room. A genuinely new HVAC-interaction caution not present elsewhere in
  this batch.

## Rules / Heuristics — Curtain-Length Regimes, with a Motorized-Track Mechanism

- **⚠️ Four named length regimes, each with a stated reason to choose it
  (more mechanically detailed than `YT__F7HqN0-HyQ`'s equivalent
  content)**:
  1. **Fabric lying on the floor** — appropriate specifically for
     floor-to-ceiling panoramic windows needing blackout, since other
     lengths can't achieve full blackout as reliably; can develop minor
     puckering/shrinkage, especially after washing. Complex-weave/
     stretchy mesh fabrics are often manufacturer-labeled specifically
     for floor-lying use — check the manufacturer's own recommendation.
  2. **Fabric touching the floor** — the most difficult but most
     striking option, needing very precise measurement; recommends a
     dense-weave synthetic or blended fabric specifically for this
     length, since natural-fiber content is vulnerable to
     humidity-driven dimensional change at this precision.
  3. **⚠️ Fabric "floating" ~1cm above the floor** — specifically
     recommended when a motorized track is used: **mechanism explained**
     — a curtain lying on the floor catches/drags at the bottom when a
     motor opens it, so the top closes/opens faster than the bottom;
     floating length avoids this drag. Also recommended for anyone
     wanting perfectly straight, unbroken pleat lines top-to-bottom — a
     floor-touching curtain's pleats crease/break at the point of
     contact.
  4. **Fabric touching the floor plus 1-2cm extra ("broken"/rumpled hem
     effect)** — a deliberate stylistic choice, said to work best with a
     shimmery or richly textured fabric specifically.

## Rules / Heuristics — Color and Pattern Guidance

- **Explicit criteria for when to match curtains to wall color vs. to
  another textile element (sofa/upholstery/rug)**: match wall color when
  curtains shouldn't draw attention (they recede into the background) or
  when the room already has many color accents and doesn't need another;
  match another textile's color when the room is fairly calm/neutral and
  needs a bit more mood/color. **⚠️ Explicit downstream caution**:
  matching curtains to a specific piece of furniture's upholstery color
  creates a dependency — replacing that furniture later will likely force
  replacing the curtains too.
- **Pattern-combination guidance**: when combining a patterned curtain
  with other patterned elements (rug, wall finish, upholstery), use
  different pattern *scales* for each element (e.g. large geometric
  pattern on curtains, small-scale pattern on chair upholstery) while
  keeping the color palette unified — technically possible but genuinely
  hard to combine many prints well; caution against overdoing it.
- **Solid-color-block combination as a pattern alternative**: combine
  solid-color fabric panels within one curtain instead of using a
  printed pattern — put darker colors lower (they read as visually
  heavier) and lighter colors higher; combining sheer/translucent and
  dense fabric panels is another option; a contrast trim/border (piping)
  around the curtain perimeter or run vertically is a simpler way to add
  interest to a plain curtain.
- **⚠️ Caution against bulky/composite patterns on drapes specifically**:
  (1) an oversized pattern draws attention away from more important
  interior details; (2) a pattern can visually distort/interrupt where it
  falls across a pleat fold, since the fold breaks up how the pattern
  reads. If pattern on drapes is wanted anyway, use abstract or calm
  repeating patterns that survive being folded.
