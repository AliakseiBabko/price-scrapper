Hi everyone, in this lesson I want to share my experience of working with Chat GPT Astra Codeex and 3DS Max. This is not a magical ready-made workflow that you can copy and use to get a perfect result immediately. It is an honest practical test. I will show you what already works well, where artificial intelligence really saves time, and where it still creates more work than value. Over the past week, I have spent a great deal of time and tokens on these experiments. That is why I want to show you not only the attractive final result, but the entire process as well. The main feature of this technology is that we can control 3DS Max directly from a chat. I describe a task in ordinary language. Codeex sends the commands to the software and I supervise the result. In theory, you can work almost without touching the mouse. However, this does not mean that the human is no longer needed. In fact, you become the operator and art director of the entire process. The final result depends on your knowledge, your brief, and your ability to identify mistakes. If you imagine that you can simply upload a floor plan in a mood board and receive an accurate finished scene, I have to disappoint you. It does not work like that yet. Mistakes can begin as early as the scaling or wall construction stage. The technology still has enormous potential. When tasks are divided correctly, it can already handle some repetitive work and complex custom modeling. In this lesson, I will show you how to connect the system. I have intentionally kept the unsuccessful moments in this video. You will see long waits, repeated commands, and situations where the system misunderstood the floor plan. This matters because short social media demonstrations usually show only the final few seconds. In a real project, we care about more than whether an object appeared in the scene. Let us begin with the basic setup. First, you need to install the chatpt or codeex application on your computer. In my case, I am working in codeex mode. It can interact with locally installed software, but it needs an MCP server to communicate with three DS Max. You can use the direct MCP from Autodesk and complete the required setup through PowerShell. I am testing a simpler option called Max Ultra MCP. It connects 3ds Max to codeex quickly and already includes a basic set of tools and skills for architectural scenes. I will leave a link to the plug-in below the video. At the time of recording, the main tools are available for free. However, I recommend supporting the developer with a donation because projects like this take time to develop. After installation, open 3DS Max. Launch the plug-in manager and add Max Ultra MCP to the quick access toolbar. Then open it settings, start the server and check the connection status. Once the server is ready and CEX is connected, you can minimize the panel. From this point on, the chat can execute commands directly in the open scene. It is important to understand that MCP is only a bridge between the chat and the software. It does not replace a proper brief and it does not automatically make the model smarter. If the command is vague, the result will also be vague. After connecting the system, the main question is not which button to press, but how to organize the entire process. Before the first major task, check the measurement units, the 3DS Max version, the render engine, the layer names, and the scene save path. In my project, the working units are millime. The render engine is corona, and every new object must be placed on a logically named layer. It is better to define these basic rules immediately than to correct the entire scene manually later. The most important thing I learned during these tests is that you need to communicate with Codeex as clearly as you would with a real specialist. When a task is abstract and chaotic, the system begins filling in the gaps on its own. It invents construction details, chooses a random modeling method, or misinterprets elements on the plan. The more uncertainty you leave, the more time and tokens you will spend on corrections. This is my process. First, I provide a short context. who I am, which software I use, what type of scene I am creating, and what result I expect. Then I add only the source materials needed for that specific stage. Finally, I describe the sequence of actions, technical constraints, and review criteria. Do not upload the plan, mood board, every piece of furniture, all materials, lighting, and decor at once, and ask the system to assemble a finished interior. You will probably get chaos and use your entire limit on corrections. It is far more efficient to work through short complete stages. Start with the walls and the openings for windows and doors. Then create the floor. After that, move on to the windows and doors. Treat skirting boards, moldings, and cornises as a separate stage. Only after the technical shell has been checked should you proceed to cabinetry, upholstered furniture, materials, lighting, and cameras. For every stage, I define what counts as a finished result. I move forward only after that result has been approved. To avoid explaining the same rules in every new project, I started creating separate instructions and skills. I uploaded my own Patreon tutorials and the guides used by my team to chat GPT. From each resource, the chat created a structured instruction. How we start a scene, build walls, and work with floors, windows, doors, skirting boards, moldings, and cornises. If you do not have your own tutorials, you can use the materials you learned from. Share a video link or upload a file and first ask the chat to describe the method step by step instead of performing the task. Then review the instruction as a specialist. Artificial intelligence may misunderstand an important detail. So an unchecked text should never become a permanent rule. My workflow looks like this. We take the instruction for one stage, add the technical brief for the specific project, and complete the modeling. I review the result, request corrections, and approve the work. Only then do I ask the system to analyze the final approved version, and update the skill. Different tasks require different skills. I do not recommend combining everything in one enormous document. In my case, it makes sense to have one skill for scene setup, walls, and the basic floor, another for windows and doors, and a third for skirting boards, moldings, and cornises. When there are too many rules, they can conflict with one another. This happened in one of my tests. The system started using a built-in architectural wall method instead of my interior visualization workflow. Instructions should therefore remain simple, and every skill should be responsible for one clearly defined part of the process. A skill should also be treated as a living working document. If we find a better method during a new project, I do not simply add another paragraph to the old rules. First, I check whether the new recommendation conflicts with the previous one. I remove the outdated version and keep one unambiguous instruction. Otherwise, the model will waste time choosing between two different methods. A short tested skill is almost always more useful than a huge archive of every conversation you have ever had. Now, let us move on to our scene. Before I begin, I prepare three groups of information. The first is the floor plan. The second is a construction brief with exact dimensions and heights. The third is the mood board which defines the atmosphere, materials, furniture, and overall style. During the technical stage, the mood board should not control the entire process. The main architectural dimensions are what matter when building the shell. It is better to introduce the design concept later after the base geometry has been approved. In Miro, you can see the format I use to record dimensions and prepare a short brief. If you only have a raster image, you need to calibrate it using the dimensions shown on the plan. The result is much better when you have a complete drawing package. In my case, however, the drawings are produced from the finished scene. I therefore use either a measured survey plan or the PDF plan supplied by the client. I then create a new project in codeex and add the floor plan, the construction brief, and the instruction for the first stage only. The system now has enough information to begin without unnecessary context that could confuse it. I also prepare the atmosphere brief in advance, but I use it later. We begin by importing the floor plan into 3DS Max. The image must retain its original proportions. In the task, we tell the system to use the real dimensions on the plan as a reference and fit the image to that scale. We will verify the walls and their dimensions afterwards. In my workflow, walls are not created from a collection of random box objects. First, we trace the plan as one plane, extend the geometry with shift, and leave openings for the doors and windows. Then, we raise the walls to the required height with the shell modifier. Lentils, areas below the windows, and other structural elements are added later using clean, editable geometry. This method may appear more complicated than placing separate primitives quickly, but it is far more convenient for interiors. Opening heights, wall sections, and window sills often change in real projects. With clean, logical topology, these corrections can be made quickly without destroying the entire model. Taste, composition, color, and lighting are much harder to convert into exact instructions. Even designers and visualizers need years of practice to learn how to create a strong emotional image. Visualization is not merely a technical illustration for builders. For a studio, it is part of its visual language, branding, and marketing. Beautiful images attract attention, build recognition, and bring in new clients. If a studio sharply reduces image quality for the sake of rapid automation, it risks losing its own style and disappearing from view. If you only need a simple image to communicate an approximate idea to a client, current AI tools are already more than capable. But if a visualization needs to sell your level of work and communicate precise materials, proportions, lighting, and brand character, human expertise remains essential. My main conclusion is that chat GPT Astra is already a powerful automation tool, but it is not an autonomous 3D visualizer. It performs specific tasks well when it receives accurate source data, a clear algorithm, and defined review criteria. If you give it an entire project in one enormous prompt, the result becomes unpredictable. While the time and token costs rise very quickly, a step-by-step approach works best at the moment. One project, one chat, one completed stage, one instruction or skill. First, define the task and review the result. Then check it. Submit a consolidated correction list. Approve the stage and only then continue. Do not build a complex system of agents simply because it sounds technologically impressive. If you can check something with your own eyes in two minutes, there is no reason to spend resources on a separate agent. Automation should simplify your work, not make you watch artificial intelligence argue with itself for hours. I see the greatest value in repetitive operations, batch corrections, and complex custom modeling, accuracy, taste, composition, and the final artistic decision still remain the responsibility of the human artist.
