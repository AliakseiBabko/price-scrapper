Blender's path tracing in Cycles has received a ton of love in the last couple of years, making it faster and more physically accurate than ever. First of all, Cycle introduced a new light tree system to efficiently handle scenes with many lights, reducing noise significantly in multi-light setups. This means cleaner renders when you have a lot of light sources. Cycles also got smarter about pathfinding, initially helping with indirect diffuse light, and by Blender 4.0, it was extended to guide glossy reflections as well. So, in practice, this finds tricky lighting paths and cleans up noise in dark and glossy scenes. Also, interestingly enough, the Principled BSDF shader had a big overhaul. The old shader was rewritten to be more modern, fixing long-standing accuracy bugs, and adding features like a proper sheen layer for cloth and fuzz, in addition to a realistic clear coat and tint controls. In other words, I would say it is more physically correct and flexible than before, making it easier to get realistic materials. We also got a new hair shader model for more realistic hair and fur, by the way. Now, I want to mention a much-requested artistic feature, which landed in Cycles, and it is called light linking and shadow linking, too, not too long ago. This let you control which objects a given light affects. It does not render fast, but it can hugely help in art directing lighting. For example, keeping a rim light only on your character and not on the background, or disabling a shadow on a specific object for a clean look. In addition, Cycles continue to support AI denoisers to clean up noise quickly. Nvidia's OptiX, in particular, even gained support for Open Shading Language, or OSL, on the GPU. Meaning advanced shader setups that used to be CPU only can now run on Nvidia GPUs. There's actually ongoing research into more temporal and stable denoising, like Nvidia's latest DLSS ray reconstruction that was demoed at SIGGRAPH, pointing to even smoother animation previews in the future. When it comes to performance and hardware, under the hood optimizations and broader hardware support actually made Cycles snappier for everyone. Blender devs completed the transition to a new Cycles X architecture, so rendering is much faster than a couple of years ago. On top of that, GPU rendering is truly cross-platform now. Nvidia RTX cards use OptiX with ray cores, and AMD GPUs use HIP, and Intel's Arc GPUs are supported via one API. Even Apple's Metal backend got optimizations, too. So, Macs can see better performance when it comes to Cycles. This is actually a bit of an old news, but you got the point. Now, a small but important change for render output. In Blender 4, Blender devs swapped out the default Filmic view transform for a new AgX color transforms as a default tone mapping. AgX gives a more photorealistic response, I mean, out of the box. This makes renders look punchier and more true to life by default, which artists have welcomed. Overall, Cycles in 2025, I think, is a beast compared to what it was before, but with Blender 5, things are getting even better. Blender 5.0 brings interesting improvements to rendering, both in the realism department and raw performance, which no one ever complained about. On the Cycles side, subsurface scattering just got an accuracy boost. The Principled BSDF's random walk subsurface scattering now supports multiple scattering bounces, which means more realistic skin and translucent materials. So, those murky dark patches will be reduced at the cost of a bit more render time. Another interesting update, the Principled BSDF and metallic shader now have a thin film option for iridescence. This simulates a rainbow sheen that you get on oil slicks or soap bubbles due to tiny film layers. The result is subtle on metals, but can produce gorgeous colored highlights on surfaces, which instantly improve the realism for automotive shaders, VFX, etc. Cycles also changed how it handles volumes internally. Smoke and fire shaders now use NanoVDB under the hood, which dynamically cuts memory usage on volumetrics, which is awesome for heavy simulations. The trade-off is a slight speed hit in some cases, but the developers also introduced a new null scattering volume sampler, which is unbiased and removes the old need to fiddle with step size settings. Essentially, volume rendering is now more physically correct and artifact-free, which is great. So, you shouldn't see those blocky step artifacts, especially in dense smoke. But, here's the [music] thing, if you prefer the old biased method, which is reliable for speed and consistency, especially with older files, there is a toggle to get it back. Another neat option for speed is that hair and curves can be rendered as linear 3D curves, which approximates curves with straight segments. It is less accurate, but can significantly speed up hair renders when perfect smoothness isn't needed. And a small but cool addition for debugging renders, a render time I/O V pass that shows how long each pixel took to render. This is great, I would say, for performance nerds or optimizing heavy scenes. Now, with Eevee, Blender's real-time render engine, it has also undergone a major evolution known as Eevee Next, which happened more than a year ago. By Blender 4.2, the new Eevee Next render became the default, bringing Eevee closer to Cycles, I mean, in quality, while still keeping it super fast. Eevee Next introduced a new screen space ray tracing pipeline for global illumination. In other words, this means Eevee can now calculate indirect light bounces, and it can do it in real time using screen space ray tracing for every material, dynamically improving the accuracy of bounced light and global illumination. This helps scenes lit indirectly, think indoor scenes, soft area lighting, and so on, which helps it kind of look closer to what Cycles can do, at least on the surface, whereas older Eevee had to rely on light probes or got blotchy in some cases. In addition, the new Eevee uses virtual shadow maps for shadows, which greatly reduces shadow flickering and allows much higher resolution shadow detail. The result is stable, crisp shadows with lots of lights. And speaking of lights, the old 128 light limit is gone, which means there is no fixed limit on light count now. So, you can flood your scenes with light, and Eevee can handle it. And lights even show up through reflective materials like glass, because previously, lights behind glass wouldn't contribute. Interestingly enough, Eevee Next added support for effects that were once Cycles only. Motion blur is now partially supported in viewport using a simplified method, so fast-moving objects appear blurred. Depth of field was also optimized to be smoother. These effects make Eevee previews and renders feel more cinematic and closer to offline render quality. But, for final renders, Cycles still has the edge in accuracy. And unlike the old Eevee, which couldn't deform geometry at render time, Eevee Next supports material displacement maps in real time. You can see tessellated bumps and terrain in the viewport now, which is huge for realism. In addition, a new thickness output in materials improves how subsurface scattering, translucency, and refractions are rendered. So, things like skin, wax, or glass look more convincing in Eevee Next compared to what it was before. I also want to mention one big focus, which was temporal stability, meaning less flickering around noise from frame to frame in animations. Eevee Next employs velocity-aware temporal supersampling to reduce noise and aliasing. The net effect is that real-time viewport renders of animations are less prone to jitter and noise popping, which is great. So, overall visual quality is just higher and more consistent, but some users are still reporting some lagging. The thing is, this is changing in Blender 5. In addition to all of this, Eevee settings and UI are refactored to mirror Cycles more closely, making it easier to switch engines without confusion. So, the overhaul was significant enough that all scenes might need mirror tweaks, but it has been worth it. In day-to-day use, Eevee now feels like a light version of Cycles. You got a lot from Cycles looks with instant feedback, which is great for look dev and previews. So, as you can see, Eevee in 2025 and beyond is way more powerful compared to Eevee from a couple of years ago. But, the good news is that in Blender 5, the developers finalized the transition to the new Eevee, replacing the Eevee Next preview from 4.x, and it now fully runs on the Vulkan backend. So, as you can see, Eevee now supports HDR and wide gamut displays on Windows and Linux when using Vulkan. So, if you have an HDR monitor, you can get proper high dynamic range viewport feedback, which is huge, especially for color accurate work. The viewport matcaps got an overhaul, too. Blender added new matcaps and updated others to include a specular light layer. This means when you use matcap shading, you will see a nice specular highlight on objects, making the shape of materials read better, especially on dark surfaces. Eevee's lighting got a tiny fix for hair curves, too, which is an internal shadow bias to reduce self-shadowing glitches on hair strands. I also want to mention one more advanced viewport feature, which is view layer overrides, because it is now supported in Eevee. This means you can override materials, samples per view layer, similar to Cycles, which is super useful for compositing different passes, like clay render override, etc. All these improvements make Eevee more capable, especially for look dev and even final rendering. And of course, Cycles and Eevee both drop support for really old GPUs. Blender 5 now needs at least a GPU with CUDA compute 5.0, roughly a GeForce 900 series or newer for GPU rendering. On a side note, Blender 5 has ended support for Intel Macs, focusing on Apple silicon going forward. So, Mac users, you will need to be on an M1 or an M2 chip for this version and future versions.
