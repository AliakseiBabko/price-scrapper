## Terminology and Taxonomy (genuinely new, fills an existing thin-stub gap)

- **"Breather" (бризер) origin**: coined by the company Tion as a product name for their first device (Tion O2, 2013) — a brand name that became genericized into the product-category name, comparable to how "Xerox" became a generic term for photocopying. **A breather is a subset of the broader "supply complex" (приточный комплекс) category** — every breather is a supply complex, but not every supply complex is marketed as a "breather" (e.g. this source's own ATMEX product line uses the "supply-purification complex" framing instead). [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **⚠️ Full ventilation taxonomy, ascending sophistication, extending the existing thin stub with concrete detail for each rung**: [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
  1. **Open window** — baseline, no filtration, no control.
  2. **In-profile trickle vent** (a small vent built into the window profile itself) — has only a basic foam/sponge filter that barely stops dust (visibly passes through onto a white profile over time); no heating; often gets taped shut by occupants because it drafts. Functions mainly as a developer's paper-compliance checkbox for "ventilation provided," not a real solution.
  3. **Wall-mounted trickle vent/valve** (100-130mm insulated bore through the wall with an adjustable cord/lever damper) — same core flaw as the profile vent: **doesn't work at all if the apartment's exhaust ventilation is inadequate or absent** (no pressure differential to pull air through), same no-heating limitation, and a genuinely new mechanism: **⚠️ a poorly-designed unit lets cold air enter and drop straight to floor level, creating a cold-draft-on-the-feet effect; a well-designed one routes incoming air upward first so it has time to warm in transit before reaching the room** — a concrete design-quality checkpoint when evaluating this device type.
  4. **Compact heat-recovery unit (рекуператор)** — cyclic "inhale-exhale" operation: draws room air out for roughly a minute (a heat-storage element inside absorbs its warmth), then reverses to draw outside air in for roughly a minute (the same element releases stored heat into the incoming air) — the source's own analogy: like breathing through a scarf in cold weather. **This duty cycle halves the unit's effective average throughput** compared to continuous one-directional airflow. Needs one wall penetration (modern compact models) or two (older/imported models). Popular in Europe for energy efficiency, but **redundant in an apartment that already has working separate exhaust ventilation** — you only need supply capacity, not a second exhaust cycle duplicating what the building's exhaust shaft already does. No compact device on the market does simultaneous (non-cyclic) bidirectional heat recovery — that requires a large cross-flow plate heat exchanger only feasible at central-system scale.
  5. **Breathers / supply complexes** — single wall penetration, continuous one-directional forced supply (no duty-cycle penalty). Sub-split by tier: **base tier** — physical buttons on the unit body (sometimes positioned so they must be found by feel once wall-mounted), coarse filtration only, generally noisier due to simpler construction; **premium tier** — CO2-sensor-driven automatic control, app/smart-home/voice-assistant integration, quieter, multi-stage filtration.
  6. **Central ducted supply+exhaust system** — one or two air-handling units (closet/loggia/dropped-ceiling-mounted) feeding ductwork to every room, with heat-recovery, electric, or hydronic heating; described as the unambiguous top performer on every metric except price and disruption (requires a dropped/boxed ceiling everywhere, typically only realistic with >2.7m ceiling height and gypsum-ceiling budget already planned).

## CO2 Physiology and Cognitive Impact (genuinely new)

- **⚠️ CO2 buildup impairs cognition primarily by blocking oxygen transfer from blood to tissue (including the brain), not by depleting room oxygen itself** — even heavy breathing doesn't meaningfully lower a room's oxygen level, but elevated CO2 specifically suppresses the body's respiratory drive above a threshold and interferes with oxygen delivery to tissue, producing a real, physiologically-caused fatigue/brain-fog effect (source's framing: "brain hypoxia"). [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **⚠️ Specific cited cognitive-decline thresholds, attributed to an unnamed American study** (seating subjects in a room, running intellectual tasks while varying CO2 level): 550 ppm — no measurable effect; **1,000+ ppm — 15% cognitive-function decline; 1,400+ ppm — 50% cognitive-function decline.** `single-account`, citing a study not independently named/verified in this source — treat the study's existence and exact figures as `unverified` pending a primary citation, but record the claim since it's specific and checkable in principle. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **Common misconception debunked: airing a bedroom for ~15 minutes before bed ("as mother taught") is not adequate** — CO2 rebuilds to an unsafe level within roughly an hour of closing the window again, well before a typical night's sleep is over. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **A CO2 sensor is framed as a strong general proxy for overall air quality, not just a CO2-specific reading** — elevated CO2 correlates with reduced air exchange generally, which also implies worse humidity control and more accumulated dust/particulates, since all three degrade together when air exchange stalls. ## Why an Open Window Often Doesn't Work (genuinely new mechanism)

**⚠️ "Bottle-cap" mechanism: without forced supply and with weak/absent exhaust ventilation, opening a window may create zero real airflow**, even standing directly next to the open window — the source's own analogy: like a capped bottle with a small hole poked in it, water won't drip out with no venting for air to enter behind it. Multiple real client cases cited where a client opens a window fully and feels literally nothing, purely because the building's exhaust shaft isn't pulling. This directly explains why supply ventilation and exhaust ventilation are coupled problems, not independent ones. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]

## Air Conditioner Misconception (genuinely new, corrects a common belief)

**⚠️ An air conditioner does not introduce any outdoor fresh air — it only recirculates and thermally conditions air already inside the room.** A real client case: a customer believed her 2-year-old AC unit was "ventilating" the apartment, when what had actually changed was that a previously-leaky window seal (which had been quietly admitting outside air) got fixed — the AC was simply recirculating room air faster, which felt similar but provided none of a real ventilation system's air-exchange benefit. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]

## Filtration Stage Taxonomy (genuinely new)

- **Three-stage filtration in a quality breather/supply complex**: (1) coarse pre-filter (fine metal mesh or washable open-cell foam) catching insects and large debris — needs washing roughly every 3 months; **⚠️ real-world compliance caution**: customers frequently skip this because accessing it requires opening a cover and undoing 4 screws — enough friction that many simply don't do it (the source's own company is redesigning toward a 2-screw mechanism specifically to reduce this abandonment). (2) HEPA-class filter — **H11 or H13 recommended for residential air purification** (H11 alone is described as more than sufficient for pollen, given pollen's relatively large particle size, and also catches PM2.5). (3) Odor-control stage — either an activated-carbon filter (needs periodic replacement) or a photocatalytic filter (decomposes odor molecules rather than adsorbing them — needs no replacement, rated ~5-year service life before gradual performance decline). [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **PM2.5 explained**: particulate matter ≤2.5 micrometers in diameter — small enough to cross the lung's own filtering barriers into the bloodstream directly, from which it's linked (per the source, framed as an emerging global public-health concern, not a settled/cited medical claim) to neurological effects, cancer formation, and cardiac effects. Only HEPA-class filtration (not a basic foam pre-filter) captures this size class. `single-account`, `unverified` as a medical claim — record the mechanism, treat the health-outcome linkage skeptically pending independent medical sourcing. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **~70% of household dust reduction claimed from a properly-filtered breather** — the source's own framing: roughly 30% of household dust is generated internally (furniture off-gassing/shedding, skin/pet dander) and doesn't come through the ventilation path at all; the remaining ~70% enters from outside (door gaps, windows, etc.) and is what a real filtration stage actually intercepts. Effect described as especially noticeable near busy roads or industrial areas. `single-account`, `unverified` precise percentage, but the underlying internal-vs-external dust-source split is a specific, checkable framing. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]

## Humidity Mechanism and Sizing (genuinely new)

- **⚠️ Winter outdoor air has very low *absolute* humidity (actual water content) regardless of a weather forecast's stated 70-80% *relative* humidity figure** — relative humidity depends on air temperature (cold air holds far less total moisture at the same relative-humidity percentage), so winter air brought indoors and warmed becomes genuinely very dry. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **A breather/supply-ventilation system dries indoor air far more aggressively than a window ever did, purely due to volume** — a small stand-alone humidifier can keep pace with a window's modest natural air exchange, but not with a breather's much higher forced throughput; anyone adding forced supply ventilation should expect to need to upgrade humidification capacity to match. **Sizing guidance: choose a humidifier rated for ≥300 ml/hour output when paired with active ventilation** — reasoning given: roughly 3 liters of water needs replenishing per 10 hours of ventilated occupancy in winter, and in winter the humidifier should effectively run continuously. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **Humidity is framed as more health-relevant than temperature itself, alongside CO2 and particulate cleanliness** — dry air/mucous-membrane dehydration increases susceptibility to airborne viral/bacterial infection (a stated mechanism, `unverified` as medical fact), and skin/hair health is also tied to humidity. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **For a whole private house, a centralized nozzle/misting ("форсуночная") humidification system is recommended over per-room standalone humidifiers**, but flagged as expensive (source's own rough estimate mentioned elsewhere in the conversation: roughly 1-1.5 million RUB for a ~30m² reference scenario, `unverified`, imprecise/anecdotal) — **must be planned during the design phase, not retrofitted**, since it's a real building system requiring its own design project. For an apartment (1-2 rooms), an ordinary ultrasonic humidifier or an "air washer" is described as sufficient, refilled daily or every 2 days. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]

## Noise, Airflow Sizing, and Buying Practicalities (genuinely new)

- **⚠️ Airflow sizing benchmark, cited to code**: **30 m³/h of supply air per person**, attributed to ГОСТ/СНиП (Russian building-code standards) — for two people, the source recommends sizing with margin at **65-70 m³/h** rather than exactly 60. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **Even a mid-range-or-better breather's lowest or second speed setting already exceeds the airflow from a typical 15-minute window-airing session** — reassurance against the common worry that a "quiet" low-speed setting is functionally useless; the trade-off exists but the low end still clears the bar a brief window-airing doesn't. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **⚠️ Continuous/steady noise (like a running breather) is subjectively far less sleep-disruptive than intermittent noise at a comparable decibel level (passing traffic, a motorcycle)** — the human ear/attention habituates quickly to a constant tone but keeps re-triggering on each new irregular sound. The source explicitly caveats this is a personal/anecdotal framing, not something he has studied the underlying research on himself — treat as plausible but `unverified`. **Practical buying tip**: compare a candidate breather's noise rating against a device you already own and tolerate (e.g. your own AC unit) to judge fit, or — the source's own company's practice — request a **free in-home demo visit**, where an engineer brings a unit to the actual room, mounts/holds it in place, and lets the buyer listen with the lights off from the actual sleeping position before committing to a purchase. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **Myth-check: summer is often framed as "you don't need a breather, just open the window" — described as backwards.** Natural stack-effect draft through a building's ventilation shaft is driven by the indoor-outdoor temperature differential, which is large in winter (good natural draft) and very weak in summer — so a breather (or any forced supply) is often *more* necessary in summer than winter, the opposite of common intuition, though dust still enters regardless of season. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **A breather is also valuable specifically during shoulder seasons (autumn/spring)** when a building's central heating isn't switched on yet but outdoor temperatures already require it — lets an occupant keep windows closed and ventilate on-demand with heated air rather than being stuck choosing between cold air and stale air. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]

## Installation Practicalities and Common Installer Mistakes (genuinely new)

- **Installation timing flexibility**: unlike a central ducted system (which must be planned during design), a breather/supply complex can be installed at *any* point in a renovation or even after moving in — only requirement is a nearby power source (an outlet, or better, a hard-wired feed run to the unit itself during finishing so no visible cord remains). Planning ahead (having an engineer visit during the design phase to mark power-routing needs) is still recommended even though it's not mandatory. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **Typical installation**: ~2 hours, minimal mess despite the large bore — **132mm diameter**, cut via wet diamond core drilling (water-cooled), with the water/debris fully contained by dedicated vacuum equipment rather than splashing/dripping. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **⚠️ Common DIY/unlicensed-installer mistakes, per the source's own field experience**: skipping duct-sleeve insulation (causing condensation to form around the bore); boring the interior and exterior openings non-coaxially/misaligned (partially obstructing the duct's effective cross-section once the unit is installed, silently reducing airflow); installing too close to a wall/corner (clearance violation); damaging exterior cladding/tile during the exterior bore, producing an ugly, crooked-looking exterior grille (and real risk of a neighbor/HOA complaint over a damaged facade); miswiring the electrical feed to the wrong point, requiring re-chasing already-painted walls. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
- **⚠️ Genuinely new technique — the exterior bore should be angled with a slight downward slope toward the outside (a couple of degrees), not drilled level.** The interior and exterior openings sit at slightly different heights specifically so that any snow/rain that enters the sleeve during a storm drains back out rather than pooling inside the wall cavity. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_uiiggEC7c9M_kruglov_breathers_podcast_goncharov|uiiggEC7c9M]]]
