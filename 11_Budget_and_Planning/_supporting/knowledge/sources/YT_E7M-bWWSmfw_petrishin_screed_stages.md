## Flooring — Semi-Mechanized ("Полу-механизированная"/"Полуручка") Semi-Dry Screed

A distinct screed-delivery method from the fully mechanized pumped
screed and the DIY hand-mixed reinforced screed already documented on
`Flooring_Guide.md` — genuinely new to this store, not corroborating
either existing method:

1. **Mechanism**: mix is prepared in a mobile pump unit's small onboard
   mixer (sand + cement, or a premixed cement-sand bag product called
   "пескобетон"), then the *same pump* delivers the mixed slurry over
   distance/height through hoses — same delivery hardware as a fully
   mechanized pour, but the mixing step is smaller-batch and slower
   than a fully mechanized rig's continuous large-volume mixing,
   hence "half-mechanized."
2. **Four named use-cases for choosing this method over a full
   mechanized pour**: (a) the apartment is above the 25th floor and
   the pump can't reach that height; (b) the building's management
   company forbids unloading bulk sand/cement deliveries needed for a
   full mechanized pour; (c) the site/parking doesn't allow the truck
   needed to bring in bulk sand; (d) ambient temperature below +7°C —
   at low temperature, mix prepared and pumped the fully mechanized way
   can **freeze inside the delivery hoses in transit**, making the pour
   impossible; this job was done at **-14°C outside**, which is also
   why pescobeton (bagged premix) was used instead of separately-batched
   sand and cement — loose sand would freeze in these conditions,
   forcing the premix choice even though it costs somewhat more.
   A fifth practical driver, not really a "use case" but a stated
   reason some crews choose this method by default: **a full
   mechanized pump rig costs roughly 3,000,000 RUB (≈$39,700, trailing
   6-month USD/RUB average ending 2021-02-14)** — not every crew can
   afford to own one.
3. **Two concrete downsides, both stated directly**: (a) roughly **2x
   slower** than a fully mechanized pour; (b) **somewhat higher
   material/labor cost** when pescobeton premix is used instead of
   separately-priced loose sand + cement (the cheaper option, but
   unusable below freezing since the sand itself freezes); pescobeton
   also produces **more airborne dust** than loose sand+cement during
   mixing.
4. **This job's real quantities**: 70 m² floor area, ~7cm screed
   thickness, 245 bags of pescobeton consumed for the pour.

## Flooring — Screed Curing/Care Routine, with Concrete Timing and Water Quantities

5. **Deformation joints between rooms**: installed room-by-room (not
   as one continuous slab) specifically to prevent cracking as the
   screed cures — a room-scale application of the deformation-joint
   principle, distinct from this store's existing perimeter-strip
   detail (Sidorik ep.18) which addresses the screed's edge, not
   inter-room division.
6. **Watering/covering cycle with concrete numbers**: cover the screed
   with plastic film the day after pouring (traps moisture so the
   screed doesn't dry/cure too fast, which risks cracking or
   "бухтение"/hollow-sounding delamination); after 3-4 days, remove the
   film, wet the entire surface with water (**≈1-1.5 L of water per
   m²**), and re-cover; repeat this water-and-recover cycle every 3-4
   days; **film can be fully removed after 14 days**. **First 10 days**:
   avoid significant loads on the screed — no heavy material staging,
   no ladders/stepladders standing on it — since this is when the
   screed gains most of its final strength.

Both curing-routine facts (5, 6) are stated as applying to the
semi-mechanized method shown here and, per the speaker, the fully
mechanized method as well — general screed-curing technique, not
specific to this delivery method, so it's a reasonable candidate to
apply broadly regardless of pour method chosen.
