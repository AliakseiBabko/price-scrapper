## Rules / Heuristics — What a Pantry Is For, and Why It Gets Omitted

Pantries store: cleaning supplies/chemicals, luggage/bags/suitcases,
professional construction/repair tools, small household appliances
(vacuum cleaner, irons, garment steamers), and even a stepladder.

**⚠️ A named, direct explanation for a common real mistake**: the
architect states he "constantly" encounters private-house floor plans
(including from professional architects) with **no pantry at all**, and
offers his own explanation: post-Soviet clients and (often) their
architects grew up in apartments, where pantry space genuinely doesn't
exist as a category, so apartment-derived planning habits get carried
over into private-house design even though a house has the space to
accommodate one properly. A house's own available space is being wasted
by unconsciously importing an apartment-scale planning assumption. (Note:
this project's own household plan is itself apartment-based — this
mistake pattern doesn't directly apply, but the underlying pantry
*sizing/planning* content below still generalizes.)

## Quantities / Measurements — Minimum Pantry Area (Cited Reference Source)

**⚠️ A cited external reference, not just the architect's own
convention**: "the book" — Ernst Neufert's *Architects' Data* (Нойферт),
a standard architectural reference text — recommends allocating **at
least 2% of a house's useful/livable area to pantry space** for a
comfortable house, where "useful area" explicitly **excludes** corridors,
halls, and stairs. Worked example given: a 100m² useful-area house should
have at least 2m² of total pantry space. **This can be distributed
across several smaller pantries** rather than concentrated in one room —
e.g. one small pantry of roughly 1m×2m holding all the listed items is
explicitly given as a workable single-pantry solution.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Don't Substitute an Attic/Mezzanine/Basement for a Pantry

**⚠️ A specific, named anticipated objection, directly addressed**: the
architect preemptively rebuts viewers who plan to rely on a large attic
for this kind of storage instead of a dedicated pantry — attics,
mezzanines, and basements have **fundamentally different usage/access
conditions** than a pantry. Concrete reasoning: an item like an iron
needs to be accessible "almost every day" — you would not climb to an
attic for it. A pantry's defining function is *frequent, low-friction
access*, not just storage volume; storage location should match access
frequency, not just be wherever there's spare volume.

## Rules / Heuristics — The "Leftover-Space" Design Method for Fitting a Pantry In

**⚠️ A concrete, repeatable design technique, new to this store**:
pantries in most real floor plans get created by the "leftover-space"
principle — slightly shrinking and/or shifting an adjacent room (worked
example: an oversized bathroom serving two bedrooms) to carve out a
pantry-sized sliver, with its own entry from a hallway or an adjacent
bedroom. A reusable technique for finding pantry space in an already-
drawn floor plan without adding new square footage, rather than needing
to plan for it from a blank slate.

## Rules / Heuristics — Pantry Ventilation

Ventilation for a general pantry is **desirable but not mandatory**
("желательно," explicitly not "обязательно") — the same rule is stated
to apply to walk-in closets/dressing rooms too. Mechanism options:

- **Passive supply**: the gap under an interior door is usually
  sufficient supply air on its own; if that gap is too narrow, add either
  a bottom door vent cutout or a decorative door-vent grille.
- **Exhaust, two options**: (a) a **dedicated vent duct routed to the
  roof**, or (b) a **compromise vent grille opening into the hallway**
  (shared with the house's general ventilation/air circulation).
- **⚠️ A concrete caution against defaulting to a dedicated vent duct,
  new to this store**: up to roughly **one-third of a house's total heat
  loss can occur through natural ventilation pathways**, especially in
  **central and northern Russia's climate**; a dedicated vent duct for
  "such a modest, leftover-space room" is called out as often not worth
  that heat-loss cost. Southern-Russia climates are more forgiving of
  adding a dedicated duct. `single-account`, no external code citation,
  but a concrete, checkable tradeoff reasoning.

## Rules / Heuristics — Kitchen-Adjacent Pantry: Why It's Now Standard

**Context shift noted directly by the source**: a kitchen-adjacent
pantry was considered a "novelty" roughly 5-6 years before this video was
filmed (i.e. mid-2010s), but is now considered standard/expected —
attributed to private-house dwellers gaining more day-to-day autonomy
from urban infrastructure over that period. **Underlying driver
(house-specific, but explains the "why")**: rural/detached houses are far
from grocery stores (car trips required), and private-house occupants are
disproportionately larger families — both push toward bulk food storage
capacity a standard kitchen cabinet run doesn't provide.

**⚠️ Two named functions of a kitchen-adjacent pantry, the core reusable
framework of this section**:

1. **"Unloads" the kitchen** — a kitchen-adjacent pantry functions
   effectively as **a second kitchen for storage purposes**, at a much
   smaller footprint. **A concrete worked comparison**: folding a 5m-long
   kitchen cabinetry run down to roughly 1/2 to 1/3 its length in a
   dedicated pantry yields comparable storage capacity in far less area
   than the equivalent kitchen-cabinetry-plus-circulation-zone footprint
   would cost.
2. **Improves kitchen-zone layout flexibility**, especially when built as
   a **pass-through pantry** ("проходная кладовая") — creates an
   alternate access route into the kitchen. **Worked example**: arriving
   home with many grocery bags, a pass-through pantry positioned near the
   entry lets groceries be sorted and moved straight into the kitchen
   without a full "victory lap" through the house in shoes and outerwear.

## Rules / Heuristics — Three Planning Types of Kitchen-Adjacent Pantry

1. **"Pantry as part of the kitchen furniture run"**: positioned adjacent
   to (not necessarily flush with) the kitchen cabinetry — extends
   storage capacity and visually screens off the kitchen corner from an
   adjoining space. Can include a **freezer/chest-freezer unit** for
   longer-term frozen storage (worked example: berries/mushrooms for
   winter), not just dry/shelf-stable goods. **⚠️ Microclimate rule**:
   this type of pantry's interior should be kept **somewhat cooler than
   the rest of the house**. Ventilation recommendation is stronger here
   than the general pantry default — **explicitly "should" have
   ventilation** (not just "desirable"), scaling up with room size and
   the specific foods stored (a larger pantry needs ventilation more, not
   less).
2. **"Pantry as a planning/zoning tool"**: used not just for storage but
   as an architect's own zoning instrument — a pantry's own wall/volume
   can be positioned between the kitchen and an adjoining living/dining
   space specifically to create a physical/visual division between them,
   with its own entry accessible from either side. A genuinely reusable
   planning technique — treating a storage room's footprint as a
   functional divider, not just found-space filler.
3. **"Pass-through pantry"** (see the two-function framework above) — the
   fullest expression of the layout-flexibility function: positioned
   directly off the entry so groceries can be routed straight to the
   kitchen, or bypassed toward a wardrobe/guest WC and onward into the
   living/bedroom zones, without unnecessary indoor travel while still in
   outdoor shoes/clothing.

## Rules / Heuristics — Storage Combinatorics (⚠️ Direct Corroboration of Existing Walk-In-Closet Taxonomy)

**⚠️⚠️ Directly corroborates, from an independent topic angle, this
vault's existing four-way closet-configuration taxonomy** already on
`14_Furniture/analysis/Walkin_Closet_Configurations.md` (single-sided,
double-sided, three-sided/L-shaped, four-sided/U-shaped, sourced there
from clothing-closet content) — this source independently derives the
**same four-way geometric taxonomy for general pantry shelving**, framed
explicitly as "pure geometry" giving only four possible configurations
regardless of what's being stored:

1. **One-sided storage** (shelving along one wall only).
2. **Two-sided storage** (shelving on two opposing/adjacent walls).
3. **L-shaped ("Г-образное") storage.**
4. **U-shaped ("П-образная") storage** — reserved for larger pantries,
   since it requires at least **90cm clearance** between opposing shelf
   runs for an adult to turn around and retrieve/replace items, with
   **100cm (1m) recommended** as more comfortable than the bare 90cm
   minimum.

**Shelf-depth guidance, new figures for this store**: **minimum 25cm**
shelf depth (fits smaller items only); **30-40cm** is the better working
range for general pantry use; **40cm+** starts crossing into
heavy-duty "стеллаж" (industrial/warehouse-style shelving) territory,
more appropriate for a garage or workshop storing large tools/tool sets
than a household pantry.
