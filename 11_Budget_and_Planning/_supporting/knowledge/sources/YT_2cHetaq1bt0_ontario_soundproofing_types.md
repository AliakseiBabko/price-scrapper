## Materials / Planning Rules — Noise Taxonomy

- **Three distinct noise types, each requiring a different countermeasure**: airborne noise (voice/sound traveling through air — e.g. a crying child heard from an adjacent room); impact noise (an object dropped on the floor, heard by the downstairs neighbor); and structural/percussive noise (e.g. hammering directly on a wall), which travels through the building structure itself and can be heard not just by the immediate neighbor but by neighbors on floors further below. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]
- **Effective soundproofing requires both a sound-insulating material and a sound-absorbing material in contact with the sound path** — the two material categories serve distinct functions and neither alone is described as sufficient for real effectiveness. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]

## Materials / Planning Rules — Soundproofing Type Taxonomy

Four distinct soundproofing system types: [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]

1. **Rolled type** — mats/membranes, rolled out directly against the surface; thin, cheapest, fastest to install, but requires a rigid finish layer over it (drywall, GVL) since it can't be plastered/painted directly.
2. **Frame-based (каркасная)** — drywall-profile framework filled with insulation (typically mineral wool); can be combined with the rolled type underneath. **⚠️ Profiles must mount on vibration-damping hangers (виброподвесы)** specifically to stop structural/percussive noise transfer through the wall — the mechanism the source calls out as the actual purpose of vibro-hangers, distinct from just holding the frame in place. Seams sealed with acoustic sealant.
3. **Acoustic panels** — a distinct dedicated product category (not detailed further in this video beyond naming it).
4. **Sandwich panels** (e.g. Zips-brand) — a factory-laminated panel combining a sound-insulating core layer with a rigid GVL face layer, producing a finished, load-bearing-capable wall surface (can hang shelves/frames) in one install step, without a separate drywall finish layer needed on top.

## Materials / Planning Rules — Decision Framework (4 factors)

**⚠️ Before choosing a soundproofing type/thickness for any given wall/floor/ceiling, decide four things in order**: (1) whether this surface actually needs soundproofing at all; (2) how much depth/space budget you're willing to give up to it; (3) what will happen to the surface afterward — will anything be hung on it, does it need to bear a load (cabinets, a range hood), and is the space on the far side even inhabited; (4) how much money you're willing to spend. The source explicitly frames "measure these four factors together" as the actual decision method, not a fixed universal spec. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]

- **⚠️ No reliable way exists to calculate exactly how much soundproofing is "enough" for a specific apartment in practice** — manufacturers publish decibel-reduction ratings for their materials, but there's no practical way to measure how much noise is actually penetrating a given wall beforehand and back-calculate a required spec from it. **Neither the material manufacturer nor the installer will guarantee any specific noise-reduction outcome or percentage.** `single-account`, but a specific, checkable claim about the limits of this trade. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]

## Case Study — Worked One-Bedroom New-Build Per-Wall Walkthrough

A visualized one-bedroom new-build apartment with four distinct wall conditions, each reasoned through the 4-factor framework above: [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]

- **Bedroom wall shared with a neighbor, nothing to be hung on it**: free choice of any soundproofing type since there's no load-bearing requirement. If using the rolled type, it must still be covered with a rigid finish layer (drywall/GVL) since plaster/paint can't go directly on a rolled membrane.
- **Facade (exterior) wall, borders nothing**: **skip soundproofing on this wall — street noise is virtually always actually a window problem, not a wall problem**, so address it via window quality/sealing instead of wall treatment. A specific, actionable cost-avoidance rule.
- **Kitchen wall shared with a neighbor, but load-bearing cabinets/range hood will mount to it**: frame-based soundproofing required (for the load capacity), but a plain drywall-and-insulation frame isn't enough — **add a plywood backing layer (or plywood blocking inserts at the specific mounting points)** before the drywall skin, specifically so heavy cabinets/appliances have something solid to anchor into.
- **Wall bordering an elevator shaft** (source notes this location is typically a hallway or bathroom wall): **decide based on what's on the far side of the wall — if it's a closet/wardrobe (not a lived-in space), a thin membrane alone may be sufficient**, saving both space and cost versus full treatment; a fully lived-in room on the other side would call for stronger treatment.

## Rooms / Zones — Floor vs. Ceiling Soundproofing (asymmetric cost/practice)

- **Floor soundproofing protects the neighbor below from you, not the reverse** — it addresses impact noise specifically (dropped objects, footsteps, activity noise), and in **~99% of cases uses the same rolled-type material**: named products **Shumanet-100** or the waterproofing-capable **Shumanet Hydro-100** variant. **⚠️ The material must be turned up the walls at the perimeter so the poured screed above it never directly contacts the walls** — a physical break preventing vibration/impact noise from transferring from floor to walls (or vice versa). Installation is fast, cheap, and effective — described as now a near-universal step in every renovation, and one some property management companies actively require in new builds. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]
- **⚠️ Ceiling soundproofing is dramatically more expensive and harder than floor soundproofing for the equivalent effect — a real cost-driver worth planning around.** The source estimates ceiling treatment costs roughly **400-500% more than floor treatment** for comparable soundproofing benefit (mostly a labor-cost driver, not material — a rolled floor product is simply rolled out, while real ceiling soundproofing needs a full frame-based build-up to be effective). Because of this asymmetry, **a common real-world workaround is negotiating with the upstairs neighbor to do floor soundproofing on their side instead** — cases exist where a downstairs owner offers to pay for the material and/or have their own crew install it for free on the upstairs neighbor's floor, since it's cheaper and more effective than trying to soundproof one's own ceiling. `single-account`, `unverified` cost-multiple figure, but a specific, named, checkable industry practice. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_2cHetaq1bt0_ontario_soundproofing_types|2cHetaq1bt0]]]
- **This company's own practice: ~95% of their projects use stretch ceilings, and the typical real-world combination is a rolled soundproofing layer rolled directly onto the structural ceiling, then covered by the stretch-ceiling membrane** — a cheap, fast, low-effort combination the source presents as adequate for most clients in practice (paired with the neighbor-does-their-floor pattern above), rather than a full frame-based ceiling soundproofing build. Self-reported business-practice figure — tag as a channel-specific practice pattern, not a universal recommendation.
