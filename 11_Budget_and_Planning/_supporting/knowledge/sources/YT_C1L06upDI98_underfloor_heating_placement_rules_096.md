## HVAC / Flooring — Where Underfloor Heating Is Needed: 3 Rules

- **Rule 1 — replace, don't supplement, a standard heat source wherever legally/technically possible**: if a room's radiator can be replaced by underfloor heating of the same "type" (see the companion source's water/electric/film framing), do so — practical effect: this only applies to a private house with individual heating (where hydronic underfloor heating is legal and preferable to hydronic radiators), since a standard apartment cannot legally substitute water underfloor heating for its central-heating radiators (see the companion source's legal-status section). In a house with individual heating, underfloor heating is stated as strictly better than radiators of the same type on every parameter: better energy efficiency for a given heated area, more even air temperature distribution across the room (a radiator only heats air near itself), better room aesthetics/layout flexibility with no protruding fixture, easier cleaning, and — the source's central comfort argument — floor-level heating lets the room reach a comfortable feel at a meaningfully lower measured air temperature and lower humidity-drying effect than radiator heating, since radiators overheat the air near themselves while leaving the floor cold, forcing occupants to over-heat the whole room (and often still wear socks/slippers) to compensate for a cold floor.
- **Rule 2 — any tile-covered floor area should be heated, for two independent reasons**: (1) **thermal-conductivity comfort mechanism, explained with a worked contrast**: cork (low conductivity) feels warm underfoot even though it's room-temperature, while tile/metal (high conductivity) feels cool at the identical room temperature — because a high-conductivity material draws heat from the body faster than the body can replace it, producing the "cold" sensation independent of actual temperature; sustained contact with an unheated high-conductivity floor (worked comparison: lying on room-temperature concrete for 30 minutes) causes real discomfort and, extended further, a genuine health risk (chill/illness), not just a subjective preference. (2) **moisture-drying mechanism**: tile is chosen in wet-prone rooms (entryway with wet shoes, bathroom with splashed water) specifically because it tolerates standing water — but that water still needs to dry afterward, and underfloor heating is the most effective way to dry the room and prevent mold, not just a comfort feature.
- **Rule 3 — any insulated (joined) loggia must have underfloor heating, no exception**: the floor is stated as the coldest surface in any room, and an insulated loggia's floor stays cold regardless of any other heat source added to the space (a space heater, a relocated radiator) — even if the loggia below/adjacent has its own floor heating, that doesn't transfer upward, because the neighboring unit's own floor insulation blocks heat transfer through their ceiling into your loggia's slab. A loggia without floor heating remains too cold to stand on comfortably even in wool socks, regardless of every other heating measure taken.
- **Explicit cost-of-deferral argument (the video's own closing point, not just its content)**: retrofitting floor heating after a renovation is complete (breaking out finished tile/flooring to add heating later) costs meaningfully more than including it in the original design-project scope — used as the source's own illustration of why hundreds of small planning details compound in cost when deferred, not just floor heating specifically.
