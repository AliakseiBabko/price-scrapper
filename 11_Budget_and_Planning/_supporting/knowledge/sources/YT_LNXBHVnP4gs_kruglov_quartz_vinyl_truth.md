## Genuinely New Content

1. **Two-camp taxonomy, first structural framing for this store**: **Camp 1** — LVT (glue-down only, one member). **Camp 2** — SPC, click-lock LVT, and the rigid multilayer "регит/ABA" type — all floating, all lock-jointed, but with **no universal cross-manufacturer standard** for any of camp 2's members; each manufacturer sets its own install-area limits, thickness minimums, and heated-floor rules independently.
2. **⚠️ Heated-floor compatibility, per-subtype ranking (extends this page's existing heated-floor caution with per-material detail)**: **Glue LVT** — the best pairing of any quartz-vinyl type (no locks to separate at all, since nothing floats); one real constraint: don't exceed **27°C** surface temperature, cited as a health consideration, not a material-failure one. **SPC** — the worst pairing: real, significant linear expansion (directly contradicting the "quartz-vinyl doesn't expand" marketing claim); uneven room heating (e.g. heated only under walking paths, not under furniture) risks lock deformation. Some manufacturers forbid heated floors under SPC outright; those that allow it require the heating element **embedded in the screed** (not just under the floating floor) and the same 27°C ceiling — and if a lock later fails, the manufacturer may deny warranty on the basis these conditions weren't followed. **Click-LVT** — more resistant to warming through than SPC (implying more insulating), combinable with a heated floor under the same embedded-in-screed/≤27°C rules, with less linear expansion than SPC thanks to greater lock/base elasticity. **Rigid multilayer ("регит/ABA")** — technically heated-floor-compatible but functionally pointless: it's the thickest of the four types and very slow to warm through, while already being the warmest-feeling underfoot unheated — little practical benefit from adding a heated floor under it. Also intolerant of partial-room heating for the same reason as SPC.
3. **⚠️ Continuous large-area/no-threshold installation capability, per-subtype ranking, extends this page's existing seamless-run figures**: **Glue LVT** — best of all (no locks at all; load is distributed per-plank, so continuous area is effectively unlimited — the source's own illustrative example: "even 10,000 m², doesn't matter"). **SPC** — worst: most manufacturers cap continuous runs at roughly 10-12m² or an equivalent length limit (brand-dependent) — comparable to laminate's own per-room separation requirement, and (corroborating this page's existing shadow-baseboard content from video 2 of this round) also poorly suited to shadow-baseboard installs. **Rigid multilayer** — second-best but highly manufacturer/collection-dependent: some lines permit up to 250m² continuous, others cap at just 10-15-20m² depending on lock strength and layer configuration. **Click-LVT** — locks are more break-resistant than SPC's; manufacturers still nominally recommend SPC-like area limits, but 2-3-room continuous runs commonly work in practice even though not officially endorsed — a lock may tent temporarily under thermal stress but typically returns flat afterward.
4. **⚠️ Installation-difficulty ranking, per-subtype**: **Glue LVT is the hardest to install of the four** — every plank must be individually glued, with excess adhesive cleaned off carefully enough not to cut/damage the material — meticulous, unforgiving work. The three floating types install essentially like laminate; **SPC is actually easier to install than laminate**, cuttable with just a utility knife. The main installation risk across all three floating types is breaking a lock during fitting.
5. **⚠️ Substrate-flatness requirement, per-subtype ranking, extends this page's existing quartz-vinyl-substrate-sensitivity content**: **Glue LVT is the least forgiving of all** — thin enough to fully telegraph any screed/self-leveler imperfection; "the flatter the better," described as needing a near-mirror substrate. **SPC and click-LVT** need a laminate-grade flat floor, because the lock is the single most vulnerable point — an out-of-tolerance bump underfoot, walked on repeatedly, can break it. **Rigid multilayer is the most forgiving of substrate irregularity** of the four, though the source still recommends meeting at minimum a SNiP-compliant screed even for this type.
6. **⚠️ Heavy-furniture-load tolerance, per-subtype ranking, extends this page's existing load content**: **Glue LVT is the most furniture-load-tolerant** — load is distributed only on the individual glued plank; furniture guide rails/tracks can even be screwed straight through it with no issue. **SPC is the least tolerant**: its own large linear expansion means a heavy four-legged sofa "locking" four planks in place while the surrounding floor keeps expanding/contracting around it creates real internal stress — the same underlying mechanism as laminate's own furniture-load caution, but rarer in practice unless installed as one long continuous run (compounding the risk). **Click-LVT and rigid multilayer sit in the middle** — click-LVT's greater base/lock elasticity makes it more forgiving; rigid multilayer's tolerance instead scales with how many fiberglass-mat reinforcement layers the specific product has (more layers = stronger).
7. **⚠️ Scratch-resistance quality-variance warning, new buying-vocabulary content**: any quartz-vinyl type scratches more easily than a quality melamine/corundum-coated laminate — an inherent property of the quartz-vinyl UV-lacquer top coat, which is always softer than laminate's wear layer. Real-world scratching mostly happens during the renovation/move-in phase itself (dragging unpadded metal furniture legs), rarely during ordinary post-renovation living. **The lacquer's actual quality is entirely manufacturer-dependent and invisible in a showroom** — the source cites a real pattern of client complaints (a customer bought a cheap no-name SPC product, only discovering it scratched easily after a month of normal use) and states the practical fix is buying only from large, established manufacturers with real QC and supplier vetting, since a wide per-m² price spread among visually similar SPC products reflects exactly this hidden quality difference.
8. **⚠️ Thickness specs and thickness-quality relationships, per-subtype, first numeric figures for this quartz-vinyl family on this page**: **Glue LVT**: 2-3mm range; thickness barely affects comfort at this range, but thicker feels marginally warmer underfoot (quartz-vinyl's own low thermal conductivity means it always reads warmer than some alternatives regardless). **SPC**: 3.5-5mm; available with integrated underlayment (bonded to the plank) or non-integrated (a separate underlayment laid first). **⚠️ Source's own recommendation: choose non-integrated underlayment for SPC** — integrated underlayment covers only the plank body, not the lock joints themselves, leaving joints unsupported and more vulnerable to damage/deformation than a full-coverage separate underlayment would allow; he expects manufacturers will eventually extend integrated coverage to the locks, but current product doesn't. Rule for SPC: **thicker is unconditionally more reliable.** **Click-LVT**: more elastic material/locks than SPC; practical minimum **>4mm** — below that, there's too little material for the lock to form properly, so thicker is always the safer choice for lock durability specifically. **Rigid multilayer**: integrated underlayment is *not* a problem here (unlike SPC) — the multilayer rigid construction is inherently stronger; what matters more for reliability is the number of reinforcement fiberglass-mat ("стеклохолст") layers — 2-3 layers is described as very reliable.
9. **⚠️ Moisture-resistance/wet-room suitability, per-subtype ranking, extends this page's existing wet-room quartz-vinyl content**: **Glue LVT** — best: usable in wet zones and loggias without restriction, steam-cleaner-safe; in constant-water-contact areas, the source recommends additionally sealing the joints with extra UV lacquer. **SPC** — the source states he'd categorically avoid it in wet rooms: the material itself doesn't fear water, but the joints do, and water ingress at a joint ruins the floor quickly; steam generators are absolutely prohibited on this type. **Click-LVT** — doesn't fear moisture at the material level (elastic locks tolerate it reasonably), and the source states he would still glue it down in a bathroom/WC despite manufacturers not recommending (and sometimes prohibiting) this — an off-label practice he's seen work reliably in small rooms specifically; steam generator use is still prohibited by manufacturers for this type regardless. **Rigid multilayer** — doesn't fear moisture, usable in bathrooms, but (being a floating floor) needs proper perimeter waterproofing; many manufacturers of this type do permit steam-generator use, unlike SPC.
10. **Comfort ranking, per-subtype**: glue LVT — quiet, modest shock absorption, fairly warm underfoot, described as feeling "semi-commercial." SPC — colder, noisier, harder underfoot than glue LVT, though still warmer/more comfortable than an average laminate — never reaching glue-LVT's comfort level. Click-LVT — noticeably more comfortable than glue LVT thanks to its thickness and underlayment, with real cushioning. **Rigid multilayer (thick variants) ranks best of all four for comfort** — most cushioned, warmest, most pleasant underfoot; thin rigid-multilayer variants land only slightly ahead of click-LVT.
11. **Repairability ranking, per-subtype, extends this page's existing quartz-vinyl repairability content**: easiest — glue LVT, especially when installed with thermally-reactivatable adhesive (heat the plank with a heat gun, lift it, apply fresh adhesive, reinstall — fast and simple). Hardest — SPC, click-LVT, and rigid multilayer share the same floating-floor problem: repairing one damaged plank requires disassembling the floor back to the nearest room edge, risking damage to previously-intact locks during that disassembly/reassembly.
12. **Eco-friendliness/off-gassing has no category-level ranking, unlike the other 10 criteria**: unlike hardness, heated-floor fit, or moisture resistance, emission safety is stated to depend entirely on the specific manufacturer's own raw-material sourcing and quality investment, not on which of the four quartz-vinyl subtypes is chosen.
