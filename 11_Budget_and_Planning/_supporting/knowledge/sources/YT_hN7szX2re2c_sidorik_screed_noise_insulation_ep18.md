## Regulations / Permits / Approvals

- **⚠️ Belarusian building-handover law requires a developer to deliver an apartment with a screed already poured** — a new-build unit cannot legally be handed over screed-less, even to a buyer who would prefer to do their own screed from scratch (as this practitioner did, demolishing the developer's dry-pressed screed and replacing it with his own reinforced cement-sand + sound-insulation build). He frames this as "extra work" from his own perspective (removing a screed he didn't want, before pouring his preferred one) — the rule itself is not framed as a complaint about the *requirement* to have a screed, but about being stuck removing a screed type/quality he didn't want in order to do his own from scratch. `confirmed`, level 1, direct statement of law, `single-account` (practitioner's own understanding of the law, not a cited statute).

## Materials

- **Named sub-screed noise-insulation membrane**: "Стопзвука М" ("Stopzvuka M") heavy bitumen-polymer membrane by ТехноСонус (Techno Sonus/TechnoSnus), designed specifically to address **structural noise** (vibration transmitted through the floor slab from footsteps, dropped objects, moved furniture, walkers) — described by the practitioner as functionally similar to a felt-backed roofing membrane ("обыкновенный рубероид с войлоком"), waterproof (doubles as a hydro-barrier), heat-fused at overlapping seams with an ordinary torch. `confirmed`, product-spec claim, self-installed.
- **Cost-comparison reasoning for choosing this membrane over linoleum as a cheaper substitute**: he specifically checked whether cheap linoleum could substitute for this membrane (similar characteristics) but found even the cheapest linoleum costs more than the dedicated soundproofing membrane — concluding there's no reason to substitute. `confirmed`, `single-account` comparative claim, no figures given.
- **Named XPS (extruded polystyrene) insulation board**: Технониколь Carbon Eco, 20mm thickness, rated to bear 10 tonnes/m² distributed load, rough/sandpaper-like surface texture specifically so mortar adheres to it, used both for weight reduction and thermal insulation under the screed. Per the practitioner's own comparison, an equivalent-volume mortar layer would be roughly 2.5 tonnes heavier across the apartment than the XPS-plus-thinner-screed combination he used — a real structural-load and cost argument for XPS over just pouring a thicker plain screed. `confirmed`, product-spec + comparative-arithmetic claim.
- **Manufacturer-technologist-confirmed system compatibility, third-party-sourced claim**: a technologist from ТехноСонус (the membrane manufacturer) reportedly confirmed directly to the practitioner that combining their bitumen-polymer membrane with 20mm Carbon Eco XPS on top is a valid, functional system — and separately that the 20mm XPS layer itself is rated to absorb 21 decibels, improving the screed's soundproofing "in every respect" (lighter, more sound-absorbing) without increasing the screed's own cost. `confirmed`, `single-account`, sourced from a third-party manufacturer rep relayed by the practitioner (not independently verified).
- **Cement, sand, plasticizer, and microfiber, named and specified**: Holcim Portland cement (chosen specifically because its stated strength grade on the bag reliably matches the actual product — flagged as not universal across cement brands); first-category (non-washed) sand, bagged (2-3x more expensive than a bulk dump-truck delivery, bought bagged only because a wash-sand supplier wasn't operating in winter); Sika superplasticizer (a dark-brown liquid, explicitly "not for drinking," reduces water demand while keeping the mix workable); Sika polypropylene microfiber (white fibers, 12mm length, 22-34 micrometers diameter) dosed at **60g per 100 liters of mix** — his ~75-liter batches used 50g, weighed on a kitchen scale. `confirmed`, product-spec claims with checkable dosing figures.
- **Plasticizer demonstration, a genuinely useful visual/practical proof**: mixed a stiff, minimal-water mortar, split it into two equal parts — one dosed with plasticizer, the other with an equal amount of *plain water* instead — the plasticizer-dosed portion became visibly workable/plastic while the plain-water portion stayed dry/unworkable with the same total liquid volume, demonstrating the plasticizer's real water-reduction effect rather than just being a marketing claim. Explicitly contrasted with a common bad practice (some tradespeople/builders use dish soap or leftover tile adhesive as an improvised plasticizer, or simply over-water the mix), both of which he states seriously degrade final strength/durability. He admits having done this himself in the past. `confirmed`, technique/demonstration, directly checkable reasoning.

## Flooring

- **Screed thickness reduced from developer default while adding features**: developer's original (now-removed) screed was 10cm; his replacement screed is 7cm, described as "technically possible" despite also adding a sound-insulation membrane, XPS layer, and rerouted electrical conduit within that reduced thickness. Slab-to-finish-floor height only increased by about 0.5cm net after full base cleanup (removing old mortar/plaster splatter with an angle grinder + diamond cup wheel + vacuum). `confirmed`, dimensioned.
- **Partitions must be built before the screed is poured, for two independent reasons**: (1) the screed must never physically contact a partition wall, to avoid transmitting structural noise/vibration between the floor and the wall — requiring a damper strip at every wall-to-screed junction (see below); (2) partitions are needed structurally/spatially before electrical conduit can be routed, since routing depends on final wall positions. `confirmed`, sequencing rule with reasoning, directly reinforces (with new reasoning detail) sequencing guidance already in this store from an unrelated source (`Flooring_Guide.md`'s existing walls-vs-screed sequencing section).
- **Perimeter damper strip ("демпферная лента") build, with a genuinely new detail**: cut from the same sound-insulation membrane roll into 15cm-wide strips, glued to the wall (at the height the screed itself will reach) with sealant/adhesive, topped with a second layer of 5mm foamed polyethylene stapled on — the extra foamed-polyethylene layer is needed because the base membrane is only 5mm thick, thinner than the ~10mm the screed's own linear-expansion movement calls for. Applied around the *entire* perimeter of every room getting a screed. Instruction manuals for this membrane call for it to run up onto the wall — accommodated here even though the walls weren't plastered yet at membrane-install time, by planning to trim/finish the strip against the eventual plaster line. Finish detail: after all finish work, the joint between screed and wall is sealed with acoustic sealant, and the damper strip is trimmed flush with the finished screed level. `confirmed`, technique detail, dimensioned.
- **Structural vs. airborne noise, a mechanism distinction that shapes what floor soundproofing can and can't fix** — stated directly by the practitioner, in his own words, as his personal reasoning for doing this work at all: structural noise (impacts, footsteps, furniture drag) is very hard to remove once it's already in the downstairs neighbor's ceiling — it's far more effective to treat it at the *source* floor (this apartment) than to try to absorb it from the receiving side below. Airborne noise (voices, loud music) is comparatively easier to address from the receiving side (e.g. double-layer drywall + mineral wool on the neighbor's own ceiling). He states explicitly that his own motivation for adding sound insulation is specifically **so the neighbors below won't be disturbed by him** — drawing on his own past experience of suffering from an upstairs neighbor's noise — and separately notes the same floor buildup incidentally also reduces airborne noise coming up *from* the downstairs neighbor into his own apartment. He anticipates viewer pushback calling this wasted money and states this explicitly as his own position/values choice, not a universal recommendation. `confirmed`, mechanism explanation + stated personal motivation (tagged as opinion/values, not a technical universal claim).
- **Reinforcement mesh specification and technique**: 10cm × 10cm cell, 4mm wire, laid with a one-cell overlap between mesh sheets, tied together at every second cell using tie wire cut into ~25cm lengths (compared two tying methods on camera — a cordless driver with a wire-twisting bit vs. a manual/semi-automatic wire-tying hook tool — and found the hook tool faster and more convenient in this case). **Reinforcement mesh is needed whenever screed thickness exceeds 3cm** — reasoning given: cement-sand mortar is strong in compression but comparatively weak against linear-expansion stresses, and the mesh compensates for that weakness. Mesh should sit roughly mid-depth in the screed layer, raised off the substrate using cut segments (~2.5cm) of scrap heating pipe (or cut XPS block offcuts) as spacers. `confirmed`, checkable spec + technique.
- **Beacon ("маяки") installation technique and precision claim**: 10mm-thick beacons preferred (stiffer than thinner types); trimmed 1cm off each end from the box before use (often arrive bent at the ends); spaced roughly 15cm narrower than the width of the leveling rule ("правило") that will ride on them; placed on lumps ("ляпы") of сементно-песчаный mortar rather than a full continuous bed, specifically so the mesh underneath stays at mid-depth rather than being crushed down by beacon weight on a thick mortar bed; lumps leveled to 1.5cm below the intended screed top surface (10mm beacon thickness + 5mm allowance for the mortar bed under it); lumps made the evening before, beacons set the next morning once they've set (avoids beacon sinkage/wobble on fresh mortar, which would produce inaccurate leveling and a shaky/unstable working surface to walk on while pouring). Classical laser-level + square method achieves **~2mm accuracy across the entire apartment's footprint**. `confirmed`, checkable spec + technique, directly comparable to/consistent with the existing `Flooring_Guide.md` beacon-leveling content from an unrelated source (template-stick technique) — both describe the same laser+square classical method without contradiction.
- **Home-mixed screed cost claim vs. ready-mix bagged screed**: mixing his own cement-sand-plasticizer-microfiber screed from raw ingredients costs roughly **half** what a ready-made bagged screed mix costs, while also allowing full control over mix quality. `confirmed`, `single-account`, no absolute figures given (relative comparison only, and no location/currency attached — not usable for cross-source price comparison per this project's rule).
- **Mixing order and rationale, itemized**: water first, then plasticizer, then microfiber added into the water (to disperse evenly before dry components go in), then half the sand (to make an initial workable "paste" easy to mix through), mixed once, then the rest of the sand added, then rest 5 minutes and mix again — this order is stated to reliably avoid unmixed lumps/dry pockets. Per-batch mix example: 75L of mix from ~75kg dry material (1 part cement : 2 parts sand by volume) plus plasticizer and 50g microfiber (see dosing above). `confirmed`, checkable recipe/procedure.
- **Screed pour and cure technique details**: convector floor area intentionally not poured with screed at this stage (handled separately, per the practitioner, to be set to match the finished floor height precisely rather than being screeded over); screed depth over the XPS layer ends up 5-6cm; fresh screed must be trimmed/cut down to the beacon level the *next day* while still soft — waiting longer means it can no longer be cut, only ground; all conduit fittings and brass crimp rings are wrapped in corrugated sleeving before the pour specifically so the pipe/cable can move slightly within its own protective sleeve without ever bonding directly to the screed around it (directly reinforces the earlier PEX-in-screed detail from episode #17, applied here to the general conduit/piping-in-screed practice). After pouring, the screed is cured by watering or covering with plastic sheeting to retain moisture (he does this regardless of whether admixtures are present, as an extra precaution, even though the plasticizer/microfiber combination is stated to reduce the *need* for this care). `confirmed`, technique detail.
- **Sub-screed noise-insulation membrane install time vs. total screed labor**: the entire screed job (excluding the sound-insulation membrane) took about **one week with two workers**; the sound-insulation membrane itself was installed separately during the electrical-rough-in stage and took about **one day**. `confirmed`, `single-account`, timeline data point (no cost attached).

## Electrical

- **Hot-melt/thermal glue reused for a second, independent purpose (Round 4's second sighting of this technique from this same channel)**: to fasten conduit-clip fixings to the floor without piercing the sub-screed noise-insulation membrane, the practitioner used hot-melt/thermal glue instead of a mechanical fastener — reports the clips hold securely and have performed well in ongoing use, keeping the membrane layer physically intact while still anchoring the cable conduit to the floor. Per Belarusian electrical code (ПУЭ, referenced generically, no article number given), routing electrical cable in corrugated conduit across a floor is permitted, and is explicitly *required* when no screed is yet in place and one is planned. `confirmed`, technique detail; `single-account` for the specific code reference (no article/section cited, generic reference to "правила ПУЭ").
