## Planning Rules / Design Process

- **Overarching principle (the video's thesis)**: never make unauthorized field changes to an approved design project. Every dimension in a properly-executed project is deliberately calculated — accounting for drainage/splash mechanics, appliance clearances, ergonomics, and downstream corridor/door widths — and a small client-initiated deviation during construction can silently break a chain of interdependent calculations elsewhere in the unit, producing real functional defects for negligible or zero actual benefit.

## Kitchen (case 1: skipped false wall)

- **Client skipped a planned false wall** (intended to conceal utility risers while creating a deepened windowsill workspace with outlets routed in the window slope) and instead built a simple box around the risers only, leaving the radiator exposed. Consequences, each explained:
  - Curtains still have to clear the radiator regardless of the false wall's presence — the actual space difference from skipping it was only ~1-2cm, not the meaningful gain the client expected.
  - The utility risers still needed boxing either way — no savings there either.
  - Lost the planned wide windowsill workspace (~1 additional m²) and the outlets-in-window-slope solution that would have let several small appliances (coffee machine, kettle, toaster) run from that surface simultaneously.
  - Lost a clean rectangular room shape, a continuous cabinet run with a properly integrated toe-kick, and a filler/spacer piece that would have let the fridge door open past 90° and prevented a dead "bag of bags" zone behind the fridge.
- **Reusable rule surfaced by this case**: routing outlets into a window-slope/windowsill area to turn a deepened windowsill into a genuine small-appliance work surface is described as one of the best, well-proven solutions in modern renovation — don't skip it to save a few centimeters that don't actually materialize as usable space.

## Bathroom (case 2: lengthened tub recess)

- **Client lengthened the tub recess by 20cm beyond the design** ("thought bigger would be better"), reducing clearance and causing a specific water-splash defect: even with the ledge slope built at the maximum feasible level, water doesn't fully drain reliably, and when someone sits, leans back, or shifts in a full tub, water reliably splashes out onto the floor at that spot — occupants have to dry the floor with a towel/hair dryer after every bath, and the leak-protection sensor at that location is reportedly triggered repeatedly by the splashing.
- **The original tub-recess dimension was deliberately calculated to avoid exactly this splash-out risk** — lengthening it, even though it intuitively seems like it should only add spaciousness, introduced a real functional defect instead.
- **The extra 20cm bought nothing functionally**: the client's motivation was a bigger space for the washing machine/dirty-laundry hamper area, but the sink and washing machine positions were unaffected by the extra 20cm either way — the tradeoff gained only a larger dirty-laundry zone at the cost of the splash-water defect.
- **Secondary defect from the same deviation**: the resulting rounded/curved tile-to-tub junction detail is hard to execute cleanly against rectangular tile, producing external corners and joints that permanently accumulate grime and become slippery — corroborates and extends this store's existing curved/radius-junction caution (see companion source `yt_CFWkoe5dsc0`, same batch).
- **Downstream chain-reaction cost**: to accommodate the marginal bathroom enlargement, a corridor originally 1.10m was reduced to 1.00m, and a planned 800mm door was reduced to 700mm — a real example of one small unauthorized field change forcing further compromises elsewhere in the unit that don't show up as obviously connected at the time.
