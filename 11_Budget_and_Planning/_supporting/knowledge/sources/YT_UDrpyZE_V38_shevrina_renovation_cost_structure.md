## Numeric Data / Cost Drivers — The Cost Structure

- **⚠️ Contractor labour + rough materials = 24.5% of the total budget** — the largest single slice, "почти четверть," and she states **this category's share repeats from project to project.** The single most transferable figure in the source.
- **⚠️ Shopping for a cheaper crew does not reduce it** — crew rates barely differ between crews. "Стоимость в данной категории уменьшается не за счёт выбора просто другой бригады, а за счёт выбора других работ." **The lever is the scope of work, not the vendor.**
- **Inside the crew's estimate, in descending order**: rough finishing works → finishing works → electrical → plumbing → demolition. **Electrical is not worth attacking** — cable runs, chasing and back-box installation are each individually necessary and the category's weight is modest.
- **Inside rough works, in descending order**: walls → ceilings → floors → **reveals (откосы), the smallest contributor of all.** Direct consequence she draws: **plastic reveals are a pointless economy** — they save very little and look bad.
- **⚠️ GKL (plasterboard) partitions cost about 2.5× more than foam-block** for essentially equal volume. "ГКЛ всегда были и есть не самыми дешёвыми перегородками."
- **⚠️ Preparing walls for painting is a very large line — large enough to invert an intuition.** Wallpaper remains genuinely budget ("как бы я их ни ненавидела" — she notes wallpaper is short-lived, behaves badly over wall cracks and can't be restored). **And on one real project where they were trying to economise, cladding a wall in 120×60 porcelain to the ceiling came out cheaper in labour than preparing that wall for paint.** A counter-intuitive, checkable claim.
- **Ceilings**: GKL ceilings dominate the ceiling line. Her cheaper *and* aesthetic alternative: **plaster the existing structural slab** and use surface-mounted fixtures.
- **Floors**: screed, sound insulation and self-levelling compound dominate — and are explicitly **not** where to economise. The expensive finish item is **glued engineered board** (gluing to plywood costs more still); **laminate or any floating floor is a genuine saving in both goods and labour.**
- **⚠️ Large-format ceramic slabs are expensive mainly in labour.** On this project, ceramic slabs of 1×3 m were used: **half the area of the porcelain, roughly double the price**, and the same inversion appears in the materials category — despite covering about half the area, the ceramic cost about the same as all the porcelain. Removing the slab ceramic would have cut the budget significantly.
- **Myth corrected: 45° mitring of porcelain is not as expensive as people assume.**
- **Joinery (столярка) is the second-largest category overall** — even with ~80% of the cabinets costed in chipboard and only the kitchen and a couple of WC wall units in MDF. **She states plainly that it essentially cannot be reduced** if already costed in the cheapest material; the only real levers are deferring purchases, or planning at the very start around ready-made units from shops and building the partitions and dropped ceilings to suit them.
- **Third-largest: finishing materials** — and within it, **the paint brand barely matters** (San Marco vs. Sherwin-Williams) because paint is a small share and the labour dominates. If you truly need to cut, switch to wallpaper rather than to a cheaper paint.

## Cost Drivers — Doors, with real percentages

- **⚠️ Reverse-swing doors cost about 20% more than standard-swing.** Her advice: design the plan so the convenient swing is also the standard one.
- **⚠️ Floor-to-ceiling doors cost about 15% more** than standard 2100 mm doors.
- **Avoid concealed doors supplied pre-finished with panels** — plain paint-ready concealed doors are cheaper.
- **⚠️ Concealed doors no longer cost more than ordinary doors** — a notable claim, and her explicit advice not to abandon concealed doors as an economy.
- Glass constructions (shower enclosures, sliding loggia partitions) contribute less than concealed doors but do add up.

## Cost Drivers — Lighting, sanitaryware, and the "useless economy" list

- **Lighting**: cutting spot count or LED strip runs saves very little; **track lighting is the heavy line.** On a plastered ceiling, **surface-mounted non-magnetic tracks** cut the budget substantially while keeping tracks.
- **Sanitaryware**: budget mixers can roughly halve that line. **Crucially, the labour to install a *concealed* mixer is not much more than a surface-mounted one** compared with the cost of running the services — so concealed mixers need not be abandoned on labour grounds, and budget concealed models exist.
- **Mirrors are cheap relative to their effect** — plain floor-to-ceiling mirrors (not brass-framed ones); people wrongly fear that adding a mirror behind a sofa or bed will blow the budget.
- **Curtain making is not a heavy category**, even at fabric category 5 and above.
- **Sockets and switches contribute negligibly** — buy the finish you actually want (metal rather than plain plastic). Her opening example is that trading Schneider Electric Merten for Atlas Design saves very little.
- **⚠️ Her summarised "useless economy" list — things that don't move the budget but make life worse**: (1) plastic reveals instead of proper ones; (2) giving up concealed doors (take them standard-height and paint-ready instead); (3) cheap socket/switch finishes; (4) avoiding concealed mixers; (5) avoiding large mirrors.
- **⚠️ Her summarised "real economy" list**: don't use plasterboard ceilings (plaster the slab); don't paint walls (wallpaper, or porcelain); don't use large-format above 120×60; don't use glued floor coverings (laminate or floating quartz-vinyl). She adds a design caveat: **if the porcelain is a natural-stone imitation, do not drop below ~120×45** — small stone-look formats look bad; small formats are only a sensible economy in non-stone designs.
