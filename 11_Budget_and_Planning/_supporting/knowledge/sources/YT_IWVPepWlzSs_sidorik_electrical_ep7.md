## Cable Circuits & Panel Design — Back-Box Drilling and Chasing Technique

- **Back-box hole-saw technique, in more mechanical detail than episode 6**: pre-drill the center point with a plain 6mm bit first, both to guide the hole saw and to give a visual check that the saw isn't drifting off-center; also trace the back-box's own outline on the wall as a second visual centering check. The hole saw's own built-in centering drill bit should be **removed once the cut has started** — leaving it in risks it punching all the way through to the far side of the block/wall before the saw itself has finished cutting, a real, named beginner mistake. **Soft block material can be drilled without hammer action at all**; the same overall method applies to concrete, but concrete needs hammer action. A vacuum can be run alongside drilling inside an already-isolated, sealed room (see episode 6's dust-barrier technique).
- **Wall-chaser (штроборез) technique, worked example**: measure the offset from the intended chase's centerline to the tool's own blade-edge reference (this practitioner's own tool: ≈2.8cm) and mark *that* offset line to follow while cutting, since the actual chase centerline itself becomes invisible once cutting starts. **Blade rotation direction matters**: cut in the direction indicated by an arrow marked on the tool (top-to-bottom, or flip the tool and go bottom-to-top) — cutting against the indicated direction still physically works, but throws dust out of the cut instead of keeping it contained for vacuum pickup. **Chase depth should be set based on the total planned finish buildup**: ~1.5cm for a chase with no further plaster coat planned over it, ~1cm where an additional finish-plaster layer will cover the remainder. Full PPE (goggles, ear protection, mask) still required even with a vacuum attached to the tool, since some debris/dust still escapes, especially where the cut first enters the wall.
- **Popping out the cut section between two parallel chaser cuts doesn't require a rotary hammer** — every wall chaser ships with a small chisel attachment specifically for this; pry the pre-cut section out by hand, which works even on high-density concrete and is markedly quieter than the "classic" rotary-hammer-knockout method (worth knowing for a noise-sensitive jobsite, at some cost in speed vs. the hammer method).
- **Chase-to-back-box offset rule**: position the chase to the **side** of a back-box opening, not centered on it, because a standard back-box's cable-entry knockout is itself on the side, not the center — undercut a little extra material there so a cable can be tucked into the box easily once it's mortared/glued in place.
- **Pro-tip for cable-clip prep**: if using T-shaped cable supports (Т-образные крепления), pre-drill their mounting holes into the chase during the initial "dirty work" (rough-chasing) phase itself, rather than generating a second round of dust later during the cable-laying phase.
- **Prime freshly-cut chases and back-box holes with a deep-penetrating primer immediately after cutting/vacuuming, while the room is still sealed and clean** (named product: **Ceresit CT 17**), applied by airless sprayer at low pressure (specifically to avoid an airborne primer mist) — a garden pump sprayer is a viable substitute if no airless sprayer is available; an airless sprayer is worth owning specifically when priming an entire apartment's worth of chases, not just a few.

## Cable Circuits & Panel Design — New Panel Niche Placement

- **Panel niche location decision**: the new electrical panel plus a separate low-voltage/data panel are both installed in a wall niche created between a ventilation duct and the wall where water-supply/sewage pipes run — with the electrical compartment **fully separated from the water-pipe side by a waterproofing layer plus a block wall**, judged the most space-efficient available location in this apartment. **External surface-mounted panel enclosures were explicitly rejected on aesthetic grounds** ("не особо нравятся") in favor of building a concealing false wall instead.
- **False-wall/panel-niche build technique**: metal profile frame screwed to the wall; GVL (gypsum-fiber board) cladding cut to size and screwed to the frame; perimeter sealed with foam, trimmed after ~1 hour cure; waterproofing (mastic at corners + waterproofing tape) applied specifically to fully isolate the panel side from the water-pipe side of the same enclosure; block sections cut and dry-fit around the panel/low-voltage-box openings — **cut with intentional margin so the panels can still be leveled after fitting**, then glued in place with foam adhesive and later reinforced with gypsum plaster; the pipe-facing side of the same assembly gets its own waterproofing pass as well.

## Plumbing / Soundproofing — Riser Sound/Thermal Insulation Before Enclosure (extends existing store rule)

- **Sequencing rule, extending this store's existing "soundproof the shaft/stack from the inside before rebuilding the box smaller" principle with a concrete real-world reason**: sound-insulate the sewage stack riser and thermally insulate the water-supply risers **before** building the partition/false wall that will enclose them — access is far easier before the wall exists, and (specific to this apartment) the sewage stack runs centrally through the unit near the kitchen and hallway, so unaddressed drain-flush noise would be noticeable in both rooms.
- **Real product and technique used**: a heavy, automotive-style bitumen-based sound-deadening mat plus a foamed-polyethylene layer, sold as a **pre-cut sleeve kit sized specifically for 110mm pipe** (cut sections wrap the pipe and are secured with hose clamps) — sourced from a named online soundproofing shop, with pickup/purchase **in Minsk** (level 1, see Region note). The speaker explicitly credits the shop's own staff for suggesting the ready-made pipe-specific kit rather than cutting flat sound-deadening sheet to size himself. A pipe cleanout/access point (ревизия) is cut into the wrap and resealed with matching sheet material rather than left uninsulated.
- **A related, secondary technique**: a steeply-angled ventilation duct segment was plastered directly (rather than framed with metal profile) specifically because its slope made profile-based framing awkward to mount — a practical exception to the more common profile-and-board boxing approach used elsewhere.
