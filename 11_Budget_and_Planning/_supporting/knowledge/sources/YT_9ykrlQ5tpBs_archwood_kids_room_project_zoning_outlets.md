## Kids Room — Durable Facts & Rules

### The "design for 10 years" rule (infrastructure layer)

- **Plan wall finish, electrical (outlets/switches), and built-in
  furniture to last ~10 years without redoing** — the underlying
  infrastructure (not furniture arrangement) should survive a child
  going from toddler through student age in the same room. Concretely:
  even if a child currently draws at a small kid's table, within 2-3
  years they'll need an adult-sized desk, so **plan outlets for a laptop
  charger and desk lamp at 90cm above the floor** now, ahead of the
  actual furniture arrival.
- **Sketch 2-3 furniture-layout plans at roughly 5-year intervals** when
  planning outlet/switch placement, rather than a single fixed plan —
  toddler stage, school-age stage, and teenager-heading-toward-adult-
  bedroom stage. **Cover unused outlets with blank plates** in the early
  stage rather than omitting them, so they're ready when needed later.
  Concretely: if the bed initially sits along a wall, place one outlet
  beside it, plus a second at "second level" height if a bunk/loft bed
  is anticipated later, plus provide switch control from bed height once
  furniture matures. Projecting ~5 years further, check whether the same
  room could become a teenager/student bedroom, and plan outlets above
  a future bedside table and a duplicate light-switch location for that
  stage too. **Where an outlet placement can serve two different
  furniture layouts, that's the ideal case** — plan outlets/switches to
  be genuinely dual-purpose or universal rather than layout-specific.
- **Wall finish should default to a neutral base (white, gray, beige)
  meant for repainting**, using purpose-made washable/anti-vandal kids'
  paint that wipes clean with a sponge. **Ordinary wallpaper (plain or
  paintable) is explicitly flagged as unlikely to survive a young
  child's room** — don't rely on it as the primary wall finish for a
  small child.
- **Dedicated drawing zone as a wall-damage mitigation, not blanket
  restriction**: a proper easel, or a full-height (floor-to-ceiling)
  chalkboard-paint or magnetic-paint strip in a neutral shape, gives a
  sanctioned drawing surface that works for both a toddler and later a
  teenager (who can pin posters/schedules to the same strip) — the
  neutral, floor-to-ceiling shape is specifically what makes it durable
  across age stages rather than reading as a "little kid" feature later.
- **Wardrobe/closet interior should be built neutral (no childish decor
  motifs) with adult-height rods, drawers, and baskets/bins from the
  start** — plan a dedicated books/textbooks storage zone separately.
  **Reuse caution**: if building many deep toy-storage compartments,
  plan ahead for what else could occupy that same volume later (bedding,
  etc.) — or plan to remove and replace toy-storage drawers with a
  pull-out guest bed later, rather than building single-purpose deep toy
  bins with no future repurposing path.

### Zoning by light priority (5-year-horizon layer)

- **Desk/work zone gets first claim on the room's best natural light**
  — explicitly framed as a health issue for a school-age child, not
  just comfort. **Hand-shadow rule**: daylight should fall from the
  child's **left side if right-handed, right side if left-handed**, so
  the writing hand's own shadow doesn't fall across the notebook/desk —
  independently corroborates the identical rule already recorded from
  `YT_Cr1vew4sA9g` (Kodolov), now a 2-source agreement within this
  vault. A student working mainly at a computer can have their desk
  placed further from the window, since screen-glare/hand-shadow concern
  differs from handwriting.
- **Play and sleep zones' priority depends on room shape and child age**:
  for young children who play at home often, put the play/table zone
  (with room for floor-based construction-toy play) closest to the
  window, bed further back — but leave enough open floor for active
  play so the child isn't confined to one small corner. For school-age
  children the play zone shrinks and a hobby zone appears instead (easel,
  piano, or a gaming chair/console corner).
- **Clothing storage should go in the room's most distant, darkest
  corner, with dedicated artificial lighting added there** — since
  clothing storage doesn't depend on natural light the way a desk does.
- **Toy/book storage should sit near the window, adjacent to the future
  desk location**: for a pre-school child, the most-used books/toys go
  there, while small-parts toys (construction sets, small mosaics) go
  higher so a young child can't scatter them; for a school-age child,
  the same shelf holds the main textbooks. **Coordination caution**:
  verify a storage shelf/unit won't block the outlets already planned
  above the future desk (at the 90cm height established above).

### Bed placement and bunk/loft-bed guidance

- **Most themed beds (house-shaped, ship-shaped) are designed for
  along-the-wall placement** — check this before committing to a themed
  bed, since it constrains layout more than a plain bed frame.
- **A loft/bunk bed with a built-in storage staircase (rather than a
  ladder) is explicitly recommended as the safer option** for a small
  room needing space savings — safer specifically because a child
  getting up at night (e.g. for the bathroom) descends stairs rather
  than a ladder, and the staircase itself doubles as a storage unit
  (drawers or open shelving under each step), preserving floor area that
  a separate storage piece would otherwise use.

### Sports corner (structural/rough-in note)

- **A pull-up bar, climbing wall, or similar sports fixture doesn't need
  to sit near the window**, but does need genuinely clear surrounding
  floor space so a child swinging on rings/a trapeze bar doesn't strike
  a nearby sharp corner. **Structural planning note, rough-in stage**:
  the wall section that will carry a pull-up bar or climbing wall needs
  reinforcement (plywood backing or chemical anchors) planned during the
  finishing/framing stage — coordinate with the foreman/site lead before
  that wall is closed up, not after.

### Design-tier longevity framework (3 tiers)

- **A three-tier longevity framework for kids'-room design decisions**:
  (1) renovation-level infrastructure (finishes, electrical, built-in
  cabinetry) — plan for **~10 years**; (2) themed furniture (a themed
  bed, decor-heavy pieces) — plan for **~5 years**, since a teenager may
  refuse to keep sleeping in a childish-themed bed; (3) decor (stickers,
  pillows, flags, rugs) — plan for **~1-2 years**, the cheapest and
  fastest layer to refresh as a child's specific interests change.
  **Practical implication**: build a neutral base at tiers 1-2 and let
  tier-3 decor carry the actual theming — a themed bed/space can still be
  achieved without buying expensive themed furniture, by decorating a
  neutral base instead (the source's own example: branded wall stickers
  that convert a room into a "spaceship" or "submarine" theme in minutes
  without buying themed furniture at all).

## Multi-Child Room Zoning — Durable Facts & a Real Case

- **Equal daylight access is the first thing to check when 2-3 children
  share one room** — not just equal floor area or equal furniture
  quality; verify no single child's bed/desk ends up in the room's
  darkest, most distant corner as an accident of layout, since (per the
  speaker) this kind of imbalance has been documented anecdotally as
  affecting a child's disposition over time (a specific referenced film,
  not independently named/verified — treated as the speaker's own
  cited belief, not confirmed research).
- **Sports/active-play zones can be shared/undivided even when
  sleep/desk zones are split per child** — no need to duplicate a sports
  corner or sports equipment for each child in a shared room.
- **Zoning technique when splitting one room between children**: divide
  by color and/or theme per half (each child gets a visually distinct
  zone), consider curtain-based or sliding-partition options for a
  soft division, and **give each child their own dedicated shelf inside
  a shared wardrobe/shelving unit** even when the storage furniture
  itself is shared — preserves a sense of personal space without a full
  furniture duplication.
- **Real multi-year case (3 children, 2 kids' rooms available)**: an
  older girl and two closer-in-age boys ("погодки," roughly a year
  apart) shared 2 rooms across 3 children. While young, one room was a
  shared bedroom (beds + clothing storage only) and the second was a
  shared playroom (no beds). **~6-7 years later**, the playroom was
  converted into the older girl's own private room as she entered
  adolescence, and the household re-split the remaining shared space
  several more times into: two small individual bedrooms (bed + minimal
  necessities per child) plus a shared common "mini living room" zone
  (play area, small sofa, shared sports corner) — avoiding duplicating
  shared-use items (e.g. the sports setup) across the individual rooms.
  **This case is a real, multi-stage precedent directly relevant to this
  project's own Phase 1→Phase 2 shared-to-split kids' room transition**
  (see `05_Kids_Room/Kids_Room_Index.md`'s own Phase 2 plan) — recorded
  as corroborating reference only, household plan notes not edited.
