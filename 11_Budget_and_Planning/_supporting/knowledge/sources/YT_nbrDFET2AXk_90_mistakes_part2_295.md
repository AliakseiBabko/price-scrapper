## Rules / Heuristics — Genuinely New Content

- **⚠️ Grid/lattice ceiling light fixture over a bed: two compounding reasons to avoid it.** A decorative grid-covered light panel positioned above a bed instead of a normal ambient fixture (1) blocks/absorbs most of its own light output through the fine grid, providing little real illumination, and (2) accumulates a large amount of dust in the grid structure regardless of cleaning effort, which then continuously sheds onto the person sleeping below. Avoid grid/lattice-style ceiling fixtures directly above a bed.
- **⚠️ Bathtub-then-tile sequencing rule, stated definitively**: install the bathtub *first*, flush against three walls, then tile *over* the tub's own edge/rim (tile overlapping the rim) — never tile the walls first and push the tub up against an already-finished tile perimeter, which forces an ugly trim/edge detail around the tub that doesn't even prevent water intrusion any better. Extends this store's existing "tile the tub apron with the same wall tile" technique with an explicit installation-order framing.
- **⚠️ Two-piece/nested decorative door handle durability caution**: a "futuristic"-looking door handle assembled from two separate components (one piece inserted into another) develops noticeable play/wobble within about a week of use, worsening to significant looseness within roughly six months — becomes noisy, uncomfortable, and annoying, and the resulting sharp mechanical edge can cause injury if brushed against. **General guidance: avoid decorative multi-piece handle assemblies for durability reasons**, regardless of how refined they look when new.
- **⚠️ Duct-box/niche "fill vs. finish" decision heuristic**: a developer-delivered structural niche/recess (e.g. around a duct or riser) can either be expensively finished with fully rounded, carefully detailed corners, or — the more rational choice in most cases — simply filled in with lightweight masonry (aerated concrete block, ~10cm) to produce one flat wall. Filling avoids both the finishing cost/complexity *and* a downstream benefit: a flat filled wall means kitchen-module electrical points can land directly in the filled niche without needing to chase (штробить) into an adjacent load-bearing wall. **General rule: when a developer niche isn't functionally needed, filling it flat is usually cheaper and more useful than finishing it as a feature.**
- **⚠️ Rendering/3D-visualization skepticism, explicitly stated as general buyer guidance**: never fully trust a polished design rendering — designers have a structural incentive to show visually "interesting geometry" to justify their fee, but complex geometric features that look striking in a render frequently look worse once actually built, and cost significantly more than a simpler alternative. **Ask what a rendered feature will actually look and cost like built, not just how it looks in the render.**
- **TV-wall electrical-point specification, precise**: exactly **2 outlets**, positioned **vertically** (not horizontally), centered on where the TV wall-mount bracket will sit — horizontal placement risks a direct conflict with the bracket's own footprint. More specific than this store's existing general "place the outlet behind the bracket" guidance.
- **⚠️ Wardrobe/closet-niche dimensional-consistency check, before committing to a door mechanism**: verify a storage niche's width is actually uniform from bottom to middle to top before choosing a sliding-door or hinged-door wardrobe system — documented real case: a ~4cm dimensional inconsistency across the niche's height made *neither* mechanism installable, forcing a downgrade to a simple shower-curtain-on-a-wire improvised solution instead of real doors. **Check this dimensional consistency early, before a door system is ordered.**
- **Wide-sink side-gap minimization rule**: a wide bathroom sink is a genuinely good choice, but leaving generous unused gaps on either side of it (documented case: 10cm one side, 12cm the other) wastes real usable countertop width. **General rule: size the sink to the largest unit that will actually fit the available space, rather than defaulting to a smaller size with wide safety margins on both sides.**
- **Backing-plate (закладная) reinforcement for a precisely-positioned or heavy fixture, reinforced with a real documented failure case**: a loggia pendant/chandelier fixture (an already-discouraged fixture type for a loggia per this store's existing guidance) took **4 mounting attempts** to find a secure, centered anchor point — 3 failed drill/mount tries left extra visible holes before the fixture was finally secured. **General reinforcement: pre-install proper ceiling blocking at the design stage for any fixture needing a precise or load-bearing mounting point, rather than improvising during installation.**
