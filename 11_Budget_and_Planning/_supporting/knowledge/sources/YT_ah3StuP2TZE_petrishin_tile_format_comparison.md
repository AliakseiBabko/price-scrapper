## Walls / Ceilings — Cornice/Stretch-Ceiling Incompatibility Mechanism

1. **⚠️ New mechanism: a wide perimeter cornice/molding combined with a
   stretch ceiling is a bad pairing, per the company's own advice to this
   client** — the raw ("black"/structural) ceiling underneath continues
   to rise and settle over time, while the stretch-ceiling membrane
   doesn't move the same way; a wide cornice bridging the two junctions
   ends up looking poor as the raw ceiling shifts relative to the
   membrane. The client's original design called for a stretch ceiling
   with a full-perimeter wide cornice; the company recommended switching
   to a drywall-boxed ceiling instead specifically to avoid this. New
   angle for this store's stretch-ceiling content — not previously
   flagged as a cornice-compatibility issue.

## Walls / Ceilings — Ceiling Soundproofing Product/Technique

2. **Named ceiling soundproofing product: "ТЗИ" (термозвукоизол,

## Bathroom — Tile Layout, Design details

Routed to `07_Bathroom/analysis/Tile_Selection_and_Layout.md` — extends
that page's existing box-sizing-for-layout and corner-mitre content with
independent, more elaborate real-object detail:

3. **Custom sloped shower-pan tile fitting**: a non-standard, custom-
   fabricated artificial-stone shower pan (built to the client's own
   sloped-drainage spec, not an off-the-shelf unit) required each
   individual small-format tile piece around its perimeter to be cut,
   ground, and beveled to its own unique angle to follow the pan's
   slope — pieces individually numbered in sequence (1st, 2nd, 3rd tile,
   etc.) so each custom-cut piece goes back in its exact intended
   position, since no two pieces are interchangeable.
4. **Client-driven 45°-mitered external corner** (repeats this page's
   existing corner-mitre convention, worked here as a real client
   preference over a corner-trim profile the client explicitly didn't
   want): first tile at the corner laid whole, the adjoining tile cut
   with a clean cut and also mitered to 45°.
5. **⚠️ Box/niche dimensions pre-calculated at the construction (框, "короб")
   stage specifically to land a whole tile at the 45°-miter cut point** —
   extends this page's existing "widen the box slightly to avoid a bad
   cut piece" principle with a second, independently observed real case:
   before building the shower-niche box, the crew counted how many tile
   rows would fit and sized the box so the row ending at the mitered
   corner would be a whole tile, not a cut sliver.
6. **Deliberate grout-joint-width variation (1.5mm vs. 2mm) used
   specifically as a layout-correction tool**, not just an aesthetic
   choice — on one section the joint was widened slightly (to 2mm) from
   the room's other 1.5mm joints specifically so the row would terminate
   on a whole tile instead of a narrow (~1cm) offcut sliver, which the
   installer states "can't be made to look right" once laid.
7. **⚠️ Access-hatch reorder + box-widening to avoid a cut tile at the
   hatch edge**: the inspection/access hatch was originally sized and
   ordered before the final tile layout was calculated; once the tile
   count was worked out, the crew reordered a different hatch size and
   widened the surrounding box to match, so the hatch could be framed by
   whole tiles on every side with no cut piece at its edge. A concrete,
   real-object example of this page's general "no fragment smaller than
   half a tile" layout principle being satisfied by changing a fixture
   order rather than the tile layout.
8. **Triangular-tile-shape seam-alignment complexity, named explicitly**:
   a triangular tile shape (used on part of this job) requires every
   seam to align continuously across the pattern — described as
   meaningfully harder to achieve than a straight-line/rectangular tile
   pattern, where the same alignment would be comparatively easy.
9. **Irregular-edge cutting against an imprecise plastic fixture
   surround**: a jacuzzi tub's own plastic apron/cladding ("кожух") has
   inherent manufacturing unevenness; the tile cut around it still needed
   to be precise and clean despite the imprecise reference edge it was
   cut against.

## Cost Drivers / Labor Prices — Complex-Tile Economics

10. **⚠️ Cost-driver mechanism for small/complex-format tile, corroborating
    and extending this page's existing "cost scales with material risk"
    labor-pricing rationale with a real mid-project revision case**:
    large-format tile is more profitable per hour for a tiler than
    small/complex-format tile, since a large-format job finishes faster,
    freeing the tiler to move to the next paid job sooner — this is
    stated as the reason many tilers are reluctant to take small/complex-
    format jobs even at good pay. On this specific job, once the crew
    understood how slow/difficult this tile would genuinely be, the
    company revisited and formally revised the cost estimate ("смета")
    mid-project and obtained the client's explicit consent before
    continuing — this was communicated to the client as a real
    possibility before work started, not sprung on them afterward.
11. **Real productivity data point**: laying one full wall of this
    complex small-format tile took a full workday (roughly 10am to
    evening), plus a full second day dedicated just to grout-joint
    cleanup on that same wall.
12. **Practitioner's own practical buying recommendation, distinct from
    his personal aesthetic opinion**: asked directly, the tiler
    recommends ordinary large-format tile to most people for simplicity/
    cost, even though he states he personally finds the small/complex-
    format tile's own visual result ("грязь" of joints, its own pattern)
    genuinely appealing too — an explicit split between a practical
    recommendation and a personal aesthetic preference.
