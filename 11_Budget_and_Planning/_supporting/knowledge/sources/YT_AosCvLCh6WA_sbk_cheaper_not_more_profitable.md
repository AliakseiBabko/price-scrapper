## Mistakes / Warnings — Contractor Vetting and Fraud Patterns

- **⚠️ Four concrete vetting questions recommended before hiring, stated as a practical checklist**: (1) visit an active job site in person (reveals real cleanliness/protection practices invisible in photos) or, if none is available, request close-up photos of real joints/corners/transitions specifically (not polished hero shots) — the *ability* to produce these on demand is itself treated as a competence signal; (2) ask directly whether gluing engineered/parquet board over the existing floor will cause problems — a competent answer proactively raises the developer-screed-defect risk and offers remediation options (reinforce vs. replace) without over-promising that reinforcement guarantees success; an unqualified "sure, no problem" is a red flag; (3) ask how often the crew will be visited/inspected — daily photo/video reporting into a shared chat is offered as this company's own standard practice; (4) ask the contractor's own attitude toward the client bringing independent technical supervision (технадзор) — hostility or discouragement is flagged as a warning sign, while premium-segment practice routinely works alongside (sometimes contractually requires) client-side technical supervision.
- **⚠️ Named fraud pattern, advertised implausibly low rates**: examples cited as fraud red flags include "elite renovation at 20,000 RUB/m²," "one-bedroom renovation for 200,000 RUB," and "three-bedroom with materials for 800,000 RUB." **Stated mechanism**: such quotes typically price roughly half the real scope, with the remainder recovered via change-order "extras" after the client is hooked, and these ads are typically run by high-volume, low-vetting economy operators (cited: 100-300 objects/month) who accept jobs sight-unseen.
- **Practical budget-limited path recommended, contrasted with the "impossible" premium-for-economy-price expectation**: simplify the design project's technical execution (with the original design team, not by hiring a cheaper crew for the same complex design) while preserving its visual outcome — the ceiling and flooring examples above are given as concrete instances of this substitution strategy.
