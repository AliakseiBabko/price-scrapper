## Mistakes / Warnings — Standout Finding

- **⚠️ Door/rough-opening height must be measured from the finished (post-screed) floor level, not from the raw structural floor slab — a genuinely severe, checkable planning-sequence mistake.** The original designer specified a technically-correct-looking opening height (2.60m, close to this store's own 2.70m current standard) but measured it from the bare concrete slab rather than accounting for the screed layer to be poured on top. Once the screed was actually placed, the real clear opening height came up short, and the door manufacturer's own installers — who came to measure for the doors — were the ones who caught the problem and said the lintels would need to be demolished and rebuilt significantly higher. **General rule: any door/opening height figure must be dimensioned from the finished floor surface (after screed/underlayment), never from the raw slab** — this single sequencing error was what triggered the client's entire wall-rebuild decision.

## Planning Rules — Family-Growth Planning

- **A layout should be planned against the family's stated future size, not just its current occupancy — a genuinely important planning-sequence principle, not just a nice-to-have.** The root cause behind the client's rebuild decision: at the time the original project began, the client's spouse was newly pregnant; by the time the walls were actually built and the door-height conflict surfaced, she was 8 months pregnant with their second child, and the client realized the existing layout (one bedroom, one kid's room) had no room for the additional child(ren) coming — the original designer never asked about planned family growth, only current occupancy. **General rule: a technical-requirements interview for a family with children (existing or planned) should explicitly ask about anticipated future children/family size, not just document current household composition** — since retrofitting a kids' room after walls are already built is far more costly than planning for it from the start (the source frames a 15-year ownership horizon as the right planning timeframe, not "this year's needs").
- **A "generously wide" corridor/hallway is not automatically good design if it comes at the cost of an undersized wet room** — the source specifically flags a 1.4m-wide corridor as excessive for this apartment's tier, preferring a narrower corridor with a larger bathroom instead, while separately noting that in a genuinely premium-tier apartment, a 1.4m corridor is legitimately desirable. **Corridor width judgment is tier-relative, not a fixed universal target** — context (what's being traded off against it) matters more than the raw number.
- **Two adjoining partition walls with mismatched, unnecessarily different thicknesses waste recoverable floor area** — flagged as a minor but real inefficiency: partition walls of different types/thicknesses can be joined even when unequal, so there's no technical reason to keep one wall thicker than needed just because it doesn't match a neighboring wall — thinning the excess wall recovers real bedroom floor area.
