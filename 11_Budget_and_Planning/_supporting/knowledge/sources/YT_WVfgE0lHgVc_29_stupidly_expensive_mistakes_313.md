## Kitchen

- **Kitchen flooring should be laid along the main direction of foot traffic, or (if that conflicts) along the direction of daylight entry** — never crossing both; crossing the light direction visually emphasizes wear, crossing the traffic direction accelerates it. `confirmed` as stated company rule, single-account/company-standard.
- **Outlet height above kitchen backsplash/countertop: 110cm from finished floor (Zemstandart standard)** — corroborates this store's existing 110cm countertop-outlet figure (multiple prior sources, same channel). **New supporting mechanism stated here**: most appliance plugs sit at 90° to their cable; an outlet placed too close to the countertop (example given: 95cm, only 5cm clearance) forces the plug to be inserted upside-down to clear the counter, which is impractical in daily use — not just an aesthetic issue.
- **Flooring should run as a single unbroken contour through a kitchen, not split into two pieces joined at a 45° diagonal seam mid-room** — stated reasons: (a) more offcuts/waste sent to the dumpster during installation (client pays for that waste indirectly), (b) a diagonal seam is more likely to end up running both across the light direction and across the traffic direction simultaneously.
- **Built-in refrigerator preferred over freestanding by default, absent a stated reason for freestanding** — freestanding leaves an open gap above the fridge (dust trap, wasted volume) and creates non-technological, ill-fitting seams between the backsplash/countertop and the fridge cabinet.
- **Flooring transitions between rooms should use a cork compensator strip, not a surface-mounted transition strip/threshold** — a surface strip is stated to (a) protrude above the floor plane and impede cleaning, (b) not seal tightly everywhere along its length, trapping dirt underneath that's very difficult to remove, (c) eventually lift/loosen at its tip, creating a snagging/tripping hazard for bare feet and socks, occasionally requiring re-gluing after being caught.
- **A wavy/curved decorative wall surface is incompatible with standard baseboard and ceiling-cornice installation** — installers cannot fit a straight baseboard/cornice against a non-planar wall, leaving a permanent, hard-to-clean junction gap along its full length (not reachable even with a toothbrush, let alone a mop or robot vacuum). Flag any decorative curved-wall design against this practical installation constraint.
- **Wall/partition layout must be finalized at the design stage, not discovered during construction** — worked example: a 40cm-wide × 60cm-deep leftover niche next to a window, too narrow to fit a closing door/panel because the panel's swept plane would clip the window; the only practical use for such a leftover niche is passive storage (e.g. an ironing board). General planning caution against leaving unusable leftover niches from unplanned wall placement.
- **Stretch-ceiling baguette/trim profile must be consistent around a room's full perimeter** — worked example: most of a kitchen ceiling perimeter got the more expensive "shadow-gap" ("теневой") profile, but a section near a window was installed with a standard baguette/gasket profile by mistake despite the same "author supervision" service being paid for; the two profiles look visibly different and inconsistent side by side. A concrete QC-failure example specifically implicating supervision, not just execution.
- **Kitchen backsplash material should match the countertop material for a clean, technologically sound joint** — porcelain-tile ("керамогранит") backsplash specifically flagged as an outdated choice not used in modern renovation: grout lines in the joints will yellow/degrade over time, grease and grime accumulate on the tile and can't be fully cleaned off, and porcelain tile has no clean junction detail against either the fridge or the countertop, forcing an ugly transition trim strip that degrades the kitchen's appearance.
- **Cooking-zone task lighting is mandatory in a kitchen project, not optional** — worked example: the project omitted cooking-zone task lighting entirely, and once construction is finished it can only be added by disassembling and redoing half the kitchen. A related billed-but-non-functional example: the light switch for that (never-installed) lighting was still installed and paid for (switch unit, drilling, chase-cutting/штроба, plus expensive drilling through the porcelain-tile backsplash for the switch), but doesn't work because there's no fixture and no cable stub-out left at the cabinet modules for it.
- **Every outlet in a kitchen should have a defined purpose — no "spare"/unassigned outlets** — worked example: six outlets clustered in one small area (two in a corner, two near the sink, two more only ~30cm further) with no stated purpose for most of them; each unnecessary outlet costs money three times over — purchase, installation labor, and (in this kitchen) an added drilling cost through the porcelain-tile backsplash for its cutout.
- **General/overhead lighting is mandatory in every room, especially a kitchen — never rely on decorative/accent lighting alone.** Worked example: the kitchen had no main ceiling light, only a dim dining-area accent fixture (explicitly described as only appropriate as supplementary lighting); demonstrated on camera (fixture on, blinds closed) that the countertop remained nearly unlit, calling it a real finger-cutting-while-cooking safety risk during evening use — even with some daylight contribution during the day.
- **Kitchen entry/passage clearance should be planned for two people passing simultaneously** — worked example: an 80cm-wide passage at the kitchen entry, with visible wear/damage on the adjacent baseboard's outer corner indicating repeated bumping even though the renovation is recent (offered as circumstantial evidence the passage really is being caught on regularly, not just a theoretical risk).

## Bathroom / WC

- **Tub, sink, and shower placement across multiple bathrooms should be chosen to minimize the specific end-clearance gap at the tub's foot, not maximized** — worked example comparing three candidate bathroom slots for a 1.70m tub: 30cm end gap and 20cm end gap were both rejected ("cannot place here"), a 12cm end gap was the accepted placement — explicit secondary reason given: place the tub where plumbing supply/drain runs by the shortest path to the fixtures. `single-account`, real worked case, not stated as a universal numeric threshold (no minimum/maximum gap value given, just "choose the tightest workable fit with shortest plumbing run").
- **Concealed-cistern installation frame ("инсталляция") requires dedicated wall-cavity space that must be planned into the layout up front** — worked example: a DIY layout positioned all three toilets correctly relative to the stack risers but omitted the wall box needed to conceal the cistern/frame, meaning the walls as built would still need modification even if the rest of the layout were kept as-is.
- **Utility storage cabinet depth should match its contents, not a single default depth** — worked example distinguishes a 600mm-deep cabinet for small tools/hand implements from a 700mm-deep cabinet needed for a full-size vacuum cleaner, buckets, and mops/cleaning equipment; the 700mm cabinet in particular needs real floor space that a narrow leftover corridor cannot supply. Extends this store's existing wardrobe-niche depth-by-purpose framework (previously 50cm technical/60cm outerwear, `yt_K-W4dLJGEtg`) with a new pairing (600mm hand tools / 700mm vacuum-and-cleaning-equipment).
- **Sink cabinet placement should avoid protruding into a door/passage opening** — worked re-plan example: shifting a door opening slightly to one side let an 80cm sink cabinet sit clear of the passage swing entirely, at the cost of a different sink elsewhere in the same layout ending up with a partial protrusion into its own opening — illustrating that eliminating one clearance conflict in a fixed footprint can just relocate it rather than remove it.

## Bedroom / Living Room

- **Glass partition between a living room and a bedroom provides effectively zero acoustic or visual privacy** — worked example: a glass partition installed between living and bedroom to "look nice" leaves anyone in the living room able to see directly into the bedroom, explicitly flagged as eliminating personal privacy/intimacy for whoever uses the bedroom.
- **Bedroom main-light switch must be positioned on the door-handle side, reachable without looking, on entry** — worked counter-example: switch placed such that the occupant must first locate it, then reach behind the open door to operate it.
- **Bedroom main-light switch must be a two-way ("passage") switch, operable from both the entry and the bed**, so the main light (which shines directly at the sleeper) can be turned off without getting up, once ancillary bedside lighting is already on. Worked counter-example: only a single-point switch was installed, forcing the occupant to get up and walk back to the door to turn off the main light after getting into bed.
- **Curtain zone must be planned before finalizing nearby electrical point positions, not after** — worked example: bedside outlets/switches and a fixture were positioned without accounting for curtain travel; once curtains are drawn, they physically touch the fixture and fully block access to the affected outlet and switch.
- **TV should be positioned directly facing the sofa/bed it serves** — general placement rule, illustrated by a bedroom TV mis-oriented enough that comfortable viewing required leaning/craning.

## Entrance / Hallway

- **Passage-switch requirement extends to hallways/entryways connecting to a kitchen**: a hallway/entry passage-switch was omitted, so returning to the "clean" zone and passing again through the "dirty" (shoe-removal) entry zone is required just to turn off the hallway light after walking through to the kitchen — inconvenient and forces re-crossing the dirty zone.

## Materials

- **Electric towel warmers are the only choice recommended in "modern renovation"; water-fed towel warmers are called outdated** — stated reason: a water-fed unit will eventually leak over time, and the worked example additionally lacked any leak-sensor protection, meaning an eventual leak would go undetected.
- **Ceramic/stone hearth protection material around a fireplace should only be used for a genuine wood/gas fireplace (for ember/spark fire protection), not for an electric fireplace** — worked example: a porcelain-tile accent area was installed around what turned out to be an electric fireplace, adding installation cost and material cost with no functional fire-protection benefit, plus creating an unnecessary transition-strip trip/injury hazard at the flooring-material seam.
- **Concealed/hidden doors require matching concealed baseboards, never a standard visible baseboard profile** — worked example: standard baseboards installed alongside concealed doors, described as looking poor together, compounded by choosing an especially low-cost baseboard profile.
- **A freestanding/vessel bathroom sink not integrated with its countertop requires ongoing maintenance the buyer should expect**: water spots, splashes, and grime accumulate on the surrounding countertop and can't be wiped away with a wet hand into the basin the way an integrated sink allows — a dedicated cleaning cloth near the sink is effectively required; dirt/soap/dust also accumulates in the gap underneath a freestanding sink.
- **Bathtub must be installed before wall tiling is completed, not after**, so the tile can overhang the tub edge for a sealed, finished-looking junction. Worked counter-example: walls were tiled first, tub installed second, leaving the tub sandwiched between finished tile walls; the resulting water-sealing gap required a visible trim/baseboard strip along the tub, especially poor-looking at the vertical corner joints, and its sealant/caulk line will discolor (blacken) over time and need periodic renewal.
