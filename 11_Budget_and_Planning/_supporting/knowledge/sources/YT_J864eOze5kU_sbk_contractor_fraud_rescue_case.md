## Mistakes / Warnings — Contractor Fraud, Abandonment, and Recovery: a Real First-Hand Case

- **⚠️ Named vetting failure, the client's own account**: as first-time renovation buyers new to the city, the clients searched for a contractor exclusively via Avito (an open classifieds marketplace) rather than through personal referrals — the contractor's portfolio photos looked professional, and he verbally described a detailed process (clients doing some prep work themselves first, then his crew continuing) that made him seem credible. The client's own explicit retrospective framing: **"finding a contractor on an open marketplace site is always a lottery, and you don't always win it."**
- **⚠️ Named red flag pattern, repeated stalling on a written contract**: the contractor verbally promised a contract "would be brought next time" at every site visit, but never actually produced one — described by the client as the contractor being skilled at reassuring/placating talk about how the renovation was progressing, while the actual written agreement never materialized. **⚠️ Explicit rule stated directly by the speaker**: without a contract, it becomes extremely difficult to prove after the fact that a specific person did specific work, received specific payment, or purchased specific materials — "no contract, no [provable] work."
- **⚠️ Named timeline-slippage pattern, framed as a real but ambiguous warning sign**: the contractor's original 2-week estimate slipped to a stated 3-3.5 weeks (explained away as the apartment being unusually run-down), then continued past a month, each time attributed to normal drying/curing time for various stages — the speaker's own framing: schedule slippage alone is common and not necessarily fraud, but this case shows how repeated, vaguely-justified slippage can also mask a contractor who's already failing or about to disappear.
- **⚠️ Real abandonment pattern**: the contractor called citing a family emergency requiring him to step away temporarily, and — a detail the client herself flags as retrospectively suspicious — **removed all of his tools from the site before leaving**, which in hindsight reads as a sign he had no intention of returning, not normal behavior for someone stepping away briefly for an emergency. He then stopped answering calls entirely after the New Year holidays and eventually changed his phone number.
- **⚠️ Real, uncomfortable observation about other tradespeople staying silent**: a plumber/sanitary-ware installer living in the same building, who had previously done similar bathroom work, told the clients afterward that he had recognized the original contractor's tiling work as incompetent while it was happening, but chose not to say anything at the time on the reasoning that "every craftsman works differently." **⚠️ Explicit correction offered by the speaker**: this reasoning is wrong — any competent tradesperson who notices genuinely bad practice on a job site should at minimum flag it to the client, since early notice can stop further wasted material/labor cost from compounding; staying silent because "it's not my job to comment" is itself a failure, not professional courtesy.
- **⚠️ Real recovery mechanism, a genuinely actionable and non-obvious tactic**: with no contract, no ID/passport details, and a disappeared phone number, the clients partially recovered funds by disputing a bank transfer through Sberbank (the transferring bank), framing it as a payment mistakenly sent to the wrong recipient — the bank could only send the recipient a request to voluntarily return the funds, but this **did prompt a partial refund** and, notably, revealed a working phone number when the contractor sent a payment confirmation screenshot from it. **⚠️ Second recovery tactic, and the one that finally worked**: after further stonewalling and repeated number-blocking (the clients resorted to borrowing friends' phones to get through), an explicit written threat to report the matter to the police as fraud was what finally got the contractor to respond and engage. **Explicit conclusion stated directly**: without a contract, going to the police is described as essentially the only remaining lawful recourse available, even though it may not always work.
- **⚠️ A real, telling coda on accountability**: when the clients finally met the contractor in person, he did not apologize or acknowledge wrongdoing — his first question was reportedly "what exactly is wrong with my work?"
- **General lesson, stated directly by both the client and the host**: the client's own explicit retrospective recommendation is to source contractors through trusted personal referrals (in her case, eventually reaching this channel's own company via a personal-network referral chain, including watching the company's own YouTube portfolio and reviews first) rather than an open marketplace listing site — repeating this project's own standing "verify a contractor independently, don't rely on portfolio photos alone" convention with a real, costly cautionary case behind it.

## Mistakes / Warnings / Engineering — Real Remediation Details From the Rescue Renovation

- **⚠️ Gas pipe legally cannot be enclosed/boxed in behind a finished wall**: an exposed gas supply pipe running along the ceiling in both the bathroom and toilet is left visibly exposed on both rooms — stated directly as a **legal requirement, not an aesthetic choice or a remaining defect** ("gas pipes cannot be hidden/bricked into a wall"). Where the pipe crosses behind a lower cabinet/install area, a removable access panel is built in specifically so the pipe stays visually and physically accessible for inspection without demolishing the surrounding finish.
- **Off-center toilet placement, a real budget-constraint case, not a mistake**: the toilet in this project sits slightly off-center in its alcove because the project's budget did not allow for replacing/relocating the original cast-iron sewer stack to achieve a perfectly centered outlet — described as not visually obvious in practice, with the extra space on one side used instead for a toilet-brush holder, a pragmatic accommodation rather than a hidden flaw.
- **Utility-meter relocation for accessibility, a real before/after fix**: the previous contractor had positioned water/utility meters behind the toilet, accessible only through two small holes requiring the occupant to nearly put their head in to read them — relocated in the rescue renovation to an accessible niche, to be finished later with a small access door.
- **Tile-layout symmetry technique, a real worked detail**: tile layout is deliberately planned so that matching-width cut/trim pieces land in corresponding corners on both sides of the room (the same cut width in corner-pairs, and matching cut widths on opposing walls) — a real applied example of a general symmetric-layout planning principle, not a novel rule but a concretely demonstrated case.
- **Pipe/wall-chase straightening via re-routing, not just re-boxing**: a previous ad hoc plumbing chase/boxed-in section with an awkward inspection hatch was eliminated entirely by re-routing (re-welding) the towel-warmer riser pipe so the wall it ran along could be built flat and straight, rather than re-building a new box around the same awkward routing — freeing enough floor space to fit both a washing machine and a sink-under-tub in what had previously been a cramped, obstructed area.
