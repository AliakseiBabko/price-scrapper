## Durable Facts

- Advises **against** mounting switches at child shoulder height "so kids can't reach them" — the speaker's counter-argument: a toddler's curiosity about a switch is a short-lived phase (his estimate: maybe ~10 times total, mostly around age 1.5–2), whereas mounting switches at ~1.70 m keeps children from reaching them until roughly age 10 — which he argues causes years of greater annoyance (child fetching a stool or asking an adult to turn on the light every time) than the brief toddler phase. `unverified`, speaker's own reasoning/opinion, not an engineering or safety fact.
- Two methods given for finding a comfortable personal switch height:
  1. **Self-calibration method**: walk into the room at different speeds (fast, normal, slow) without looking and mark on the wall where your hand naturally lands each time; repeat for every adult household member; find the midpoint between the highest and lowest marks recorded, then subtract another **3 cm** — that becomes the switch's center height. Reasoning given: a switch is comfortable when, in the OFF position, its top half already protrudes outward, so pressing it "down" to turn ON works with a natural downward/across motion; setting the center 3 cm below your natural reach point ensures you slightly reach for it rather than needing to bend the wrist upward to reach a raised top half. `unverified`, speaker's own method.
  2. **Standard reference figures** ("per the ergonomics textbook", no source cited): **90 cm** from finished floor for average-height people, **95 cm** for above-average height; if a household mixes tall and short members, default to 90 cm — reasoning: reaching down to a lower switch is described as more comfortable than bending the wrist up to reach a higher one. `unverified`, no citation given for the "textbook."
- **Horizontal offset from a door opening**: for a single-gang switch point, center it **15 cm from the rough (unfinished) edge of the door opening** — after door casing/architrave installation, this produces a visually correct gap between the casing and the switch plate. Too little offset makes the plate look like it touches/overlaps the casing (or forces trimming a wide casing); too much offset makes the switch harder to reach without looking and reduces usable wall space for furniture (commonly a wardrobe) placed between the switch and the next corner. `unverified`, specific checkable number from a single speaker.
- For multiple switch/outlet points grouped near one door: mark the first point at 15 cm from the opening, run a level horizontal line from it, then space additional single-gang box centers along that line. The speaker explicitly flags this method as a DIY approximation only — a professional designer/electrician should instead calculate exact coordinates from each point's center rather than eyeballing spacing along a line. `unverified`
