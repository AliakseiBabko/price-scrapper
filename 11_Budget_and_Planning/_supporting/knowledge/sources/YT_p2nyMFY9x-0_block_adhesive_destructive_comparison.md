## Walls — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Six materials tradespeople use to bond gas-concrete ("aerated concrete"/газобетон) blocks, per this source — only two are actually correct choices, per Zemskov and the guest technologist**: (1) cement-sand mix, (2) Rotband (gypsum-based universal plaster), (3) tile adhesive, (4) specialized mineral block adhesive, (5) specialized polyurethane block adhesive ("foam-glue"/пеноклей), (6) ordinary construction/mounting foam. The source's stated reasoning for each material's real-world adoption despite most being wrong choices: tradespeople default to whatever's already mixed/on hand from another task (cement-sand mix from bedding the first course, Rotband from plastering, tile adhesive from tiling elsewhere on the same site) rather than buying a dedicated product.
- **Cement-sand mix — correct only for the first course, never for subsequent rows.** Zemskov/the guest's stated reasoning: the first course sits on an uneven poured-slab surface with real irregularities that only a thicker mortar bed can absorb — recommended minimum horizontal-joint thickness 10mm (up to 1.5cm+), vertical joint 12mm. Beyond the first row, cement-sand mix is explicitly **not recommended**: material consumption is roughly 5× higher than the specialized options below (driven by the required joint thickness), plasticity is poor, and strength develops slowly. **Mixing note**: after initial mixing, wait 5 minutes then re-mix — skipping this rest period costs 20-30% of the mix's final strength, per the guest.
- **Rotband (gypsum plaster) — technically bonds, but not recommended for block-to-block joints.** Manufacturer-recommended minimum application thickness is 5mm, which the source states makes it needlessly material-expensive for this use versus a specialized thin-joint adhesive. **A second, more serious reason given**: in a humid room, gypsum-based Rotband used as a wall-plaster substrate would end up finished over with cement-based plaster (since gypsum plaster isn't used in humid rooms) — the two chemistries reacting against each other is flagged as a real compatibility risk, not just a cost issue.
- **Tile adhesive — bonds effectively but is a real, quantified overpay for this use.** Per the source: tile adhesive is formulated with costly water-retention/adhesion-boosting additives specifically needed to hold heavy porcelain tile onto a wall — a load gas-concrete blocks (simply stacked on each other, not hanging off a vertical surface) never experiences, making those additives pure unneeded cost here. **Stated real cost delta: roughly 50% more expensive than specialized block adhesive** for materially the same core bonding job — negligible on a single small partition wall, but scaling to "tens of thousands of RUB" in overpay on a full apartment or house-scale project. **Practical guidance given**: acceptable/convenient for a single small wall (already on hand, no extra purchase needed), but explicitly **not** recommended at real project scale.
- **Specialized mineral block adhesive — the standard correct default, per this source.** Purpose-built for thin-joint (танкошовная) masonry once block manufacturing tolerances became precise enough to support it (see also this store's existing 7-blocks-per-m² / 1-bag-per-m³ material-calculation content from `yt_8kOUv9EVQTQ`, same channel, complementary). Can be mixed far more fluid than tile adhesive, achieving joints as thin as ~0.5mm, cutting material consumption several-fold versus tile adhesive. **Rated working temperature range: -10°C to +35°C** — one product usable year-round, versus needing three different mixes to cover the same seasonal range with less temperature-tolerant alternatives. Real-world availability caveat given by the guest: markup on this product is thin enough that many retail stores stock it reluctantly, sometimes forcing a tradesperson to buy directly from the manufacturer even for a single bag — offered as the source's own explanation for why this correct product hasn't fully displaced tile adhesive/Rotband in common practice despite being the better choice.
- **Specialized polyurethane block adhesive ("foam-glue"/пеноклей) — the source's own top recommendation, with four stated real advantages.** Distinct from ordinary construction/mounting foam (see below) despite superficial visual similarity. (1) **Dry, clean work** — no wet mixing, no dust or mess, versus every mixed-material option above. (2) **No water or electricity needed on site** — decisive for an off-grid/early-stage build, per the source. (3) **~3× faster installation** — blocks are simply placed and set, rather than troweled on and manually pressed/adjusted into a mortar bed. (4) **Much faster strength development**: initial strength in 30 minutes, full strength in 24 hours — versus roughly 3 days initial / 28 days full strength for any of the mixed mortars/adhesives above. **Coverage cited**: one canister yields 70-100 linear meters of adhesive bead, enough for up to 20m² of 100mm-thick block masonry. **Stated hard caveat**: because foam-glue doesn't allow adjusting/leveling of subsequent rows the way a workable mortar bed does, the **first course must be leveled with extreme precision** before switching to foam-glue for the rest — an imperfect first row cannot be corrected later the way it can with mortar-bedded courses.
- **Ordinary construction/mounting foam — explicitly, categorically wrong for structural block bonding, despite being visually similar to the specialized polyurethane block adhesive above and sometimes mistaken for it.** The critical difference, per the source: ordinary mounting foam has real secondary expansion after application; specialized block adhesive does not. In the destructive test, foam-glued joints held up well under an isolated break test, but the source demonstrates separately that even mild secondary expansion pushes/shifts blocks out of alignment on a real wall, distorting the course's geometry and requiring significantly more plastering afterward to correct the resulting unevenness. **Never substitute ordinary construction foam for a purpose-built block adhesive**, regardless of apparent bond strength in isolation.
- **Zemskov's explicit meta-point on why the video's own destructive break-testing (breaking joints after 3-4 hours and again after 24 hours) is largely beside the point for real-world material selection**: a finished, standing wall is never subjected to the kind of pulling/twisting/impact force applied in the test, and every candidate material's true strength only fully develops after ~28 days regardless of which one is used — so comparing joint-breaking force at 3-4 hours or even 24 hours doesn't predict long-term wall performance. **The three criteria Zemskov states actually matter for choosing a block-bonding material**: (1) speed of construction; (2) cost — specifically avoiding paying for capability the application doesn't need (echoing the tile-adhesive-overpay point above); (3) whether the next course can be built without waiting for the current course to reach its full design strength.

## Numeric Data

- Cement-sand joint thickness: 10mm horizontal / 12mm vertical minimum (first course only).
- Tile adhesive vs. specialized mineral block adhesive: ~50% cost premium for tile adhesive, negligible on one small wall, "tens of thousands of RUB" at full-project scale.
- Specialized mineral block adhesive: working temperature range -10°C to +35°C.
- Polyurethane block ("foam-glue") adhesive: initial strength 30 minutes, full strength 24 hours (vs. ~3 days/28 days for mixed mortars/adhesives); coverage 70-100 linear meters per canister, up to 20m² of 100mm-thick masonry per canister.
- Mixing rest period for cement-sand and Rotband-type mixes: 5 minutes before re-mixing and use; skipping this costs 20-30% of final strength, per the guest technologist.
