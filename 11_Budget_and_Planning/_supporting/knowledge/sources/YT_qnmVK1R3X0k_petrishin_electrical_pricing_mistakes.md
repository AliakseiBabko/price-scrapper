## Rules / Heuristics — New Mechanisms

1. **Project/smeta cross-review before work starts, explicit reasoning**:
   the foreman and an experienced electrician jointly review the design
   project and cost estimate in detail specifically to catch designer/
   estimator errors on paper — reworking a wrong outlet/switch position
   or a wrong circuit line after installation is far slower and costlier
   than correcting the same error while it's still just drawings.
2. **Panel-size decision framework, by client need and budget**: absent
   a formal single-line electrical design, the foreman + electrician
   assess client needs directly and propose a panel size (cited
   examples: a compact 12-breaker panel, or 36/48/72-breaker panels)
   plus whether a smart-home system is wanted — a structured "how big a
   panel do you actually need" conversation rather than a fixed default.
3. **Wire-end labeling as a serviceability requirement, with an explicit
   hiring-dependency red flag**: every wire end should be marked
   (marker directly on the wire, or a written tag) with its destination
   room/device — this materially eases later panel assembly and
   junction-box wiring. **If you see unlabeled wires during or after an
   electrician's work, treat it as a red flag either way**: it means
   either the electrician is unskilled, *or* — more insidiously — a
   skilled electrician deliberately skipped labeling to make the client
   dependent on him personally for any future work. **Concrete cost
   consequence**: if you later need to bring in a different electrician
   on unlabeled work, expect to pay a large sum, since every connection
   has to be traced and re-tested from scratch rather than read directly
   off labels. A genuinely new contractor-vetting/hiring-risk mechanism.
4. **Live-circuit-during-drywall-install damage-detection mechanism**:
   after rough electrical is installed, temporary outlets/switches/light
   sockets are connected and functionally tested (plugging in a real
   load — a hammer drill or phone charger). Beyond confirming the
   network works, **keeping the panel connected and circuits live
   through subsequent trades' work (e.g. drywall installers screwing
   metal profile track to the walls) means a screw that accidentally
   hits a live wire trips the breaker immediately** — alerting that
   trade on the spot that they need to open and fix that spot before
   continuing. **If the panel is left de-energized instead, the same
   screw-through-wire damage goes completely undetected until the panel
   is later connected** — by then it's far more expensive and difficult
   to trace and repair. A genuinely new mechanism, distinct from this
   project's existing (corroborated, not re-extracted) basic temp-outlet
   functional test.
5. **Ceiling priming before cable-clip installation, a client-consent
   cost item**: on most of this company's own objects, the ceiling is
   treated with a priming compound before cable clips are affixed for
   ceiling-routed trace lines — done with the client's explicit
   agreement (implying it's a chargeable, opt-in step, not a silent
   default).

## Cost Drivers — A Structured 4-Factor Framework

6. **Explicit 4-factor cost-driver framework for electrical rough-in**,
   offered directly by the source as the complete list: (1) density of
   outlets/switches; (2) wall material — a reinforced-concrete panel
   wall costs more to route/chase than aerated/gas-block; (3) panel
   size — a bigger panel costs more labor to assemble; (4) aesthetics —
   perfectly straight, measured, chase-hidden trace routing and a
   painted/primed ceiling all cost extra time (and therefore money)
   without changing the electrical system's actual performance.
7. **Explicit performance-vs-aesthetics admission, worth quoting for
   client-expectation calibration**: neat, straight, hidden-back-box
   cable routing does **not** improve "electron speed" or any functional
   network parameter — the system would work identically routed
   diagonally, unmeasured, unprimed. The extra cost buys a purely
   cosmetic/serviceability benefit (easier future troubleshooting, a
   tidier look) — the source states this candidly rather than presenting
   the aesthetic work as a technical necessity. A useful line item for
   distinguishing "must-pay" from "nice-to-have" spend on an electrical
   quote.
