## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules (safety-relevant)

- **Real discovered defect**: a brick parapet's entire structural integrity turned out to depend on the old aluminum glazing frame itself, plus a handful of embedded metal brackets — already-paid-for, already-delivered new glazing installation had to be paused mid-demolition once the crew realized removing the old glazing would cause the brick parapet to fall off the building. **Diagnosis method**: a push/kick test showing visible wobble, especially severe at one corner that was barely holding together.
- **Why the embedded metal brackets couldn't simply stay in place**: they form a solid, unbroken metal path to the building's exterior — a direct thermal bridge. Installing warm glazing on a base still cold-bridged this way, per Zemskov, guarantees ice formation at that point and defeats the entire purpose of warm glazing — the brackets had to be removed, but removing them (before reinforcing the masonry another way) would have dropped the parapet immediately.
- **A second builder defect found in the same inspection**: the parapet's masonry didn't even reach the adjoining walls on either side — genuine gaps on both left and right, held together only by the same failing metal brackets. Zemskov's own framing: entirely avoidable with correctly executed original construction.
- **Zemskov's repair technique: fabricated welded steel brackets bridging the parapet to the adjoining structure at three points** — a Z-shaped bent bracket for one section (two fixing points, a bend, two more fixing points), and paired angled corner brackets (two per corner) for the corner. **Metal thickness was determined on-site by physically testing rigidity** at a hardware market, not decided purely in advance.
- **Standardization decision, worth noting as a general practical heuristic**: although a simple two-point bracket location would only need 4mm steel, and 6mm would be excessive, Zemskov used **5mm steel everywhere** rather than juggle two different material thicknesses on the same small job — reasoning that the flexing Z-shaped bracket and the large-open-span corner genuinely needed the full 5mm, and standardizing saved fabrication complexity elsewhere. All pieces were also standardized to 900mm length for the same reason ("easier to remember one number than several").
- **Fixing-point-count rule**: exactly 2 fixing points per bracket segment on each side — **not 3, not 6** — stated as both the practical minimum and the practical maximum; more points don't meaningfully add strength.
- **Fabrication technique**: steel strips can't simply be cold-bent to the needed angle without cracking/weakening — instead, notch/score the strip and weld it into the bent shape. **Weld QC**: align both segments so they're co-planar/parallel before welding, then hammer-test the weld afterward to confirm it holds under a firm strike.
- **Real material cost cited**: ~2,300 RUB for the steel strip stock, plus a separate batch of compression-type anchors.
- **Corrosion-protection rule, stated as mandatory, not optional**: any bare/unfinished ("black") metal that will be plastered over must always be painted, two coats — **even though the underlying structural risk is already resolved by that point** (the window frame itself will end up bearing the parapet's load once installed), because unpainted 5mm steel will eventually rust away to nothing over the renovation's intended lifespan. **Paint application technique**: apply first coat, wait until it's dry enough that a gloved finger no longer sticks, then apply the second coat; **hold the spray can vertically or only slightly angled — never sideways/horizontal**, which sprays mostly propellant rather than an even paint layer (an explicit warning against a real bad habit Zemskov has seen from other installers).
- **Anchor selection — flagged as "the single most important thing in this whole job"**: use only **compression-type ("прижимающие") anchors**, never standard window-frame anchors, which have a completely different head/cap design meant to seat against a frame's own face, not to clamp two masonry-facing plates tightly together. **Mixing up the two anchor types can undo everything else done correctly in this repair.**
- **Depth-control technique for drilling anchor holes without going all the way through the masonry**: rather than relying on tape-marking the drill bit (a common but error-prone trick), **use an anchor intentionally shorter than the masonry's own depth**, so its thread length itself naturally limits how far drilling needs to go — eliminates the risk of a slipped tape mark.
- **Hole-placement rule in brick**: pre-planned fixing points must never land on a mortar joint — check both horizontal *and* vertical joints; shift a point sideways if it would land on or too near one. **General principle**: space two fixing points on the same bracket as far apart as practical for better leverage resistance, but if one specific spot can't get solid purchase, it's acceptable to move both points closer together instead of leaving one point unreliable.
- **Drilling technique in brick specifically**: drill carefully, don't wiggle the rotary hammer side-to-side — make several careful passes to slightly widen the hole instead, aiming for a snug fit where the anchor goes in with a slight "whistle." Real material variability should be expected (the crew hit embedded rebar unexpectedly mid-hole and had to angle off and redrill nearby rather than force through it).
- **Anchor-tightening QC check**: after tightening, try to turn the anchor by hand — **a properly-seated anchor will not spin freely**; if it does, the installation has failed regardless of how tight it visually looks. **Over-tightening warning**: never tighten to the point of stripping/spinning the anchor in its hole — a stripped hole can't be reused, requiring an entirely new hole elsewhere; if a bracket already has other good anchor points set and needs one more new hole, the whole bracket must be removed first before drilling nearby, since drilling through already-installed metal risks dulling the bit and, if it contacts brick underneath, further damaging the masonry.
- **Technique for closing a slightly out-of-square welded joint**: fully tighten one side of the bracket first, then use the other side as a lever — one person holds firm, constant pressure while a second person pulls/adjusts the joint into a tight seat. Demonstrated as difficult but achievable with two people working together, not a one-person task.
