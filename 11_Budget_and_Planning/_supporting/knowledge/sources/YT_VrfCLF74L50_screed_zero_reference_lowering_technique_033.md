## Rooms / Zones — Flooring / Materials / Quantities / Cost Drivers

- **"Find the highest point, establish a zero reference" laser-leveling
  method for screed** (a general version of the specific 995mm-template
  technique already recorded in this store for self-leveling floor, from
  the same channel's `yt_awsVIxZlskQ`): place a self-leveling laser at
  the room's rough center on its **first placement in the apartment at
  any height** (the absolute height doesn't matter — a virtual reference
  plane is what's needed); mark that plane with a pen on every wall in
  every room the laser reaches from that one position. For every
  subsequent room the laser can't directly reach, re-position it, level
  to one of the already-marked points, then mark the new room's walls
  from that same plane — propagating one single reference plane through
  the whole apartment. **Use a leveling tripod with an adjustable
  vertical rod/crank, not a plain tripod** — fine height tuning by leg
  length alone is described as impractically difficult.
- **"Zero mark" terminology, explicitly disambiguated from the unrelated
  construction-industry term of the same name**: within this technique,
  "zero" (`0`) is defined as the apartment's single **highest** raw-floor
  point (found by measuring down from the laser reference plane at many
  points — the smallest measured distance is the highest point); every
  other point is recorded relative to it as a negative offset (e.g. "-7"
  = 7cm lower than the highest point).
- **Real statistical claim: average raw-floor height variation across
  100 sampled new-build apartments is ~7cm for a ~100m² unit**, with
  observed extremes from 3cm up to 20cm in some units. `single-account`,
  `unverified` (no methodology/sample given beyond the claim itself), but
  a specific, checkable planning-sanity-check figure.
- **Minimum cement-sand screed thickness: 3cm — a hard floor, not a
  guideline** — thinner risks the screed crumbling/detaching from its
  base. The screed must cover the zero-reference point (the apartment's
  highest raw-floor spot) by at least this 3cm minimum; every other point
  gets screed thickness = 3cm + however far below zero that point sits.
- **Screed-quantity/cost formula, worked example**: average thickness =
  (min layer + max layer) / 2. One 25kg cement-sand-mix ("пескобетон")
  bag covers **~1m² at ~2cm thickness**, so roughly **3 bags per m² at
  6.5cm average thickness**; a 100m² apartment at this average needs
  **~320 bags** (~13 tonnes, ≈8.5 "Gazelle" van-loads for context). **2019
  Moscow prices**: ~115 RUB/bag → ~37,000 RUB material for 320 bags;
  delivery ~4,000-10,000 RUB depending on distance within Moscow/oblast;
  carrying bags up to the floor (no elevator/freight assumed) ~1,500
  RUB/tonne → ~19,500 RUB for 13 tonnes. **Total worked estimate: ~65,000
  RUB** for this example apartment's raw screed materials+delivery+
  carry-up alone (excludes labor to actually lay it). `single-account`,
  `unverified` pricing (stated as the practitioner's own current rates,
  no invoice shown), but concrete and checkable, and — per this project's
  price-comparability rule — carries both location (Moscow) and year
  (2019, upload-date-confirmed) explicitly.
- **Entrance-threshold level-matching planning rule**: the finished floor
  level at the apartment's entrance must match the building corridor/
  landing floor level — a client should never have to step up or down
  entering their own apartment. If the corridor floor was raised
  significantly above the apartment's raw floor (a specific defect
  pattern named: up to ~15cm in some new-builds), screed is *not* the
  tool to close that gap economically — use expanded-clay
  (керамзит)/lightweight fill mixed in instead of pure cement-sand screed
  until the level reaches the future entrance door's base. Most units
  only see a 2-4cm gap here, where the cost-saving technique below
  applies instead.
- **The core technique: deliberately "lower the zero reference" by
  2cm** (worked example) to cut material cost, when the real, un-lowered
  zero point isn't load-bearing for anything at the entrance threshold:
  redefine the reference so the true highest point becomes "+2" instead
  of "0"; screed is only poured down from the *new*, lower "-3" datum
  (still respecting the 3cm minimum) to the floor's lowest point; the
  small remaining wedge between the old and new zero (0 to +2 in the
  worked example) is left as bare, sloped raw subfloor, **entirely
  covered later by self-leveling compound instead of cement-sand
  screed**, which is thin enough (a few mm) to not need the 3cm minimum.
  **Stated visual-perception threshold: a 2cm level difference across
  more than half a room's floor is not visually detectable** once
  finished flooring is down — justifying why this "cheat" reads as a
  perfectly flat floor in the real completed project shown. **Real
  project case (140m² apartment)**: raw-floor variation ranged 0 (kid's
  room, highest point) to -8/-9cm (kitchen, lowest point), with the
  entrance-door area at roughly -4.5cm from the highest point. Applying a
  2cm zero-reference lowering there avoided a ~4.5cm threshold step at
  the entrance door that would otherwise have required trimming/exposed
  the flooring's cut edge in the corridor-facing sightline — while the
  building-corridor level stayed essentially unaffected (only a ~1cm
  tile+adhesive buildup on that side). **Cost claim for the 140m²
  project**: the 2cm zero-reference lowering alone saved ~140 bags
  (~6 tonnes, ~4 vans, ~30,000 RUB).
- **Cost-saving claim for the 100m² virtual example**: saving 3cm of
  screed thickness across the whole apartment (by shifting excess volume
  onto thinner, pricier self-leveling compound instead) saves ~150 bags
  (~6 tonnes, ~4 vans, ~30,000-35,000 RUB in cement-sand mix cost) against
  a ~10,000 RUB extra spend on the more expensive self-leveling compound
  needed to finish the thin top layer — net savings claimed, plus reduced
  ceiling-height loss (not further quantified).
- **Screed vs. self-leveling compound, three distinguishing
  characteristics stated**: (1) screed needs beacons/manual leveling by a
  worker; self-leveling compound self-levels by its own fluidity/gravity
  (mechanism explained via analogy: soup in a bowl, bathwater, ice on a
  still pond — genuinely explanatory, not just asserted); (2) self-
  leveling compound is a finer, higher-strength mix that resists cracking
  even at very thin layers; screed cannot reliably go below the 3cm
  minimum without failing; (3) self-leveling compound is significantly
  more expensive per unit thickness — impractical to use for a full 10cm
  fill, but ideal for a thin (few-mm) finishing/leveling pass, including
  as a thin topping layer over *any* cement-sand screed (even a very
  well-executed one) to eliminate minor surface irregularities before
  flooring goes down.
- **Electrical-conduit-routing rationale, extending this store's existing
  "route conduit on the ceiling, not the floor" rule**: floor-routed
  modern low-voltage/data cable trunking bundles are commonly ≥5cm thick
  and not load-bearing like a slab — screed poured directly over such a
  bundle at only 3cm will crack "like an eggshell." **Concrete numeric
  consequence, tied directly to the zero-reference technique above**:
  a floor-routed cable bundle forces a local minimum screed buildup of
  5cm (bundle) + 5cm (screed cover) = 10cm at that spot, which single-
  handedly can turn a 2cm entrance-threshold gap into a 10cm gap — using
  the 140m² case's own numbers, a floor-routed bundle at the entrance
  would have meant an 8cm-taller flooring buildup there than the
  corridor side, a serious defect. **Cost delta claim for the 140m²
  project**: routing electrical on the floor instead of the ceiling
  there would cost an estimated extra ~560 bags (~22.5 tonnes, ~15 vans,
  ~120,000 RUB) in avoidable screed volume alone, before counting the
  resulting threshold/step defect.
