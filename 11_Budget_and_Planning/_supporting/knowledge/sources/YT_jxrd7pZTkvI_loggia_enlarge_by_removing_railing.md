## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's warning against a "cold cantilever extension" (вынос)** for anyone gaining space with future insulation/joining in mind: a вынос can be added to almost any loggia, but only accepts lightweight cold sliding glazing, and its junctions to the original structure can never be properly insulated even if a warm frame is somehow fitted on the extension itself — he calls choosing this method purely to "win space" while planning eventual insulation the single dumbest approach available.
- **Zemskov's alternative technique: build the support masonry *in place of* the old metal railing, not alongside it** — reclaims real usable floor width that would otherwise be lost to both the railing's footprint and the masonry built next to it. **Worked example: up to ~15cm on a typical 6m loggia, equivalent to roughly 1m² of usable floor area** — a meaningful gain for a loggia. Explicitly not viable on every loggia, only ones where the old railing's removal is structurally/practically sound.
- **Zemskov's answers to three common objections to this technique**:
  1. **Won't the slab collapse under the masonry's weight?** No — worked numbers: 6m of masonry needs 50 blocks at 7.2kg each ≈ 400kg total including adhesive; spread over a 6m² loggia that's ~66kg/m², against a rated capacity of ~800kg/m² even for a basic slab — Zemskov states the slab would be fine even with a much heavier material like brick built floor-to-ceiling.
  2. **Is this a facade modification requiring formal approval?** Yes, unambiguously — Zemskov states that glazing an originally-open loggia, cladding the old parapet in siding, or even just hanging an AC unit **all** count as facade modifications requiring approval, and that in practice "everyone except the most basic Moscow new-builds" ignores this.
  3. **Is removing the parapet dangerous?** Genuinely, yes — **unlike the rest of this technique, which can be DIY, parapet removal must be done by professionals working with fall-arrest harnesses**, not attempted by a homeowner.
- **Zemskov's demolition procedure**: cut the old railing into **at least 4 pieces of ~1.5m each** so they can be carried out through the building's front door — never remove it as one whole panel, and **never drop pieces (whole or cut) off the building**, which he calls categorically forbidden.
- **Base-prep after demolition**: remove all old screed, old galvanized flashing, and waterproofing — with particular attention to the old railing's mounting feet and the precast slab's own **crane lifting lugs** (embedded during original building construction) and any other protrusions. Low spots are fine (filled with adhesive later); protrusions must be removed, or blocks will rock and eventually fail.
- **Block-laying follows `yt_cRRwnezXMcw`'s general technique, with two thickness-specific corrections**: **never carve an internal reinforcement channel for embedded rebar in a 100mm-thick block** — internal reinforcement is only valid starting at 200mm block thickness; on a 100mm block, carving a channel leaves the remaining wall material too thin to hold anything (only the external tensioned-strap technique from the companion video applies at 100mm). **Joint tuck-pointing is strictly mandatory for this specific thin-joint support masonry**, unlike an ordinary ≥300mm structural wall (where excess squeezed adhesive can just be scraped off) — because a 100mm-thick support masonry course rests on both an uneven slab *and* uneven side walls (unlike normal construction, which sets flat blocks on a flat foundation), so blocks can shift slightly during placement, constantly creating small through-voids ("свищи") that must be filled.
- **Exterior protection and the masonry/siding installation sequence**: blocks must never be left exposed outdoors — protected with vinyl siding, a lap-siding system that clips upward course by course starting from a bottom starter strip. **Because siding installs bottom-up and clips onto the row below, the full masonry cannot be built first and then sided afterward** — the lowest starter strip becomes unreachable. **Correct sequence: alternate — one masonry course, one siding course, one masonry course, one siding course**, and so on. A practical trick: leaving the very topmost sliver of the old railing in place temporarily during demolition/construction reduces vibration/flex during the subsequent work.
- **Loggia vs. balcony siding-alignment difference**: on a balcony, siding aligns flush with the slab's own bottom edge, fully enclosing it. **On a loggia, the slab stays exposed at the bottom — the siding's starter profile fixes into the bottom of the block masonry itself, overhanging the blocks by only ~2cm**, not into the slab.
- **Siding sheet length and seam-staggering rule**: sheets run ~3.6m, so a 6m loggia will always have a lengthwise seam — **stagger seams between alternating courses so odd- and even-row seams land at opposite ends of the loggia**, reading as one continuous surface rather than a repeated, obviously-aligned seam line. Siding attaches directly to the masonry (no separate furring/guide rails) with 35-40mm wood screws.
- **Top junction (siding-to-frame) sequencing rule — flagged as the most common mistake**: cutting the top siding row to fit *before* the frame is installed, only to discover after installation a gap with spray foam visibly poking through. **Correct method**: install the top siding row (initially uncut) only *after* the plastic frame is fixed in place, but *before* the drip-cap/flashing is installed and before the foam joint is formed — at that stage the drip-cap's exact final position is already known, letting the last siding row be trimmed precisely to fit around it, rather than guessed at in advance.
