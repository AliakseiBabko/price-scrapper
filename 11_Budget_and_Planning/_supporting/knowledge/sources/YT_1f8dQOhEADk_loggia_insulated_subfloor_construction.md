## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's height-planning rule — don't reference the existing developer sill/threshold height** (it can be trivially knocked down or built up) — **the real constraint is minimum subfloor thickness for adequate insulation**: 7cm absolute minimum, 10cm better, 15cm ideal — thicker is warmer, but Zemskov explicitly warns against over-optimizing for warmth alone (a 30cm raise would be very warm but forces a 30cm step at the doorway, drops usable ceiling height to ~2.4m — reachable by hand — and lowers the effective railing height enough to become a real fall risk).
- **Zemskov's three hard constraints on how high the subfloor may be raised**: (1) step height at the doorway ≤20cm; (2) final loggia ceiling height ≥2.45m; (3) final railing/parapet height ≥95cm — **but ≥1.10m specifically if children aged 2-3 will use the loggia**, not the bare 95cm minimum.
- **A second, easy-to-miss reason to raise the subfloor ≥15cm**: baseboard height plus the width of the trim angle covering the panel-to-floor junction need enough clearance that the two elements don't visually overlap/crowd each other if the raised section is shorter than that.
- **Zemskov's worked real example**: 16cm raise, chosen because (a) it gives enough insulation depth for a loggia adjoining a children's room (an especially important adjacency to insulate well), (b) it keeps the final railing height safely at 1.08m (down from the original 1.25m pre-renovation), and (c) it accounts for a common mismatch between standard developer ceiling height (>2.80m) and cladding panels sold pre-cut at 2.70m — insufficient floor-raise forces ugly small offcuts to fill the gap at the panel's bottom edge.
- **Zemskov's framing lumber and cross-joist installation sequence**: 50×70mm timber; cut cross-joists (поперечины) to width by pushing stock against the parapet and cutting in place (no need for precision — they're set back from the wall regardless); space cross-joists 40-60cm apart; fix with 100-112mm frame anchors, **pre-drilling the timber to ~3/4 depth first** (drilling wood directly with a rotary hammer is slow/inefficient). **Fixing-point count**: 2 points suffices for most standard-width loggias; more only needed if the loggia exceeds 1.5m in that dimension; keep spacing between fixing points ≤80cm. **Concrete drill-hole depth should exceed the fastener's own length by ~3cm** — otherwise the anchor's head interferes and it can't be tightened properly. **Caveat explicitly flagged**: these anchor-length figures are for modern loggia slabs only — using the same 100-112mm anchor on an old Khrushchev-era balcony slab will drill straight through it.
- **Zemskov's anchor-tightening technique**: an anchor is not a clamping fastener that pulls the joist down on its own — **manually press the joist flat against the floor (knee or foot) while tightening**, don't rely on the anchor itself to seat it.
- **Zemskov's leveling-efficiency rule, flagged as the most common mistake at this stage**: don't try to individually level every cross-joist (6-7 elements) — cross-joists just get pulled snug to the (uneven) slab and screwed down; **only the 2-3 longitudinal joists (lags) actually need to be leveled.**
- **Zemskov's timber-selection rule**: use the crookedest available stock for cross-joists (their straightness doesn't matter), and save the straightest stock for the longitudinal lags (which do need to be straight/level).
- **Zemskov's longitudinal-joist spacing and positioning rule**: spacing ≤45cm; 3 lags suffice for most standard loggia widths, 4 only needed above ~1.2m — just shift the two outer lags inward from the wall rather than adding a 4th. **Center each lag along its own length rather than trying to reach both end walls with jointed pieces** (most modern loggias run just over 3m) — up to ~15cm of positioning slack (7.5cm per side) can be absorbed this way with the subfloor sheathing still safely supported, since the actual unsupported cantilever ends up around 2.5cm per side once ~5cm is covered by the wall-adjacent cladding.
- **Zemskov's leveling method for the longitudinal lags**: find the single highest point across all cross-joists with a precision level; level the **two outer lags independently** to that reference using leveling wedges/shims placed on **both sides** of the timber (single-sided shimming lets the joist rock); then use a long straightedge across the two already-leveled outer lags to bring any middle lag(s) into the same plane — faster and less error-prone than leveling middle-from-edge-from-middle sequentially (fewer measurements, less accumulated error). **Screw-length correction**: a 7cm-thick timber only needs a 7cm screw properly seated, not the commonly assumed 10cm+.
- **Zemskov's mineral-wool insulation rule — ignore the package's stated coverage claim.** He calls a manufacturer's typical "one roll per ~2.5m²" claim unreliable ("полная фигня") and recommends budgeting a full wool package per loggia without economizing. **Correct first-layer technique**: lay the base insulation layer snug against the floor with no gaps, cutting individual pieces around each cross-joist rather than trying to weave one continuous piece through them. **A common but wrong belief flagged explicitly**: skipping insulation at the section adjoining the "warm" apartment wall (reasoning: that wall itself is deliberately left uninsulated to preserve heat exchange with the apartment) — Zemskov's correction: the floor *below* that same section may belong to an uninsulated downstairs neighbor's loggia, so it still needs full insulation regardless of the adjoining wall's own treatment. Leftover wool afterward is layered in between the longitudinal joists as a bonus second pass, though the single full-coverage layer alone is already sufficient.
- **Zemskov explicitly recommends against spray-foam-sealing the insulation-to-wall joints** — his reasoning: the insulation contour is meant to be warm but air-*permeable* (an under-panel-cladding cavity is deliberately ventilated), not airtight; foam-sealing this joint wastes material and time for zero benefit, analogous to not needing to tape the edges of a thick blanket for it to keep you warm.
- **Zemskov's subfloor sheathing rule: use chipboard/particleboard (ДСП), not plywood or solid boards, and explains why.** Solid boards start squeaking within about a year under humidity/temperature swings; plywood delaminates and starts squeaking within roughly 3 years; chipboard's only real failure mode (linear expansion) requires the panel to be literally soaked/flooded, far beyond normal loggia humidity swings — the most durable of the three for this specific application. Screw with 41mm screws, marking joist centerlines on the wall in pencil first to avoid missing them once covered.
- **Zemskov's sheathing-installation cautions**: don't stand directly between joists (on unsupported sheet) while screwing it down — stand over a joist, or the sheet deforms before it's fixed. **Deflection QC check**: a correctly-supported subfloor sheet should deflect only 2-3mm under an 80kg load between joists. **Threshold detail**: notch a relief cut into the sheet nearest the doorway, extending to the adjacent joist's centerline, so the separate threshold ramp piece (also chipboard) can share support from that same joist rather than needing its own dedicated backing block — saves material/time and avoids a plane mismatch that would otherwise telegraph through the finished flooring if the separate backing were off by even 1mm. **Leave 2-3mm gaps between sheets (and at walls)** — too small for flooring to fall through, but sheets butted perfectly tight will rub and audibly squeak underfoot.
- **Zemskov explicitly recommends against treating the framing lumber with any wood preservative** — his stated reasoning: even untreated, dry, quality lumber lasts 30+ years in this application, which is more than sufficient, regardless of any preservative manufacturer's safety claims.
- **Zemskov's final sealing step — seal the subfloor's own perimeter contour with spray foam, for a distinct reason from the insulation-contour point above.** The cavity under the panel cladding is directly ventilated (air exchange with the room via the convection grilles) — an unsealed subfloor perimeter lets foot-traffic-disturbed mineral-wool fibers rise into that cavity and exit through the grilles into the room, meaning the occupant ends up breathing wool fiber and itching. Sealing the subfloor perimeter (distinct from the insulation-to-wall joint, which is deliberately left unsealed) prevents this specific migration path.
