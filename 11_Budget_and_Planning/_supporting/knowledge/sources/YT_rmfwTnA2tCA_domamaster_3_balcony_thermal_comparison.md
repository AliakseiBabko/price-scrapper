# Source Note — "Domamaster.com", real pyrometer-measured comparison of 3 loggias at -10°C outside

**Genuinely rare source type for this page**: a company visits one real
3-room apartment with **three loggias finished to three different
insulation/heating levels**, and takes actual pyrometer (surface
temperature) readings on each, in the same cold-weather conditions
(outside air ≈ -10°C). This is real comparative quantitative data, not
a single anecdote — three real-world data points on exactly the
question this project's own plan cares about (does insulation +
glazing, without active heating, actually stay warm/frost-free?).
Region unresolved (no city named). Low promotional ratio — almost
entirely a walkthrough of measured readings.

## ⚠️ Directly Relevant to This Project's Own Unheated-Plan Assumption

1. **Loggia #1 — unimproved glazing, ceiling/floor insulated only, heated
   floor**: original developer aluminum glazing left unchanged (for
   cost reasons), side walls **not** insulated, only floor+ceiling
   insulated (10cm XPS total), heated floor running. Measured: enclosure
   surface ≈1°C, ceiling only 6°C, floor warming toward 30-35°C after an
   hour, ambient air ≈9°C. **Company's own conclusion, directly relevant
   to a heating-vs-no-heating question**: even with a heated floor
   running, an unimproved aluminum glazing + uninsulated side walls
   create a "cold radiant background" that itself prevents the space
   from feeling comfortable — heating one surface (the floor) does not
   compensate for cold walls/glazing.
2. **Loggia #2 — walls/parapet insulated 70mm, glazing upgraded to PVC,
   NO active heat source at all**: this is the configuration closest to
   this project's own plan (insulation + new glazing, no permanent
   heating). Measured with the adjoining room's balcony door **open**
   (their own normal-use assumption): walls 15°C, parapet 10.5°C
   (cooler — adjacent to an unheated corridor), ceiling 15°C, **floor
   only 2°C** (too cold to stand on barefoot), air ≈14°C. **⚠️ Explicit
   caveat this project should weigh directly**: the company states this
   warmth exists *only if the balcony door to the room stays open* — "if
   you keep it closed, warmth won't come from anywhere on the loggia and
   the temperature here will be quite low." **This project's own plan
   keeps the loggia door closed by default** (see
   [[10_Balcony/Balcony_Index|Balcony_Index]]'s Family Needs section) —
   this source's own data suggests that configuration, absent an open
   door or any heat source, would run meaningfully colder than the 14°C
   air/15°C wall readings shown here, since those readings depend on
   borrowed room heat through an open door, not on insulation alone.
   **This is a real risk data point for this project's "frost-free
   without heating" goal, not a direct contradiction (the goal is
   frost-free, not comfortable, and this data doesn't test a closed-door
   no-heat scenario directly) — flagged for the household's own
   judgment, not resolved here.**
3. **Loggia #3 — 100-120mm insulation, upgraded glazing, active heated
   floor + in-floor electric convectors**: not applicable to this
   project's own plan (active heating explicitly excluded), but included
   for contrast — with active heating running, parapet 28°C, walls
   26-29°C, ceiling 29°C, floor 32°C, convector body 45°C, air 29°C, and
   even the glazing itself (the coldest surface) held at 17-18°C. Company
   conclusion: this setup "can survive any harsh winter." Confirms that
   reaching genuine winter comfort on a loggia requires an active heat
   source — insulation and glazing alone (loggia #2) do not get there,
   even at a comparable insulation thickness.

## Balcony / Loggia — New Numeric Data / Technique

4. **Insulation-thickness recommendation, sharper than this page's
   existing rule**: the company states 70mm total wall insulation
   (loggia #2's own spec) is *not enough* for the warmest possible
   loggia — recommends **two full 50mm layers (100mm total)** instead,
   consistent with the general 5cm/2-layer stacking rule already on this
   page but stating a specific "not enough at 70mm" threshold not
   previously recorded here.
5. **New parapet-upgrade recommendation for an existing concrete
   parapet**: when new glazing must be mounted onto an **existing
   concrete parapet** (not rebuilt), the company recommends building a
   *new* gas-block parapet in front of/on top of it with a 5-10cm
   insulation break between the old concrete and new block parapet, then
   mounting the new glazing on the new block parapet rather than the old
   concrete one — moves the glazing's fixing point into the "warm zone"
   and eliminates a windowsill-area freezing zone, at the cost of a
   narrower windowsill. A specific retrofit variant of Zemskov's general
   support-masonry-course rule, for the case where an old concrete
   parapet is being kept rather than demolished.
6. **In-floor electric convector detail**: on loggia #3, in-floor electric
   convectors (embedded in the floor along the glazing line) are used
   specifically to cut the cold-air draft/downdraft coming off the
   glazing, rather than as the main heat source — the heated floor still
   handles general floor warmth. A specific technique for handling
   glazing-adjacent cold air, not previously documented on this page.
7. **Facing-brick lamination detail**: the PVC glazing used on loggias #2
   and #3 has an exterior lamination finish specifically to preserve the
   building facade's visual appearance — a real reason (not just cost)
   a developer/HOA-facing project might specify laminated PVC rather than
   plain white profile.
8. **Cheap partial-upgrade option for an unchanged aluminum-glazed
   balcony** (loggia #1's own improvement suggestion): rather than a full
   glazing replacement, insert a break/thermal-separation block between
   the window and the aluminum frame, add insulation there, install a
   PVC unit into that block, and fabricate matching drip-caps for the
   new profile — a cheaper partial fix than full facade-glazing
   replacement, though the company notes facade glazing replacement has
   its own complications (likely HOA/facade-integrity approval, though
   not stated explicitly).
