## Design Concept — New Facts

- **⚠️ Overall practitioner framework, the most durable/reusable item in
  this video**: choose color starting from (1) general color-psychology
  associations, then (2) cultural/regional associations specific to the
  client's own country/culture (worked example: white is a wedding/
  purity color in Russia/CIS but a mourning color in India), then (3) the
  client's own personal history/associations with a color (a worked
  example: a personal trauma or a disliked ex-boss's signature color can
  override any "objective" color-theory argument for that color) — in
  that priority order. **Itten's color wheel** (Иттен) is named as the
  standard practical tool for checking which specific hues pair
  harmoniously, rather than combining colors by unstructured personal
  taste.
- **Two personality-driven color-appetite patterns, independent of any
  specific color**: some people (the source associates this with
  "intellectual professions") deliberately choose muted/desaturated
  ("chromatophobic") colors specifically to signal seriousness/
  intelligence, not from genuine visual preference — flagged as
  sometimes actually fear-of-color rather than considered preference,
  worth surfacing in a trust-based client/designer conversation rather
  than assumed as a fixed preference. Conversely, some people
  (self-described "creative" personalities) actively dislike muted
  environments and prefer living "inside color."
- **White**: visually enlarges small rooms, doesn't date stylistically,
  reads clean/festive but can feel cold/lifeless/institutional in a full
  apartment context and can measurably cool down family interaction in
  the source's framing; for a person living alone, white is described as
  potentially counteracting depression/loneliness feelings. **Practical
  mitigation**: wood accents, textured textiles, and decor objects break
  up the "pressure" of an all-white room, letting white function as a
  backdrop rather than the dominant presence.
- **Pastels**: broadly non-irritating, good as a background tone —
  practical caveat: pastel surfaces show stains/marks very visibly, a
  real consideration for households with kids/pets.
- **Beige**: calming, softening, sleep-promoting, but a beige-monochrome
  room risks feeling stuffy/stifling — recommend cutting a beige palette
  with cooler-toned elements (violates, deliberately, the general
  warm-only/cool-only-per-room convention this source otherwise
  endorses). Explicit personality framing: calms an aggressive/energetic
  person; can read as "boring/stable" to someone already high-energy;
  associated (per the source's own framing, not clinical) with fear of
  stepping outside one's comfort zone and reduced future-orientation —
  offered as a reason to reconsider an all-beige room if someone is
  actively trying to make a life change.
- **Acid/highly saturated colors**: described as genuinely energizing —
  used in gyms, kids' rooms, food courts by design convention, per the
  source. A specific caution: acid colors can reportedly cause literal
  discomfort for some psychiatric conditions per (unnamed) psychologists
  the source cites — `unverified`, single-hearsay claim.
- **Red**: associated with passion/urgency/appetite stimulation and
  short-term motivation/energy boost — explicitly analogized to an
  energy drink "high" followed by a comedown, so recommended for a
  time-boxed task zone (a focused work corner) rather than an entire
  room meant for sustained occupancy.
- **Light blue**: calming (cited as why kindergartens/clinics use it),
  suited to a bedroom in a light shade — but flagged with a genuinely
  interesting reasoning chain: overly blue interiors are associated in
  this source's framing with status-comparison behavior/snobbery
  ("голубая кровь"/"blue blood" association), potentially reinforcing a
  desire to have one's home judged/admired by visitors — worth being
  aware of as a possible unconscious motivation, not a design defect
  itself.
- **Yellow**: a stimulant (grouped with red/orange), suited to living
  room/kitchen more than bedroom; softer variants (mustard, light
  yellow) are less overstimulating than pure bright yellow. Flagged as
  potentially worsening mood for someone with low self-esteem (source's
  own framing, `unverified`) — in reasonable amounts, described as
  boosting alertness/concentration.
- **Brown**: associated (per the source, referencing Soviet-era interior
  norms) with hoarding/attachment-to-possessions tendencies and a
  psychological association with material stability/control; can read
  as visually cluttering in a genuinely small room; recommended
  specifically for someone seeking calm/stability after a chaotic period,
  discouraged for someone already prone to over-accumulating possessions
  or excessive conservatism. A stated (and explicitly caveated as
  `unverified`, "I don't put much stock in this") psychosomatic claim
  links brown to digestive-system issues — recorded only as the
  source's own aside, not adopted.
- **Orange**: stimulates appetite and a sense of urgency — flagged as a
  genuine caution for a kitchen specifically if weight/appetite
  management matters to the household (an actual functional tradeoff,
  not just aesthetic). Warm-toned rooms including orange can read
  psychologically "warmer" by a couple of degrees — offered as a
  potentially useful trick for a north-facing/cold-exposure room.
- **Green**: associated with nature/youth/wellness; cooler green
  tones are calming/non-provocative (suited to someone prone to
  anxiety); lowers perceived heart rate/pulse, described almost as a
  sedative, and can dull appetite — but overly dark green tones are
  flagged as depressive/apathy-inducing, and green carries a specific
  CIS-cultural association with envy/poison the source says is worth
  considering for a CIS-based household even though it isn't universal.
- **Dark/saturated blue**: visually "pulls inward," associated with
  empathy/family/motherhood in moderate use, but described as easy to
  visually "drown in" if overused, with a specific caution about
  eroding a less-confident person's sense of individuality. A claimed
  insomnia-fixing effect is explicitly flagged by the source as
  unverified personal-experience-only (he uses blackout curtains
  everywhere regardless of wall color and has not tested the claim).
- **Violet/purple**: associated with creativity, introspection,
  independence — suited to someone in a genuinely creative/meditative
  period of life; explicitly discouraged for someone whose current
  priority is expanding social reach/outward engagement, since the
  source frames violet as an inward-facing color.
- **Gray**: reads as formal/composed/orderly — suited to someone with
  (or wanting to build) self-discipline/objectivity; the source
  explicitly jokes about gray's "only facts, no distraction" reading
  being why diplomats dress in gray, offered as a light illustrative
  aside rather than literal design advice.
- **Pink**: culturally read as "girly"/frivolous by default, but framed
  as a genuine boldness signal when used deliberately, particularly a
  saturated pink; light pink is described as anxiety-reducing but can
  read as infantilizing if overused for someone already prone to that;
  offered as a potential confidence-balancing color for a naturally
  intense/dominant personality.
- **Black**: described as a "space-absorbing"/psychologically heavy
  color that can amplify subconscious anxieties with extended exposure
  — but also as genuinely effective for deliberately creating intimacy/
  coziness ("камерность") in an otherwise large room. Explicitly framed
  as carrying opposite cultural readings depending on context (elegance/
  luxury/status vs. mourning/fear/violence) — the source's own
  one-line summary: "black contains no love," offered as a personal
  observation, not a design rule.
