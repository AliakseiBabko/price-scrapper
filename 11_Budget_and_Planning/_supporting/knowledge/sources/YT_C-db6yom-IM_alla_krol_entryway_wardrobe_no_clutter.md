## Entrance / Furniture-Built-ins — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Wardrobe sizing/zoning should be derived from actual household
  composition** (couple + N children, or childless), not drawn as a
  generic "however much space is available" box — Krol frames this as a
  "Lego" approach: assemble the storage system to the specific client's
  needs, then size the visual corridor width around it.
- **Kids' zone placed first, at the entry, sized to their height** —
  build a dedicated low-height section for children right at the door so
  they don't track shoes/outerwear across the rest of the floor; the
  hanging rod for children's clothing should be the lowest rod in the
  first (family) section, with an adult rod above it, so the whole family
  splits up and hangs items immediately on entry rather than carrying
  them further into the home.
- **Reserve explicit guest-coat space inside the family's own first
  section** at design stage — a token "two hooks" allocation is
  insufficient once the household actually starts hanging its own
  overflow there instead; plan a real slot for guest outerwear rather
  than assuming it will stay free.
- **Pull-out shoe platforms inside the wardrobe's first section**
  (rather than a household leaving daily-worn shoes loose on the floor
  or in an open floor-level cubby) let shoes dry/air out contained and
  keep the corridor floor clear — Krol states this is more practical
  than the "gap under a bench, door stops 5cm above floor" version she
  used to build, which worked but looked worse visually.
- **Trumeau-mirror technique using the wardrobe's own door leaves**: when
  a wardrobe's opened door leaves meet at an angle (like a "✓" shape)
  rather than lying flat, position mirrors on the leaves so they form a
  side-angle pair around a straight mirror — the historic "trumeau"
  effect (a flat mirror flanked by angled side mirrors) that lets someone
  see their outfit from multiple angles without a separate full-length
  mirror elsewhere.
- **Plan the entry doormat at the design stage, not after move-in** — a
  recurring failure mode: a finished, styled entry photographs well, then
  a mismatched doormat gets added post-occupancy and visually undermines
  the whole space; choosing/sizing the mat as part of the original
  interior plan avoids this.
- **Design a hidden cutout for a robot vacuum's charging dock inside the
  wardrobe's base**, behind a door panel, so the vacuum can dock/charge
  without sitting exposed in the entryway — referenced her own published
  project (in L'Officiel Decoration) as a worked example of hiding a robot
  vacuum in an interior.
- **Flush cat-litter-tray wall cutout ("лаз")**: a wall opening finished
  flush in the same material/color as the surrounding wall so it doesn't
  visually stand out, routing a litter tray into a WC or a dedicated
  utility room rather than leaving it visible in the entry/corridor.
- **Storage-drawer sizing formula, per person**: at minimum **4 pull-out
  drawers per person** for order/organization (hats, mittens, gloves,
  caps, etc.), each **no taller than 150mm** (enough for roughly one to
  two stacked items) — explicitly framed as a floor, not a
  one-size-fits-all cap; also size the boot/coat hanging-rod row count
  (two rows of rods, or a "pantograph" double-rod lift mechanism that
  fits fewer items per row but doubles rod count) against how many long
  items (coats, winter items) the household actually owns, and split
  storage sections by child vs. adult and by gender rather than a single
  shared section.
- **Design-process critique of common wardrobe planning**: many wardrobes
  get designed backward from "how much wall space is free" (e.g. "we
  have 2.5m, put a rod at the top, a shelf in the middle, one drawer
  underneath") rather than forward from the household's actual stored-item
  inventory — Krol's studio instead builds a household "storage map" of
  every category of item that needs a home, matched to drawer/shelf/rod
  counts, before finalizing wardrobe dimensions.
- **Practical flooring material note**: porcelain stoneware ("керамогранит")
  preferred over quartz-vinyl for entryway flooring specifically for
  durability/practicality in this high-wear zone (personal preference
  stated as "not a quartz-vinyl fan," used elsewhere in projects but not
  favored for entries).
- **Narrow-corridor visual-widening technique**: since a corridor's walls
  usually can't be physically moved, enter through the widest available
  zone and widen it visually (mirrors, light tones), then divide a long
  corridor into a **minimum of three visual segments** so each reads as
  closer to a square proportion rather than one long uninterrupted tunnel
  — a mirrored "narrow point" segment can bridge two wider segments.
- **Stretch-ceiling-to-wardrobe integration detail**: when installing a
  stretch ceiling above a built-in wardrobe, add a filler/trim piece atop
  the wardrobe carcass so the ceiling's edge profile lands exactly on that
  trim, avoiding a visible gap/seam where wardrobe meets ceiling.
