## Furniture / Built-ins — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Cascade-partition core mechanism and why it's popular**: leaves slide
  and "park" stacked at one edge of the opening, freeing roughly
  **two-thirds to three-quarters of the opening width** for movement,
  while the leaves can still be drawn closed to fully divide the space.
  Framed as easy enough for a child to operate. This is the same "leaves
  stack, not all covering the opening at once" trade-off already recorded
  from UNION Doors Furniture's 2-3-leaf taxonomy, here from a fifth
  independent installer confirming the same mechanism with real project
  photos.
- **Top-track-only reasoning restated with a concrete wear/maintenance
  mechanism**: this installer deliberately never uses a bottom guide
  track — beyond the visual "floor doesn't read as divided" reasoning
  already recorded from other sources, this source adds a **stated
  maintenance reason**: a bottom track commonly accumulates dirt/debris
  over time, and the small wheels/rollers riding in it wear out and break
  as a result. Top-track-only leaves last longer, run more smoothly, and
  look better over time — a genuinely new *reason* (not just another
  restatement) for the same existing top-track-only convention.
- **Real Case 1 — kids' room zoning (~25m², frosted glass)**: a single
  window at one end of the room; frosted/matte glass used specifically so
  light still reaches the far half of the room when the leaves are closed,
  while the closed leaves still block a direct view into that half. 4-leaf
  cascade, one leaf stationary + three sliding leaves stacking behind it.
- **Real Case 2 — kitchen/dining-living zoning across two separate
  rooms**: a cascade partition (4 leaves) joining two rooms rather than
  dividing one. When open, leaves park neatly behind an adjoining cabinet
  (with a built-in fridge) without protruding past it — a real fitted-
  furniture integration detail. **Practical smell/sightline trade-off**:
  closing the partition while cooking contains cooking odors from
  spreading through the rest of the apartment, but since the glass stays
  transparent, the person in the kitchen can still see and monitor what's
  happening in the other room (e.g. watching children playing) even with
  the partition fully closed — a genuinely useful worked example of
  "closed for smell/sound containment, open-sightline for supervision" at
  once.
- **Pipe/utility-line boxing-in technique**: visible heating-system pipes
  or similar utility elements near a partition installation were boxed in
  with trim finished to match the partition's own color/style, so they
  read as an intentional part of the installation rather than an
  unaddressed utility run — a general finishing technique transferable
  beyond this specific installer.
- **"English lattice" (decorative grid molding) leaf pattern** — a named
  decorative option that reads well both in the open (stacked) and closed
  positions, distinct from the plain-leaf default.
- **Real Case 3 — bedroom/living-room zoning (~18-20m²), 3-leaf more
  common than 4-leaf per this source's own market observation**: one
  stationary leaf + two sliding clear-glass leaves. **⚠️ Integrated
  curtain-track detail**: a curtain track (holding a light blue tulle
  panel in this example) is built directly into the partition's own
  header/system, reading as one unified product rather than a separately-
  mounted rod — a real detail worth asking a manufacturer about directly
  if adjustable light/privacy control is wanted alongside the glass
  itself.
- **⚠️ Genuine cross-source disagreement — blackout curtain + sliding
  partition sound-isolation claim**: this source states that pairing the
  glass partition with a **blackout curtain** achieves sound isolation
  "equivalent to a 10cm drywall wall with sound-absorbing infill." This
  **directly contradicts (or at minimum substantially qualifies) the
  existing "essentially no soundproofing"/"purely visual, screen-
  equivalent" framing** already recorded on this page from dver1.ru and
  from Kruglov/Ontario (`HGN_3WeL0Jk`,
  [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/analysis/Bedroom_Design_Principles|Bedroom Design Principles]]).
  Both dver1.ru and this source agree curtains materially help beyond the
  glass alone — but this source's specific "10cm drywall-equivalent"
  figure is a much stronger claim than anything else recorded, comes from
  a single self-interested installer with no measurement method stated,
  and should be treated as `unverified`/`single-account`, not adopted as a
  reconciled number. Recorded as an open disagreement, not resolved either
  way — a real reason to independently test (or discount) before relying
  on it for the household's own Phase 2 plan.
- **Baseboard-to-leaf abutment options, a second independent source**:
  either notch/cut the baseboard where the leaf lands, or cut the notch
  into the leaf itself instead — the same two options (in the same
  order) already recorded from dver1.ru's four-option list on this page,
  now independently corroborated by a second installer.
- **Real Case 4 — 5-leaf cascade partition (~3.5m opening), living
  room-to-hallway/entry-passage zoning, frosted glass**: a real numeric
  stack-depth data point — **5 leaves in the fully-closed/stacked
  ("parked") position occupy roughly 25cm of total depth**, i.e. ≈5cm per
  leaf including gaps between leaves — a genuinely new planning figure for
  estimating how much wall/corner depth a multi-leaf stack needs when
  fully open. A sound-dampening gasket/weatherstrip between adjacent
  leaves is also used specifically for extra noise reduction (distinct
  from — and smaller-scale than — the disputed curtain-based claim above).
- **Real Case 5 — opaque/blind partition variant for a kids' room split
  (boy/girl zones, one long ~7m room)**: 3-leaf (one stationary, two
  sliding), using **tempered safety glass panels described as "seven
  times stronger than ordinary glass"** (the installer states he
  personally tried and failed to break a sample by hand several times,
  `single-account`/`unverified` claim, not a lab-tested figure) — offered
  as an option for households worried about a child breaking the glass.
  For households wanting zero light transmission at all, an **opaque MDF-
  insert variant** (blind panels instead of any glass) is also available.
  **⚠️ Sound-isolation comparison, stated directly**: the opaque MDF-insert
  variant provides noticeably better sound isolation than the transparent-
  glass variant — a real, checkable material-choice trade-off (light/
  visibility vs. acoustic performance) distinct from the disputed curtain
  claim above. An internal lock ("замок") between the two zones is also
  mentioned as an option, letting either side secure privacy independently.
