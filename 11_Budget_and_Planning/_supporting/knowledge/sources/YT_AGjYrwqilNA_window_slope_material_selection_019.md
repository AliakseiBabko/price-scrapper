## Source Metadata

A direct explainer answering "why not plastered window slopes," framing
window quality as a three-part product (unit + installation + slope
finish) and arguing slopes are the weakest, least standardized part of
the whole system. No pricing; closes with a genuine buying caution
(companies that only install windows, not slopes, can void warranty
claims later).

## Materials (Window Slopes)

- **Three slope types named and compared: insulated sandwich-panel,
  decorative (thin plastic, no insulation), and plastered.** Plastered
  slopes are stated as **never appropriate for a PVC window, under any
  circumstances** — this store's `yt_nb3L-k69yx8` already covers the
  sandwich-panel install technique in detail; this source adds the
  *why-never-plaster* reasoning and the decorative-slope exception cases
  neither prior source covered.
- **Why plastered slopes fail — three concrete, mechanism-based reasons**:
  1. **Thermal-expansion crack, structural mismatch**: PVC and plaster
     have different thermal-expansion coefficients, and no sealant exists
     that bonds well to both simultaneously — a crack reliably forms
     along the frame-to-plaster joint (and along the sill-to-plaster
     joint too) regardless of installer skill.
  2. **Plaster itself cracks over time even done well** — no installer
     can deliver a plastered slope guaranteed crack-free for even 5
     years, while the window unit itself is rated 10-20 years; paint
     over plaster also yellows (visible within ~1 year, pronounced by
     3-4 years) and glossy paint finishes peel/reflect poorly while matte
     finishes stain permanently.
  3. **No closing/retaining contour possible on a plastered slope** —
     without a proper closed perimeter profile, the cut edge of
     wallpaper terminating at the slope is always exposed and will
     eventually peel.
  - **A fourth, more technical reason**: to plaster a slope, the
    installation foam joint must be trimmed flush or slightly recessed
    relative to the frame — but the first (frame-to-wall) foam joint is
    already this store's known weakest point in a window installation
    and should always be maximized/thickened, never thinned — plastering
    forces exactly the wrong compromise on that joint.
- **Decorative (uninsulated, thin) plastic slopes — two legitimate,
  narrow use cases** where they're the *correct* choice specifically
  because insulation/soundproofing isn't the point: (1) a **temporary/
  non-structural partition wall** (worked example: a thin ~58mm stud-
  and-drywall partition inserted mid-opening to split one space into
  two) — no thermal/acoustic slope performance is needed for a wall this
  light, so a decorative slope is the right-sized solution, not an
  under-spec compromise; (2) the **exterior-facing slope of a balcony/
  loggia glazing block specifically** — the "quarter" (четверть) ledge a
  window frame normally seats against typically leaves only ~2-3cm of
  frame visible from outside, too little clearance to tuck an insulated
  sandwich panel behind the frame at that specific junction, so a
  decorative panel is butted and glued directly to the quarter instead —
  a structural clearance constraint, not a cost-cutting choice.
- **Five hard requirements for a correct insulated (sandwich-panel)
  slope, stated as a checklist**: (1) mechanically fixed securely to the
  opening (not just glued) so it can carry a thick foam joint behind it;
  (2) must be true sandwich-panel construction (foam core), never a
  hollow hollow-core plastic panel — hollow panels show visible internal
  reinforcing-rib lines through the surface and insulate far worse; (3)
  must have a closing/retaining perimeter contour (not just butted and
  puttied) both for appearance and to prevent the wallpaper's cut edge
  peeling; (4) that closing contour must be a **snap-together two-piece
  profile system (inner J-profile + outer profile), never glued** —
  glued corner trim eventually detaches from repeated impact (from a
  heavy sash swinging), where a snap-fit system flexes on impact and
  returns to position; the same snap system also lets an installer lift
  and re-tuck wallpaper edges cleanly during later touch-up/repainting;
  (5) both the panel and the profile hardware must be made from
  **primary (virgin) plastic, never secondary (recycled) plastic** —
  secondary plastic has unknown/potentially harmful composition and
  visibly yellows within a few years (a named real-world example: window
  sill end-caps, commonly made from secondary plastic, visibly yellow
  against a still-white sill within ~3 years).
- **Named common installer shortcut/defect, explicitly flagged as never
  acceptable**: skipping the closing/retaining contour entirely — butting
  the raw hand-cut sandwich-panel edge directly to the wall, puttying the
  gap, and wallpapering over it. **Three concrete failure mechanisms**:
  (a) hand-cut sandwich panel edges (cut from 3m×1.5m factory sheets with
  a knife, jigsaw, or circular saw) are visibly wavy/uneven once puttied
  and wallpapered — visible as an undulating line when viewed along the
  slope's own plane; (b) putty/wallpaper bonds far better to a wall
  surface than to a sandwich-panel's raw cut edge, so this joint reliably
  peels; (c) a sandwich panel is far softer/more flexible than plaster —
  demonstrated by hand-flexing — so a heavy sash's repeated impact at
  its two contact points (top/bottom, where the sealed glass unit is
  recessed relative to the frame) flexes the panel enough to crack
  puttied/wallpapered seams over time, a failure mode that wouldn't occur
  against a rigid plastered or properly-contoured surface.
- **Buying caution**: many companies install only the window unit itself
  and decline slope finishing, leaving the client to DIY it or hire a
  second firm — and if a problem later appears with the window itself,
  the original installer can (and reportedly does) deny warranty claims
  by blaming improperly-executed third-party slope work. **Recommendation:
  buy windows and slope finishing as one bundled product from one company
  offering one combined warranty**, not as separately-sourced work.
