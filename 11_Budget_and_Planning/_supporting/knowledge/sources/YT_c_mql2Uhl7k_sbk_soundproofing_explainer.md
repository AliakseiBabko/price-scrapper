## Engineering / Systems — Noise Taxonomy and Transmission Mechanics

- **Noise taxonomy, three types, corroborating this store's existing `12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Soundproofing.md` taxonomy directly**: airborne noise (voice, crying, barking — air vibration that excites surrounding structure: walls, ceilings, floors); impact noise (direct contact with a source — a dropped weight, footsteps, a hammer drill); structural noise, explicitly framed as a *subset* of impact noise — vibration transmitted from a source (e.g. an exterior AC condenser vibrating against a wall) into the surrounding structure rather than through the air.
- **⚠️ Direct vs. indirect noise transmission, a distinction not previously recorded on this store's soundproofing page**: direct transmission is what you feel by placing a hand on the wall a neighbor is drilling into; indirect transmission is the same noise reaching you through vibration carried into floors, ceilings, and even radiators from a source in a *different* stairwell/section of the building entirely — offered as the reason soundproofing has to address more than just the shared wall itself.
- **⚠️ Named health framing (medical claim, not independently verified here)**: sustained noise exposure above ~90 dB is associated with cardiac problems in addition to the more commonly cited attention/concentration effects — recorded as the speaker's own stated claim, not cross-checked against a medical source.
- **⚠️ Gap-size caution, stated as a direct proportionality**: any gap in an enclosing surface (the speaker's own example: the gap under a door) degrades soundproofing performance in direct proportion to its size — soundproofing must treat every penetration/adjoining element (door gaps, pipe penetrations, ventilation shafts) as part of the system, not just the flat wall/floor/ceiling surfaces.

## Materials — Window Upgrades as a Named, Quantified Soundproofing Lever

- **⚠️ Window soundproofing frequently overlooked as part of a soundproofing scope, with four separate quantified levers given**: increasing glass pane thickness gains **+3.8 dB** at low/mid frequencies; increasing the number of glass layers gains **+3-8 dB** at mid/high frequencies; increasing the air gap between window sashes/frames gains **+5-10 dB** across all frequencies; adding interior blinds/shutters gains **+7-13 dB**. `single-account`, no cited testing methodology, but concrete and directly actionable figures not previously recorded in this store's window or soundproofing content.
- **⚠️ Diminishing-returns illustration for slab mass alone**: doubling a monolithic floor slab's thickness (the speaker's own example: 20 cm → 40 cm of poured concrete) improves sound insulation by only **≈9 dB** — offered as a concrete illustration that adding raw material mass is a comparatively weak lever versus a properly engineered multi-material soundproofing system, with a real-world reference scale given alongside it (≈80 dB ≈ a shout; ≈70 dB ≈ a lecture-voice level, i.e. roughly the size of the gain from doubling an entire structural slab).

## Materials — Reflect / Absorb / Disperse Framework and Material Taxonomy

- **⚠️ Three-mechanism framework for how a soundproofing material actually works, explained via a water-jet analogy**: a soundproofing material must **reflect**, **absorb**, or **disperse** the sound wave — illustrated as a stream of water against glass (reflects, nothing passes), against a coarse mesh/colander (passes through almost entirely, no soundproofing), or against a sponge (partially absorbed, partially still passes) — material choice should be based on calculation for the specific frequency/noise-type profile involved, not selected by guesswork.
- **Material taxonomy named directly**: porous vs. non-porous — non-porous being a simple monolithic construction; porous materials named as a real product category list: porous inorganic boards (pumice-based, kaolin-based), fiberglass boards, sandwich panels with a smooth/perforated face and mineral-wool infill, sound-absorbing plasters, soundproofing mats, and porous absorbers faced with metal/wood/plastic screens. Offered as evidence of the real range of engineered soundproofing products available, supporting the "calculation, not guesswork" point above.

## Planning Rules / Budget — Project-First Methodology and a Real Cost Figure

- **⚠️ Explicit "no project, no soundproofing" rule, stated as a direct warning**: undertaking soundproofing work without an engineered project (a brief/TOR intake, on-site measurement where needed, and material selection matched to both the specific noise-control goal and the client's budget) is characterized directly as "money thrown to the wind" — the correct sequence given: brief intake (what specifically needs to be blocked — e.g. "I want to play drums and have neighbors not hear it" vs. "I just don't want to hear the neighbor next door") → project design → material procurement matched to that specific project → installation.
