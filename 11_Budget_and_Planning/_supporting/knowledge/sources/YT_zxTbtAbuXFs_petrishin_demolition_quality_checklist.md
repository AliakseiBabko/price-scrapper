## Demolition & Site Management — All New for This Store

1. **Written scope-of-work requirement, with a stated dispute-resolution
   mechanism**: even without a full design project, write a technical
   task list by hand specifying exactly which walls/openings to
   demolish, keep, or partially modify, and duplicate it in a messenger
   thread with the crew — creates a record that settles later disputes
   over whether a mistakenly-demolished wall/opening was the client's
   own planning gap or the crew's execution error. Stated real incident:
   demolishing a wall or door opening that should have been kept, caused
   by relying on verbal discussion alone.
2. **Furniture handling rule, with a stated cost mechanism**: fully
   remove all furniture from the object rather than wrapping it in
   plastic and leaving it in place — (a) ambient humidity during
   renovation ruins wrapped furniture anyway, (b) crews meaningfully
   raise their price because they're constantly forced to shift heavy
   furniture out of the way to work.
3. **Window/entrance-door protection during demolition dust**: cover
   plastic windows and sills with film, taped securely — porous plastic
   sills specifically get dust ground permanently into the surface,
   very hard to clean afterward; also cover an entrance door being kept
   (not replaced) — it can be damaged by rebar/profile material carried
   past it during debris removal.
4. **Temporary lighting as a quality-and-safety requirement, with a
   named failure mode**: insufficient lighting during demolition can
   cause incomplete removal of old plaster/oil paint layers (crews
   simply can't see what's left), besides being a basic safety issue.
5. **Temporary construction sink with a real plumbing connection, and the
   flooding risk it's meant to prevent**: install a temporary sink with
   both water supply and drain — water lets the crew wash hands/tools
   and mix dry material; the drain lets waste water go to the sewer
   instead of the floor. Named real-world failure this prevents: crews
   repeatedly forgetting to close a tap and flooding not just the object
   but the downstairs neighbor.
6. **Neighbor-relations practice, three distinct tactics**: notify
   neighbors on both sides, above, and below before demolition starts
   (loud, dusty work); specifically ask households with small children
   about nap/sleep schedules to avoid the loudest work during those
   windows; give the building concierge/doorkeeper a small gift (candy,
   ≈200-300 RUB/person) — the company's own 18-year stated experience is
   that this reliably buys goodwill that helps de-escalate future noise/
   access complaints from other residents.
7. **Daily hallway-cleanliness practice and door signage**: keep a damp
   rag/mop at the entrance door and clean the stairwell landing after
   every debris-removal trip; post a note on the door apologizing for
   the inconvenience with a phone number for emergencies/off-hours
   noise complaints — both framed as low-cost ways to avoid neighbor
   conflicts.
8. **Debris-disposal cost lifehack**: negotiate directly with the
   building's own janitorial staff for debris removal/disposal terms —
   can work out meaningfully cheaper than ordering a standard dumpster
   container, though the terms/timeline must be explicitly agreed in
   advance.
9. **Pre-priming dedusting requirement, with a stated adhesion
   mechanism**: fully dedust walls/ceilings with a brush before priming
   — priming over a dusty wall prevents the new plaster from properly
   bonding to the underlying brick/concrete.
10. **Flooring-removal-to-bare-substrate rule, adhesive-residue-specific
    failure mode**: remove all laminate/plywood/baseboard remnants down
    to the bare substrate; specifically remove leftover adhesive residue
    after pulling old linoleum — if left, it can crack later once a
    self-leveling floor is poured over it, so it must be cleaned and
    primed first. **Old bitumen layer (older apartments)**: can be left
    in place only if well-adhered, but then needs at least 4-5cm of
    screed poured over it; if dried out/barely adhered, remove entirely
    instead.
11. **Panel-ceiling joint ("руст") treatment for older low-ceiling
    Stalin/Khrushchev-era buildings, and the same method extended to
    exterior corners generally (new-builds included)**: strip whitewash/
    oil paint down to bare concrete at the panel-to-panel joint, score/
    open the joint, fully foam-fill any void, apply reinforcing mesh,
    then plaster — this is the standard method for these older
    reinforced-concrete-panel ceilings specifically. The identical
    score-clear-foam-mesh-plaster sequence is recommended for exterior
    wall corners too, in both secondary housing and new-builds, as a
    crack/draft-prevention measure.
12. **New-build developer insulation-replacement recommendation**: where
    a new-build developer's own slab-to-wall joint insulation (mineral
    wool or expanded polystyrene) is poor quality, remove and replace it
    with a penetrating treatment/impregnation instead of reusing it.
13. **⚠️ Named critical incident — delegating demolition itself to
    unsupervised, inexperienced labor, distinct from Sidorik's existing
    "informal student labor is fine for debris hauling" note (a
    different, lower-stakes task)**: company founder Sergey Petrishin's
    own early-career account — bathroom demolition was delegated to
    local building janitors without supervision while the crew went to
    another job; a piece of flat asbestos-cement sheet ("шифер") fell
    during ceiling/wall demolition and struck a hydronic towel-warmer
    pipe, rupturing it and flooding **7 apartments** below (settled with
    a mix of replaced flooring, re-papering, and direct cash payments).
    Stated lesson: even a seemingly simple task like demolition needs
    experienced, careful workers — not the cheapest available labor,
    unlike genuinely low-skill tasks like debris hauling.
14. **Ventilation-shaft resize lifehack, with an explicit legal
    caveat and a break-even threshold**: developers sometimes oversize
    the ventilation shaft's boxing well beyond the actual duct's real
    footprint to hide it — cut a small inspection hole to check for
    unused space inside; if there's a genuine 40-50cm of slack, it may
    be worth demolishing and rebuilding a smaller shaft to reclaim floor
    area. **Explicit legal caveat**: ventilation shafts are shared
    building property, and resizing them without formal permission from
    the relevant authority is prohibited — only worth pursuing at all
    given real gain (40-50cm), not for a marginal 10-15cm.
