## Planning Rules / Electrical — Underfloor-Heating Thermostat Placement, Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's core placement rule: a thermostat must always be visibly mounted, never hidden**, regardless of a designer's aesthetic objection. His stated reasoning: unlike a light switch (where the light itself tells you the state at a glance), an underfloor-heating thermostat in a room you're not standing in gives no other visual cue of whether it's on, off, or at what temperature — you either have to walk to the room and check, or see the thermostat itself. His pushback framing for a designer who wants it hidden: "then hide every switch and outlet too, since they're equally (un)attractive" — singling out the thermostat for concealment while leaving other electrical points visible is, in his view, an inconsistent argument.
- **Zemskov's rule: a loggia/balcony should always get underfloor heating, even if already well-insulated and even if a radiator has been (against code) relocated there.** His stated mechanism: the unit below is very likely to have a cold, unheated loggia, so heat loss through the floor slab is significant regardless of how well the loggia above is insulated, and a radiator alone warms the air, not the floor — leaving feet cold even in an otherwise warm loggia. He does not recommend running loggia underfloor heating continuously (real running cost), but recommends turning it on briefly before use, or keeping it on a comfortable low setting if the loggia sees regular/heavy use.
- **Zemskov's loggia thermostat placement rule, with two explicitly wrong alternatives named**: (1) **wrong** — mounted on the interior wall next to the loggia entrance, because curtains typically hang there and block it; (2) **wrong** — mounted inside the loggia itself, because checking its status means physically entering the loggia, and adjusting it means standing on the (potentially cold) loggia floor while doing so; (3) **correct, per Zemskov: mount it on the loggia door's own reveal/jamb (откос)**, always reachable and always visible from the room side. **Sub-rule for which side of the reveal**: the side visible from the bed while lying in the connected bedroom — Zemskov frames this as a simple, memorable rule ("lie on the bed, see which reveal is visible, mount it there").
- **Zemskov's bathroom/WC thermostat placement rule**: always mount **outside** the room it controls, never inside — his reasoning: mounting it outside lets you check at a glance (before entering, or on the way out of the apartment) whether the underfloor heating was left on, without having to enter the bathroom to check.
- **Zemskov's general inside/outside placement heuristic, stated as an easy-to-remember rule**: **place the underfloor-heating thermostat on the same side (inside or outside the room) as that room's own light switch.** If a room's light switch is outside the room (his bathroom/WC example), the thermostat goes outside too; if a room's switch is inside (his bedroom example), the thermostat goes inside. He extends this same logic back to the loggia case: the loggia's own light switch should also go on the door reveal, for the same reachability reasoning as the thermostat.
- **Zemskov's stated mounting height for thermostats: 160-170cm from the floor (eye/chest level), not waist height (~pояс) and not floor/baseboard level.** He explicitly argues against a widely-repeated "mount switches at chest height so children can't reach" folk rule, calling it ineffective in his experience (children lose interest in switches quickly, and mounting a thermostat within child's reach risks a child setting it to a damaging extreme temperature that could burn out the whole heating element) — **his stated real reason for eye-level mounting is ergonomic/legibility, not child-safety**: a thermostat's LCD display (unlike a modern smartphone screen) is only readable head-on, not at an angle, so a low-mounted thermostat forces the user to crouch or kneel to read/operate it; eye level avoids this. He explicitly criticizes hiding a thermostat at baseboard/under-furniture height as impractical for the same reason.
- **Zemskov's thermostat-type recommendation, three types compared**: **fully mechanical** (a physical dial, no visible read-out of the current setting once you step away) — rejected as impossible to visually confirm the current setting from a distance; **fully electronic/touchscreen** (LCD touch interface with many programmable functions) — rejected on two grounds: touch response feels sluggish/frustrating compared to a modern smartphone's, and per his own stated observation, roughly 98% of his clients who buy a fully-loaded electronic unit never use more than one basic function despite paying for the extra complexity; **his recommended middle option, which he terms "electro-mechanical"**: an LCD display for information only, paired with physical tactile buttons for control — his stated ranking: most reliable, most convenient, and best value (no unused premium functions paid for).
- **Zemskov's electrical-point alignment rule for stacked switch/thermostat groups**: when a wall has just one switch and one thermostat stacked vertically, both can share the same offset from the door reveal (his example: 15cm from reveal, switch at 90cm, thermostat at 165cm) and look fine. **But when a wall has multiple adjacent points (e.g. two switches — light and exhaust fan — plus a thermostat above them), align the thermostat to the horizontal center of the switch group below it, not to the same reveal-offset distance** — because a thermostat's faceplate is typically a different width/brand than the switches below it, so matching by offset-from-corner instead of by center will look subtly, distractingly misaligned. He gives a worked example: nearest switch 15cm from the reveal, thermostat centered above the switch group at roughly 22.5cm (sometimes 27cm depending on the actual group width) from the reveal — the center-alignment is the fixed rule, the exact resulting number varies by group width.
- **Zemskov's single most emphasized rule in this source: always extend the thermostat's temperature-sensor wire, and always do it by soldering, never with twist-splices or standard screw/spring-clamp connectors.** His stated mechanism: a stock thermostat's sensor wire is short (designed for waist-height mounting), too short to reach from an eye-level-mounted thermostat down into the heated floor's own sensor pocket — so extending it is mandatory for eye-level mounting, not optional. **Why soldering specifically**: this is, per Zemskov, literally the only place in a modern renovation where he still recommends soldering a wire joint (everywhere else, self-clamping/screwless connectors are preferred, consistent with this store's existing finish-electrical terminal-type guidance) — because sensors, unlike heating elements themselves, do fail over the years (he states modern heating-element failure is now rare, having been solved by better cold-lead-to-element joint technology, but sensor failure remains common), and the joint needs to be small/flexible enough to pass back and forth through a conduit sleeve repeatedly over the years as sensors get replaced — a bulkier clamp-type connector won't reliably fit through the same conduit multiple times.
- **A companion, load-bearing planning detail from the same rule**: always install a dedicated sleeve/conduit tube running from the thermostat location down to the sensor's pocket in the heated-floor assembly, specifically so a failed sensor can be withdrawn and a replacement fed back through the same channel without disturbing the finished floor — omitting this conduit means a failed sensor (which, per Zemskov, is a real, expected eventual failure point, unlike the heating element itself) cannot be practically replaced later.
- **Zemskov's closing pushback against installers/designers who resist the short-wire consequence of eye-level mounting**: he anticipates and directly rejects the argument "the wire's too short, so eye-level mounting must be wrong, mount it at waist height instead" — his framing: manufacturers standardized short sensor wires decades ago around outdated waist-height mounting conventions and haven't caught up; the fix is to ask/insist the installer solder an extension, not to revert to an inconvenient mounting height to avoid that extra step.
