## Rules / Heuristics — Genuinely New Content

- **⚠️ Spill-containment tile-zone undersizing caution**: a porcelain-tile "moat" strip (~70-80cm) placed around a kitchen zone specifically to protect adjacent hardwood flooring from spilled water is too narrow in practice — water doesn't stop at an arbitrary boundary and will travel past a too-small tile zone onto the wood regardless of intent. **General rule: size a spill-containment tile zone generously, not to the minimum that looks proportionate on a rendering.**
- **⚠️ Resonant steel ladder/stair caution for kids' play structures**: a cheap sheet-steel ladder/staircase element in an elaborate kids' loft/playhouse creates a loud bell-like resonance when climbed, audible throughout the apartment — especially disruptive if a child uses it at night. **Avoid resonant thin-steel structural elements in kids' play furniture; choose a material/construction that dampens sound instead.**
- **⚠️ Decorative wall panel height caution — dust-ledge tradeoff**: decorative wall panels that stop short of the ceiling (to make room for a top-mounted backlighting strip) create a dust-collecting ledge/gap at the top instead of a clean flush line. **A lit-reveal-at-the-top design choice should be made deliberately, weighing the dust-collection tradeoff, not treated as a free aesthetic upgrade.**
- **⚠️ Full-length mirror and small lit vanity mirror are not substitutes for each other**: a documented entrance zone had an excellent full-length mirror but completely omitted a small backlit close-up mirror for grooming/makeup checks. **General rule: an entrance zone benefits from both mirror types, since they serve genuinely different functions (whole-body check vs. close-up detail check) — one does not cover for the other's absence.**
- **⚠️ Honeycomb/cell-pattern tile caution for a genuinely small WC**: a trendy honeycomb-mosaic tile pattern photographs well but, in a room too small to accommodate the pattern's natural repeat, produces a room that's almost entirely narrow cut/scrap pieces. Extends this store's existing classic-tile-layout-centering content with a caution specific to this pattern type in tight rooms.
- **⚠️ Floor-embedded outlet, cleaning-conflict caution**: an in-floor electrical point with a flip-up cover is a real housekeeping obstruction in an open kitchen-living space — a robot vacuum can't pass over or navigate around it, and a mop/broom repeatedly snags on the raised cover, creating chronic dust buildup at that exact spot. A more specific version of this store's existing floor-outlet skepticism.
- **⚠️ Door-swing-direction-consistency-across-a-hallway usability principle**: when a sequence of doors sits along the same hallway, people develop a directional expectation that all of them swing the same way — a door swinging the opposite direction from its neighbors is a genuine, documented usability friction ("very uncomfortable"), even when that specific door's own swing direction is independently justifiable by the general swing-direction rules. **Worth checking a hallway's full door sequence for directional consistency, not just each door in isolation.**
- **⚠️ Handle-free-appliance-system consistency caution**: paying a real premium for handle-free, fingerprint-resistant cabinet fronts (e.g. "Perfect Sense" material) specifically to eliminate visible hardware, then adding crude mismatched exposed handles to the fridge/freezer and a shiny handle to the oven, undermines the entire investment. **If committing to a handle-free design system, apply it consistently across every appliance in the run, not selectively.**
- **⚠️ Black radiator-grille color caution, a counterintuitive finding**: painting a radiator's concealment grille black specifically makes accumulated dust *more* visible, not less — the opposite of the intended "hide the ugly radiator" effect. **A lighter/neutral grille color hides dust better than black.**
- **⚠️ Door-open-limiter (rubber wall-mounted stopper) failure timeline, a concrete data point**: this stopper type absorbs 100% of the door's impact energy through the door handle itself (no separate stop point) — the handle starts visibly wobbling after just 2-3 uses and typically needs replacement within 1-2 weeks of real use. **Extends this store's existing door-stopper leverage-failure mechanism finding (from `Rlyx2F7Aaxg`) with a concrete failure timeline.**
- **⚠️ Shower-enclosure bottom-threshold/seal omission caution**: a premium/"elite" shower enclosure was installed without a proper bottom threshold or door-sweep seal — a feature present even on budget balcony enclosures — inevitably letting shower water pool on the floor outside the enclosure. A site-applied silicone bead was used as a stopgap, explicitly described as temporary and likely to fail and need redoing. **Don't assume a premium-priced shower enclosure includes a genuine bottom seal — verify it directly.**
- **⚠️ Backsplash material-matching-to-water-exposure rule, mechanism explained**: a decorative concrete-look plaster finish applied directly beside a kitchen sink (rather than porcelain tile) absorbs water at the wall-counter junction, cracks, and delaminates within about a year, requiring a full redo. **Porcelain tile should specifically cover a sink-adjacent backsplash zone even within an otherwise fully-plastered open-plan kitchen-living space** — match material choice to each zone's actual water exposure, not the room's overall aesthetic theme uniformly.
- **⚠️ Divided-storage-unit zone-usability mismatch**: a coat closet split between a "dirty" and "clean" zone had its most conveniently-accessible half land in the dirty zone and its less-convenient half in the clean zone — backwards from actual daily use. **Verify which half of any divided storage unit lands in the more convenient zone actually matches which half will see more frequent use.**
- **Windowsill/false-wall complementary-upgrade miss, a documented inconsistency**: a client paid extra to upgrade from developer-standard radiators to better ones, but the designer never proposed the obvious complementary upgrade (a wider windowsill, or a false wall with a decorative grille to conceal the same radiators) — worth checking, whenever a client invests in upgrading one fixture, whether an obvious complementary comfort/concealment upgrade should be bundled into the same decision.
