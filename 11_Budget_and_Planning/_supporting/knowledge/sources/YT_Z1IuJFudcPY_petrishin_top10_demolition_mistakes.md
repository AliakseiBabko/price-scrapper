## Site Management / Demolition — New Facts (Mistakes / Warnings, Rules / Heuristics)

1. **⚠️ Teach-back verification method for a technical demolition
   project**: beyond just handing the crew a written scope document,
   explicitly ask the foreman/master to explain back, in his own words,
   how he understood the document and how he intends to execute each
   part — catches a misunderstanding before work starts rather than
   after a wall is already gone. A concrete new verification step for
   this page's existing scope-of-work content.
2. **Use delicate/gentle tape, not ordinary masking or painter's tape,
   to secure protective film over windows/doors during demolition** —
   specifically to avoid leaving adhesive residue marks. A concrete
   material-choice detail extending this page's existing dust-protection
   content.
3. **⚠️ New mechanism: irreversible glass pitting ("окалины") from
   angle-grinder sparks on unprotected window glass**: cutting metal
   (rebar, leveling beacons, guide rails, etc.) near an uncovered window
   throws sparks that pit the glass surface with tiny permanent marks,
   invisible from a couple meters away but clearly visible up close.
   Unlike dust/dirt, **these marks cannot be cleaned off** — the only
   fix is replacing the glass unit itself, at roughly **50% of the
   window's total cost**. A distinct, previously unrecorded window-damage
   mechanism for this page.
4. **⚠️ White vs. green heavy-duty debris bags, material distinction**:
   green bags are a garden/lawn-waste type, not durable enough for heavy
   demolition debris — they tear, spilling debris into shared building
   corridors/elevators/stairwells (a real neighbor-relations risk). White
   bags cost only slightly more and are meaningfully more durable. A
   concrete, checkable material-buying rule.
5. **⚠️ De-energize old wiring and run temporary wiring before demolition,
   with a stated hiring red flag**: old wiring is typically embedded in
   walls being broken/cut, risking electric shock; de-energize it and
   run temporary power first. **If a crew says they'll just "work in the
   dark" (skip disconnecting) to save time, treat that as a signal about
   their overall care/competence** — a concrete vetting heuristic, not
   just a safety step.
6. **⚠️ Photo-document the apartment directly below before hammer-work
   starts, explicit fraud-defense rationale**: heavy demolition work
   (sledgehammer/jackhammer) can transmit vibration that cracks a
   downstairs neighbor's ceiling/walls — but some neighbors have
   pre-existing, unrelated cracks and may later claim your renovation
   caused them, seeking a free repair at your expense. Photographing
   every room/wall/ceiling below beforehand documents the pre-existing
   condition and removes the leverage for this claim.
7. **Debris-removal timing rule, midday not rush hour**: avoid carrying
   debris bags out during the after-work rush hour when residents are
   returning home — crowds the elevator/hallways with dusty bags and
   creates a bad impression; midday is the better window.
8. **⚠️ Container-placement approval distinction, new-build vs.
   secondary-market, real cost consequence**: in a modern residential
   complex (ЖК), large trucks/containers are barred from entering without
   prior approval from the building's management company; without that
   approval, the driver won't be let onto the property and will simply
   drop the container roughly **300m** from the entrance — forcing extra
   paid labor to haul every bag that distance, since no one will do it
   for free. In an older secondary-market building, the constraint is
   different: all nearby curb space is usually occupied by parked cars,
   so the driver drops the container at whatever spot is free, which may
   also be inconveniently far. Extends this page's existing container-
   negotiation content with two distinct real-world failure modes and a
   concrete labor-cost consequence.
9. **Towel-warmer/heated towel rail as the single most dangerous point
   in demolition, a stated incidence figure**: "90% of all accident
   situations at this stage happen right at this point" — **a different
   framing from this page's existing figure** (an earlier Petrishin-Stroi
   source states towel-rail floods occur in "3-4 out of 10" projects
   overall) — this is a share-of-accidents statistic, not a share-of-
   projects statistic; recorded as a related but distinct figure, not a
   contradiction, since the two describe different measures.
10. **⚠️⚠️ Standout finding — real named flooding incident from
    unsupervised, unskilled demolition labor**: early in the foreman's
    own career, he and a partner assigned demolition on two objects to
    unsupervised local building janitors ("дворники") to save time and
    work two jobs simultaneously. While demolishing a flat-asbestos-
    cement ("шифер") shower cabin, a piece of ceiling fell onto a
    protruding towel-warmer, rupturing it; the resulting flood damaged
    **5-6 apartments below**, requiring the foreman personally to visit
    each affected household to redo wallpaper/laminate or pay direct
    cash compensation. A real, first-person, high-consequence example of
    the general "vet who you trust with demolition" caution already on
    this page — this is the *story behind* that caution, not a duplicate
    of it.
