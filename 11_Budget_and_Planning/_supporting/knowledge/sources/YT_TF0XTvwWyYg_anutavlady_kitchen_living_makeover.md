## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Wall-color-selection method for a space with fixed, unchangeable
  materials**: use a color-picker/eyedropper tool (the source
  demonstrates in Photoshop) on a fixed material already present and
  staying (here: fireplace stone) to derive a coherent, closely-related
  paint-color family for the walls, rather than choosing wall color in
  isolation from the room's fixed materials.
- **Paint color can only be reliably chosen by physical on-wall
  testing** — buy small sample cans, apply swatches directly to the
  actual wall in several candidate shades, and decide from those; a
  color viewed on a monitor, in a reference photo, or even the same
  paint code in a different room's lighting will read differently once
  applied, due to each space's own natural/artificial lighting mix.
  Framed as the only correct method, and the one professional designers
  actually use — not a shortcut-able step.
- **Color-psychology caution, muted lavender/dusty-pastel tones
  specifically**: these read as associated with sadness/melancholy: fine
  as a small accent, but risky as the dominant palette across an entire
  room — a room saturated in this tone family reads as gloomy even with
  good natural light.
- **Repainting/refinishing an existing "must-keep" furniture piece to
  match a room's other fixed-white elements (e.g. kitchen cabinet
  fronts) is presented as a first concrete step toward unifying a
  kitchen-living combined space** — rather than replacing the piece,
  bringing its finish into the same color family as an already-present
  anchor color pulls it visually into the shared space.
- **Black-accent-multiplication technique**: scattering small black
  details (hardware, small objects) across multiple points in an
  otherwise flat/neutral room adds visual contrast and expressiveness
  without needing to introduce any bold/saturated color.
- **Avoid stacking multiple strong focal objects in the same sightline**
  (here: a TV and a bold painting both above/near a fireplace) — pick
  one dominant accent per zone; a large mirror pair was kept as the
  zone's single accent instead of adding a second competing artwork.
- **A rug's function in a combined space is dual, not purely
  decorative**: it zones a sub-area *and* visually lightens/offsets a
  dark floor tone, helping unify the room's overall palette — worth
  choosing rug tone deliberately for the second reason, not just the
  first.
- **Deliberate contrast-echo furniture-detail choice**: darker chair
  legs were picked specifically to introduce more visual contrast into
  an otherwise same-tone-heavy kitchen zone and to "rhyme" with the
  living room's darker furniture tones — lighter-toned legs matching
  the surrounding light furniture were explicitly rejected as reading
  "bland."
- **⚠️ Two-tone sofa upholstery (darker frame/base + lighter cushions)
  is now dated, not current** — this was a common furniture-
  manufacturer default at one point but a single unified fabric across
  the whole sofa is described as the more timeless, currently
  fashionable choice; reupholstering (rather than replacing) a
  structurally-fine, otherwise-liked sofa is the recommended fix.
- **Light-fixture/furniture axis-alignment tradeoff**: ideally center a
  hanging fixture directly above the furniture piece it lights (e.g. a
  dining table), but if that furniture might realistically be
  repositioned later, keep the fixture centered on the room/ceiling
  structure instead — an off-axis fixture matched to today's furniture
  placement becomes a visible problem if the furniture later moves, and
  is harder to fix without rewiring than a furniture rearrangement is.
- **Mirror-as-decorative-"window" technique**: mirrors sized and placed
  to mimic a window's proportions/position (rather than an actual
  window) work as a wall accent above furniture like a dresser/console,
  especially useful in a windowless wall zone.
- **Kitchen-hood modernization as a discrete high-impact fix**: an
  otherwise well-functioning, non-dated kitchen can still read as dated
  purely because of a visible domed/hood-style overhead extractor —
  replacing it with a fully built-in (cabinet-concealed) extractor
  meaningfully modernizes the kitchen's overall read without touching
  the rest of the cabinetry.
- **Full-space curtain-treatment consistency**: unify every window
  across a combined open kitchen-living space with the same curtain
  fabric/treatment, even where one zone previously had a different
  treatment (e.g. sheer tulle only vs. lined curtains) — reinforces the
  sense that the two zones are one continuous space.
