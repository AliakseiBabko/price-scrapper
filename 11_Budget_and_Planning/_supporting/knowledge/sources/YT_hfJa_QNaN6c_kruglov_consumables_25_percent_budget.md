## Planning Rules / Budget — What Counts as "Rough Material" and "Consumables"

- **⚠️ Explicit definition, previously undocumented at this granularity
  in this store**: "rough material" (черновой материал) is anything used
  during the renovation that stays in the apartment but becomes
  invisible once finish work covers it — electrical cable, water/
  heating/sewage pipe, manifold nodes, filters, electrical breakers,
  plus the raw block/plaster/tile-adhesive/primer/putty materials
  themselves. **"Consumables" (расходники) is a separate line**: buckets,
  paint rollers/trays, spatulas, core drill bits, cutting discs, gloves,
  trash bags, respirators. **Professional tools are explicitly excluded
  from both** — the crew or company is expected to own its own tools;
  billing tool cost to the client is flagged as improper.
- **⚠️ Temporary utility installation for new-build apartments is a
  real, unavoidable cost line, not a company favor**: a temporary
  toilet, temporary lighting, and temporary water supply are needed to
  perform renovation work at all in a new-build unit, and **the client
  always pays for the equipment itself** regardless of company — the
  variation between companies is only whether the *installation labor*
  for this temporary setup is billed separately or bundled free (Ontario
  states it does this installation free; other companies charge for it).
- **⚠️ Debris/garbage removal cost scales sharply with floor level and
  elevator access, with a concrete example**: on a 5th-floor secondary-
  market apartment with no service elevator, removal cost can require
  hauling out roughly **15-20 separate container loads**. Recommended
  cost-saving channel for small volumes: local building
  janitors/dvorniks will often remove modest debris quantities cheaply
  and informally; **legally, construction debris requires specialized
  disposal**, but a small residual quantity (the source's own example:
  ~10 bags of leftover tile-adhesive packaging) is treated as a
  practical exception. For volume at scale (hundreds of bags of
  sand/cement across even a 50 m² secondary-market renovation), a
  full specialized dumpster/skip is the only realistic option, not an
  informal one.
- **Common-area protection/cleanup during material delivery and debris
  removal is its own real, recurring cost line** — many residential
  complexes require ongoing lift/hallway protection or cleaning after
  each delivery/removal pass; a small but real budget item, framed as a
  reasonable requirement (protecting communal areas from scuffed
  corners, scratched walls, dropped tile) rather than a nuisance charge.
- **⚠️ Management-company plumbing work is a distinct rough-material
  cost line specific to secondary-market apartments**: welding/replacing
  riser connections (heating and, less often, sewage) requires the
  building's own management-company plumber, a real, non-trivial cost.
  **A related new-build-specific version**: converting a new-build's
  default series-connected ("последовательное") radiator plumbing to a
  parallel/manifold-fed configuration should be followed by a
  management-company plumber's pressure test (опрессовка) and a signed
  compliance certificate — protects the client by having the management
  company itself certify the renovation company's plumbing work meets
  code, heading off later disputes.
- **⚠️ Post-renovation cleaning is explicitly *not* offered in-house by
  this company, with a named reason**: after trialing "a large number"
  of private cleaners and both small and large cleaning companies over
  time, Ontario states it found none it could work with long-term
  without recurring damage complaints (chemical-solution damage to
  laminate, wallpaper, walls; scratches) — so clients must source their
  own post-renovation cleaner. **A named cost-tension example**: a
  cheap cleaner quoted around 20,000 RUB (≈$220) for an hourly clean
  scratched a cooktop during the job, and the resulting damage-repair
  cost was framed as landing back on the client's own pocket despite the
  "cheap" quote, versus a cleaner confident enough to quote roughly
  double (≈40,000 RUB, ≈$440) for reliably careful work — framed as the
  real cost/risk tradeoff behind hiring a cleaner, not evidence a
  specific price threshold is safe. *(Extends this vault's existing
  Post_Renovation_Cleaning.md content with a first concrete
  cost-vs-damage-risk anecdote from this channel — not previously
  recorded there.)*
- **Apartment/renovation insurance is framed as optional and genuinely
  tricky, not a simple add-on**: before buying, the client must clearly
  understand what is insured, for how much, and — critically — what
  legally counts as an insured event and what doesn't. **Named example
  of a claim likely to be denied**: if a contractor (not the client)
  leaves a shutoff valve open and hot water damages the apartment, the
  insurer still needs an identifiable at-fault party before paying —
  it may pay the client and then separately sue the party actually at
  fault, rather than simply covering contractor negligence directly.
  **Practical framing**: it's simpler for the renovation company to
  prevent and directly remediate this kind of damage itself than to rely
  on insurance litigation after the fact.

## Planning Rules / Budget — The Headline Figure and Three Procurement Rules

- **⚠️ Headline benchmark, from the source's own stated 60-object
  analysis**: rough materials + consumables + all the cost lines above
  (temporary utilities, delivery/lifting, debris removal, common-area
  protection, management-company plumbing) together should be budgeted
  at **≈50-60% of the labor-only smeta total** — worked examples: a
  1,000,000 RUB (≈$11,100) labor smeta implies ≈500,000-600,000 RUB
  (≈$5,600-$6,700) for this combined category; a 2,000,000 RUB
  (≈$22,200) labor smeta implies ≈1,000,000-1,200,000 RUB
  (≈$11,100-$13,300). **⚠️ Flagged scope-definition note (not a
  contradiction)**: this video's own title states "25% of your
  renovation budget," while the body content states "50-60% of the
  labor smeta specifically" — the two figures describe different
  denominators (25% likely of the whole-project total across all 8
  budget categories above; 50-60% of the *labor-only* line item) and are
  not treated here as conflicting, since neither the video itself nor
  this extraction resolves the exact relationship between the two
  percentages.
- **⚠️ Direct explanation for wildly divergent competing quotes on
  "rough materials," tying back to this same figure**: a company quoting
  150,000-200,000 RUB (≈$1,700-$2,200) against Ontario's own
  500,000-600,000 RUB (≈$5,600-$6,700) isn't necessarily overcharging or
  undercharging — the real question is **what each company's own
  definition of "rough material" actually includes** (e.g. whether
  temporary utilities, debris removal, and management-company plumbing
  are bundled in or quoted as separate line items elsewhere). **Practical
  mitigation**: ask each competing company/foreman explicitly what they
  include under "rough material" before comparing totals — the same
  underlying "equalize scope before comparing $/m²" principle already
  established elsewhere in this guide (segment-matching, area-scaling),
  now applied specifically to the rough-materials line.
- **⚠️ Rule 1 — approve rough-material volumes/types before purchase,
  not after**: the company/crew should present a proposed materials
  list and quantity for client sign-off before ordering; material
  should not be delivered to the site until the client has approved it.
- **⚠️ Rule 2 — material cost should not be folded into the same fixed
  contract sum as labor cost**: bundling both into one contract number
  removes the client's ability to independently source or substitute
  materials (e.g. choosing a pricier plaster or tile adhesive than the
  bundled default), and a company that pre-quotes bundled material
  quantities (worked example: "100 bags at 400 RUB each") typically
  prices with enough built-in buffer to stay profitable even if real
  unit prices rise months later — meaning "locking in" a material price
  this way protects the client's rate, not the true purchased volume,
  which is still billed by actual quantity used. Keep material and
  labor as separate, distinctly tracked cost lines in the contract.
- **⚠️ Rule 3 — agree on an explicit reference-price benchmark up
  front (e.g. Leroy Merlin or Петрович retail chain pricing)**: if a
  company's actual delivered material pricing lands more than
  ~15-20% above the agreed reference retail price (worked example
  named: a Ротбанд plaster line coming in 15-20% above Leroy Merlin's
  own price), that is evidence the company isn't honoring its pricing
  agreement — at that point, self-purchasing directly is a reasonable
  response. Ontario states its own practice: selling rough materials to
  clients at Leroy Merlin price ±1% (the small variance attributed to
  retail price movement between quote and purchase), payment only on
  confirmed delivery (not in advance), with the company itself absorbing
  procurement/delivery/lifting logistics and material-quality
  responsibility.
