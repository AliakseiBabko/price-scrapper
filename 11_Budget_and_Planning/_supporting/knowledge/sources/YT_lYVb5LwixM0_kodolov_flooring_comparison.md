## Materials — New Facts

- **⚠️ Material composition breakdown, all three are composites, not
  solid wood**: laminate = wood-fiber particle board + printed image
  layer + lamination (no real wood at all); engineered/parquet board
  (инженерная доска) = plywood base (multiple cross-grain layers) + a
  genuine natural-wood veneer top layer + a protective top coat; quartz-
  parquet = **75% stone (SPC — stone-plastic composite) + 25% PVC** as
  the base, + a genuine natural-wood veneer top layer + protective
  coating. Reasoning for composites existing at all: pure solid wood
  requires heavy humidity-control maintenance (swells/shrinks/cracks with
  seasonal humidity swings — searchable as a common real-world complaint)
  that composites are specifically engineered to avoid.
- **⚠️ Formaldehyde-emission classification and a real cross-material
  comparison, with a citation caveat flagged by the source itself**: the
  E0.5/E1/E2 emission-class system exists specifically for formaldehyde
  content (E0.5 and E1 legal for residential use, E2 not); Kodolov's own
  stated skepticism (not adopted as fact, but worth recording as a
  reasonable buyer caution) is that the E0.5/E1 threshold gap is narrow
  enough that manufacturing variance/human error in a given production
  batch could plausibly cross it even when the product is certified
  correctly. **Real comparative figures cited from his own certificates/
  screenshots**: laminate's tested formaldehyde emission was **~26×
  higher** than quartz-parquet's in this comparison; engineered board —
  despite being "more wood" and popularly assumed the safer choice on
  forums — can legally emit **more** than laminate specifically because
  its plywood base is bonded with adhesive rated only to the same E1/E2
  system as laminate's, not automatically cleaner. **Stated ranking (all
  three legally compliant per Russian Federation law, none framed as
  literally lethal): quartz-parquet lowest emission, laminate second,
  engineered board highest** — flagged as counter-intuitive since
  engineered board looks the most "natural."
  - **Practical caveat given directly**: formaldehyde emission increases
    when underfloor heating is installed beneath a material, because
    heat accelerates outgassing — relevant specifically to the heated-
    floor-under-laminate combination below.
- **⚠️ Water-damage-recovery comparison — a real, checkable mechanism
  distinct from a "which is more water-resistant" framing**: laminate
  swells and structurally breaks apart when flooded — not repairable in
  place, forces full floor replacement (and collateral demolition:
  baseboards, then wallpaper/plaster damage from baseboard removal).
  Engineered board, when flooded, tends to "tent" (buckle upward in
  ridges) rather than disintegrate — repairable in place by cutting
  relief kerfs, laying it back flat, and injecting adhesive underneath;
  furniture must still be moved but the floor itself can usually be
  saved. Quartz-parquet's SPC/stone base is not water-absorbent at all,
  so flooding doesn't meaningfully affect it. **Explicit ranking: quartz-
  parquet 1st, engineered board 2nd, laminate 3rd for flood resilience.**
- **Real price ranges stated (RUB/m², as of this 2024 video, source
  caveats these move with exchange-rate volatility)**: laminate
  ≈1,000-3,000 RUB/m²; engineered board ≈4,000-7,000 RUB/m² (up to
  ~10,000 RUB/m² still not "ultra-premium" at time of filming); quartz-
  parquet ≈4,000-6,000 RUB/m², with exotic species (walnut, wenge veneer)
  reaching closer to ~7,000 RUB/m². **Explicit ranking: laminate cheapest
  by a clear margin; engineered board and quartz-parquet roughly tied for
  2nd/3rd.**
- **Seamless (no-expansion-joint) maximum installation area, a specific
  number**: SPC-based flooring (quartz-parquet) can be laid continuously
  up to **144m², with a maximum single side length of 12m**, before an
  expansion break is needed — cited as second only to porcelain tile for
  seamless coverage. Laminate and engineered board should be laid
  per-room instead (their conventional expansion-gap practice, already
  covered on this vault's page); explicit reasoning given: differential
  expansion is proportional to run length, so a long room (e.g. 6m) and
  a short room (e.g. 2m) sharing one continuous run will expand by
  different absolute amounts, causing visible gapping/warping at the
  boundary regardless of material. **Explicit ranking: quartz-parquet
  clear 1st; laminate and engineered board roughly tied 2nd/3rd.**
- **⚠️ Heated-floor compatibility comparison, mechanism-based, not just a
  compatibility checklist**: engineered board's own wood layer acts as a
  thermal insulator, significantly reducing heated-floor efficiency
  (COP) — heated floor is "usually not installed" under engineered board
  for this reason, though the source's personal preference is still to
  walk on natural wood underfoot and considers engineered board "warm
  enough" without heating for most of the year. Laminate transmits heat
  well and is well-suited to heated floors technically — **but doing so
  measurably increases formaldehyde emission** (see above), which the
  source states he personally would not accept for a household with
  children or elderly residents specifically, while calling it a
  reasonable individual choice for an adult-only household. Quartz-
  parquet's stone base has the best heat transfer of the three (every
  watt converts to usable warmth); its thin wood veneer top layer is too
  thin to meaningfully insulate against that. **Explicit ranking for
  heated-floor suitability: quartz-parquet 1st, engineered board 2nd
  (walkable-warmth reasoning, not heated-floor compatibility), laminate
  3rd (technically compatible but a real health-tradeoff caveat).**
- **⚠️ Wear-layer/finish maintenance comparison — genuinely new
  distinction not on this vault's page yet**: solid/engineered wood's
  visible "wood" surface is never actually touched underfoot — a
  protective coating sits on top and wears unevenly by traffic pattern
  (a real personal example: kitchen floor needed re-coating far more
  often than a low-traffic spot near a radiator in the same room), making
  "does it need recoating yet" hard to judge by eye until it's
  visibly failing; full refinishing (sanding + relacquering) requires
  emptying the room of furniture and is expensive enough that many
  owners (the source's own family included) simply let a worn floor
  degrade rather than maintain it properly. **Four named lacquer/coating
  types for engineered board, in two tiers**: water-based lacquer and
  synthetic-oil-resin-based lacquer (thinner, closer tactile connection
  to the real wood grain, but need re-coating roughly every ~6 months to
  stay protected); solvent-free polyurethane lacquer and acid-curing
  lacquer (thicker, far more durable — cited at up to ~50 years before
  wearing through — but fully seal the wood surface under a film, so you
  never actually touch the real wood despite feeling its texture).
  Quartz-parquet ships with **7 layers of protective coating** and no
  serviceable/refinishable variant at all — a deliberate "unkillable, no
  choice" design, contrasted with engineered board's explicit choice
  between low-maintenance-but-short-lived and durable-but-fully-sealed.
  Laminate has no wood layer to maintain at all ("no wood, no problem").
  **Explicit ranking for this dimension: engineered board 1st (offers a
  real choice), quartz-parquet 2nd (durable but no choice), laminate 3rd
  (nothing to maintain, but nothing to experience either).**
- **Quick supplementary comparison facts**: density ≈1 tonne/m³ for
  laminate and engineered board vs. **≈2 tonnes/m³ for quartz-parquet**
  (more force needed to damage it); engineered board and quartz-parquet
  can both be **custom-painted any color** (including green, per the
  source's own stated real example) since their surface is genuine wood
  veneer; laminate cannot be painted (nothing but a printed image to
  paint over). **Quartz-parquet is explicitly stated as suitable for a
  bathroom/wet-room installation; the other two are not.**
- **⚠️ Overall scoring framework, methodology worth noting**: the source
  proposes a point system (3/2/1 points for 1st/2nd/3rd place across each
  comparison dimension above) to reach an aggregate ranking rather than
  asserting one universal winner — an explicit "your own priority weights
  may differ" framing, consistent with this project's own general
  "record the criteria, not just a verdict" approach to comparisons.
