## Balcony / Loggia — ⚠️ Real 3-Year Failure Case: Cork Wall Finish

1. **Cork wall panel finish failed over 3 years**: shrank and developed
   gaps ("образовались щели"), corners abraded/peeled, faded unevenly in
   sun exposure (visibly different color where a shelf blocked direct
   sunlight vs. exposed areas), didn't clean well, and changed dimension
   with humidity swings. Practical conclusion stated directly: cork is
   "definitely not suited for corners" and, more broadly, not suited to a
   loggia's temperature/humidity-swing environment — a real negative data
   point on a wall-finish material not otherwise covered on this page.

## Balcony / Loggia — ⚠️ Disagreement: Quartz-Vinyl Laminate Over a Heated Loggia Floor

2. **Direct disagreement with this page's existing Petrishin-Stroi-sourced
   recommendation** (see [[10_Balcony/analysis/Insulation_and_Heating|Insulation & Heating]],
   which documents a real failure case — gaps, cupping, squeaking — and
   recommends ceramic/porcelain tile *instead of* quartz-vinyl over a
   heated loggia floor). This source installed quartz-vinyl laminate
   ("кварцевый ламинат", branded "Парк Stone Collection") directly over
   his own heated loggia floor as a **floating** click-lock floor (glue-
   free, mandatory 10mm expansion gap at every wall) and reports it
   performing well, citing good thermal conductivity (compatible with
   underfloor heating) and dimensional stability under heat/humidity
   swings. **Flagged as an open disagreement, not resolved**: one
   plausible reconciling factor this source itself doesn't address is
   installation method — Petrishin's failure case was likely a glued-down
   installation (implied by the failure symptoms) vs. this source's
   floating floor with an explicit expansion gap, which could plausibly
   explain why one installation tolerated heat-cycling and the other
   didn't. Not confirmed either way; recorded as a genuine two-source
   contradiction on the same underlying question (quartz-vinyl compatible
   with a heated loggia floor?), not silently resolved in either
   direction.
3. **Quartz-vinyl laminate material claim**: described as "kварцевый
   ламинат" (quartz-vinyl laminate) = stone + primary/virgin PVC composite
   ("камень и первичный винил"), making it water-resistant, self-
   extinguishing (tested on camera — doesn't sustain a flame), and
   dimensionally stable under heat/humidity swings (unlike his failed cork
   panels or an earlier failed setup — implied comparison to laminate/
   engineered flooring generally).

## Balcony / Loggia — Insulation Material Swap: PIR Board vs. His Old Scheme

4. **New material, not yet on this page**: ТехноНИКОЛЬ "Логикпир"
   (Logicpir) — a PIR (polyisocyanurate) foam insulation board, factory-
   foil-faced on both sides, recommended to this source by his own
   viewers. Selling points he states: non-combustible / does not sustain
   combustion and holds its shape under direct flame exposure (tested on
   camera, couldn't ignite it) — a genuinely different material class from
   this page's existing пеноплекс (EPS)/mineral wool/пенофол comparison,
   worth cross-checking against those given the fire-safety framing (a
   loggia often sits on an escape-route path, raising the stakes of
   insulation flammability beyond ordinary comfort/cost tradeoffs). Large-
   format panels reduce the number of seams (fewer heat-loss paths than a
   scheme built from many smaller boards). Already field-tested by this
   source in an unrelated application (garage/workshop insulation, a
   compact home sauna's thermal insulation) before being used on the
   loggia redo.
5. **His old (now-abandoned) subfloor/wall scheme, for contrast**: original
   insulation + ГВЛ (gypsum-fiberboard) sheathing under a branded floor
   channel product + waterproofing, with a Lavsan (полиэфирная/mylar-type)
   film vapor barrier glued on for both vapor blocking and infrared-heat
   reflection. Named downsides he now states explicitly: wood in the floor
   assembly is moisture-prone and can rot if enough water gets onto the
   floor; metal mounting legs/posts ("ноги") in that scheme create a cold
   bridge; the assembly consumes ~4cm of floor width per side to fireproof
   the wood/ДСП adequately if redone — he says he'd now prefer an
   insulation+finish combination that avoids both wood and highly
   combustible materials entirely, which is exactly the reasoning behind
   his PIR-board switch above.

## Balcony / Loggia — ⚠️ Alternative Mounting Technique: Glue-Only, No Mechanical Anchors

6. **Genuinely new technique, contrasts with this page's anchor-fixing
   content**: this source glues both the PIR insulation board (foam
   adhesive, "клей-пена") and the quartz-vinyl laminate finish directly to
   the wall with **no mechanical fasteners/anchors at all**, explicitly
   framed as avoiding the cold-bridge risk of a dowel/anchor-based mounting
   scheme ("мостики холода как схема с нагелями"). **Load test performed
   on camera**: a 32kg weight hung from a shelf mounted only into the
   glued insulation+laminate assembly (screw doesn't reach the wall,
   anchored only in the finish/insulation layers) held without failure —
   his conclusion: no embedded backing blocks ("закладные") are needed for
   a light fixture like a shelf with this glue-only assembly. **Relation
   to existing content**: this is a materially different approach from
   Zemskov's anchor-fixing rules and Petrishin's plastic-dish-anchor
   insulation mounting (both documented on
   [[10_Balcony/analysis/Glazing_and_Structural_Work|Glazing & Structural Work]]
   and [[10_Balcony/analysis/Insulation_and_Heating|Insulation & Heating]])
   — not a direct contradiction (different insulation material/wall
   construction, and this source never tested a heavier load than 32kg),
   but a genuinely distinct third data point on how to avoid a cold bridge
   at a wall fixing point, worth recording as an alternative rather than
   silently merging with the anchor-based rule.

## Balcony / Loggia — Corner-Cutting Technique for Rigid Laminate Panels

7. **Detailed corner-forming technique for a rigid click-lock quartz-vinyl
   panel** (not documented elsewhere on this page, which currently only
   covers a *flexible* PVC panel's seamless-corner technique): score/cut
   the panel from its back (non-face) side at a 45° double-cut without
   cutting through the face layer, then heat the scored line with a heat
   gun to soften it enough to fold without cracking the rigid front layer
   — produces a finished, factory-look external corner without a separate
   trim profile. A router (freser) with a jig/fence is described as a
   faster, more precise alternative to hand-cutting with a knife or angle
   grinder once multiple identical corner pieces are needed, at the cost
   of requiring test cuts first (a single misjudged pass risks ruining
   several pre-cut blanks at once).
8. **Practical installation notes**: install the top course of panels
   first as the leveling reference for every other row (rather than
   starting from the floor); keep glue application thin and squared-off
   with the last trowel pass so excess doesn't ooze into the click-lock
   joint; wipe wet glue off tools/hands immediately, since cured glue is
   very difficult to remove afterward.
