## Rules / Heuristics — Visual Electrician-Quality Checklist at Rough-Stage Handover

- **⚠️ Site cleanliness at handover as a leading quality indicator**: floor swept/vacuumed clean, debris bagged and removed — framed not just as tidiness but as a direct enabler for the next trade (painters can start immediately without their own cleanup pass first).
- **⚠️ Outlet-box caps (заглушки) left in place through the plastering stage, a named cross-trade protection technique**: professional electricians cap every back-box (подрозетник) before painters/plasterers start skim-coating, so putty/dust cannot enter the box mechanism. A gig-style electrician's uncapped boxes fill with plaster dust/debris that later degrades switch/socket contact mechanisms and shortens their working life — a concrete, checkable mechanism (visually inspect a sample box before final devices are installed), distinct from this store's existing box-flush-fit acceptance check (which tests physical seating, not debris ingress).
- **⚠️ "Штроба" (cable-chase groove) fill-flush spatula test, a distinct check from this store's existing box-flush-fit spatula check**: hold a wide spatula flat against a filled chase line — if it sits flush with no gap, the chase was filled level with the wall surface by the electrician, meaning painters/plasterers don't have to redo that section themselves (and the client isn't billed twice for the same work). A visible gap under the spatula indicates the chase was left unfilled/uneven and will require rework at the painter's expense (in time or cost).
- **Neat, straight conduit/cable routing on the ceiling (before drop-ceiling install) as a visible care indicator**: routing described as "even and tidy" is treated as direct evidence the electrician cared about workmanship quality, even in an area that will be permanently hidden — contrasted with visibly messy/kinked routing attributed to gig-style work.
- **⚠️ Trunking channel ("кабель-канал") for consolidating multiple conduits before they enter the panel, a named technique to avoid loose/hanging cable ends ("сопли")**: where the physical width of the incoming conduit bundle exceeds the panel's own cable-entry width (the video's example: a low-voltage/weak-current sub-panel narrower than the incoming conduit run), conduits are gathered into a single trunking channel, stripped of their individual conduit sheathing inside the channel, and the bundled cables enter the panel cleanly from there — an aesthetic-and-organizational technique applied even to concealed above-ceiling work.
- **⚠️ Hidden conduit stub-out ("закладная") for a future not-yet-installed device, worked example: a video intercom panel**: where a device (here, a video door-intercom) will be installed in a room but isn't being connected yet, a conduit stub is run and left capped/ready specifically so no wall-opening or re-routing is needed later — panel installation later becomes a simple cable-pull-and-mount, not new construction work.
- **Panel-face labeling for safety/usability, plus a caution about who should act on it**: every breaker/switch position labeled with a clear visual legend so any household member (the video's example: even a child) could operate it correctly — immediately followed by the caveat that children specifically should **not** actually be the ones operating breakers, despite the labeling being clear enough for them to.
- **⚠️ Panel internal cleanliness as a named fire-risk mechanism, distinct from cosmetic panel-face labeling**: remove the panel's front cover and inspect internal wiring/assembly quality and cleanliness directly — dust/dirt accumulating inside a panel and around breakers is stated to progressively degrade breaker mechanisms over time, shortening their working life, and is explicitly named as a fire-risk factor, not merely an aesthetic concern.
- **⚠️ Junction-box labeling plus photo-documentation, a named future-serviceability technique distinct from this store's existing remote-video-acceptance protocol**: junction boxes are individually labeled by the electrician not just for the electrician's own on-the-job reference but so a future occupant/technician can identify circuit groupings years later; the stated companion practice is photographing every labeled junction box at handover so that, if an issue arises after the space is finished (the video's example: years later, under a stretch ceiling), the occupant can consult the photo album, identify exactly which section of stretch ceiling to open, and access only that specific junction box rather than removing an entire ceiling to search for it — saving both time and money.
