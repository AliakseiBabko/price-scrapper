## Kitchen / Planning Rules — Large Kitchen-Living Layout: Island vs. U-Shape-with-Bar-Tongue vs. Split L-Shape+Bar-Module

**Context**: an open kitchen-living combo where the wife wanted an L-shaped kitchen plus a separate island for extra work surface, and the husband wanted a U-shaped kitchen with an integrated bar-counter "tongue" extending the L into a U. Both rejected in favor of a third option.

- **Why the island was rejected — four independent problems, not one**: (1) **plumbing infeasibility**: a sink in the island would need its own drain, and the room's floor level couldn't be raised enough (~15cm) to route a gravity drain there without disruptive floor buildup — a sump/macerator pump was considered and rejected as an unacceptably noisy fixture for a living-room-adjacent space. (2) **ventilation conflict**: a cooktop on the island would spread cooking odors through the whole open-plan room, and a hood over it would need to be mounted low enough to conflict with the island's own task lighting. (3) **unprotected exposed corner + circulation conflict**: the island would create an exposed corner that gets scuffed/bumped in normal use (matches this store's existing curved/protruding-corner caution, extended here to a freestanding island's corner specifically), while also leaving no good sofa placement (either blocking a walkway or conflicting with the room's entry) and no viable path for a dining table. (4) **poor fit as a dining or prep surface**: kitchen-cabinet-based islands (vs. a furniture table on legs) don't allow legroom underneath, making them impractical for actual seated dining beyond a few minutes — reducing the island's real function to food-prep/quick-breakfast use only, undermining the original rationale (extra usable surface) once the other three problems are counted.
- **Why the U-shape-with-bar-tongue was rejected**: the bar "tongue" segment blocks convenient access to the cabinet module behind it (both upper and lower sections awkward to reach around the tongue), forces a long detour path (~2m out, ~0.5m to turn, ~2m back) to reach the far side of the kitchen for routine daily use, and — the decisive problem — leaves no viable location for a sofa (the tongue-adjacent zone conflicts with both the walkway from the entry and the space needed for the tongue itself), even though this layout does allow a dining table to be squeezed in with adequate (if not spacious) clearances.
- **Chosen solution — split the L-shaped kitchen and the bar counter into two physically separate modules along different walls**, rather than one continuous run: the L-shaped work section covers cooking/prep with more than enough surface area on its own; a separate, freestanding bar module sits on an adjacent wall purely for quick drinks/snacks, not full meals. **Stated benefits, addressing every rejected option's specific problem**: direct, unobstructed entry-to-any-module access (no bar-tongue detour); enough dedicated floor space to fit a real dining table with stuff-under-the-table legroom and workable (if modest) chair clearance on all sides; the split bar module's own footprint incidentally shields the room's one exposed exterior corner (explicitly credited as a bonus, not the original design goal); and a sofa fits comfortably alongside the bar module, oriented toward the TV, with no conflict against any of the other zones.
- **Reusable planning principle, stated explicitly by the source as the point of the whole example**: on a sufficiently large open-plan kitchen-living space, don't default to one continuous kitchen run (L, U, or island-extended) — splitting distinct functions (cooking/prep vs. casual bar seating) into physically separate modules along different walls can simultaneously solve circulation, furniture-placement, and corner-protection problems that a single continuous layout can't solve all at once. The source frames this as an "obvious in hindsight" insight that neither the client, nor the kitchen-company's own prior designer, had considered before this redesign.
