## Rooms/Zones — Bedroom Typology (New Distinction)

The speaker opens with an explicit four-way bedroom typology used to
structure a house's own bedroom hierarchy — not previously recorded in
this store:

- **Master bedroom** ("мастер-спальня" / master bedroom) — the owners'
  own bedroom, always designed to the highest comfort tier, ideally with
  its own ensuite bathroom and wardrobe/dressing room. The speaker states
  he tries to include at least one master bedroom in every project, and
  increasingly multiple master bedrooms even in mid-size houses, as
  client demand for this has grown "every year."
- **"Просто спальня" (ordinary/secondary bedroom)** — belongs to
  household members other than the owners; typically the room with the
  most turnover of occupants over a house's lifetime ("spальня на
  минималках" — a minimal-comfort-tier bedroom by comparison).
- **Children's bedroom** — explicitly flagged as its own separate topic,

- **Guest bedroom** — explicitly described as **multifunctional**: not a

## Planning Rules — Room Proportions

- **Two workable proportion types for a bedroom**: (1) **a square room**
  — works especially well with a king-size bed (stated as 2m×2m in plan,
  itself roughly square), since a square bed footprint sits naturally
  inside a square room ("similar figures," an architectural-composition
  argument). A square room reads best at **larger sizes**; at smaller
  sizes it's worse for lighting/zoning, because daylight from one wall
  alone leaves dark alcoves — unless the room is wide enough to fit
  **three windows on one wall**, whose combined light-front can reach
  even the bed. (2) **A rectangle with a mild aspect ratio, ideally
  around 3:4** — close to (though not exactly) the golden ratio
  (φ≈1.618). Recommended as the **general-purpose default** for an
  average-size bedroom; the pure-square option is reserved for
  large/premium projects (>1000m² houses), not the average case.
- **Elongated-bedroom window-placement rule**: a good elongated bedroom
  has its windows along the **long** wall, so the light-front covers
  nearly the whole room. A **bad** elongated bedroom has windows only on
  a **short end wall** — the light-front then covers only half the room,
  leaving the other half permanently dark. Practical implication: when a
  facade-driven window layout conflicts with interior comfort, orient
  windows to the room's longest interior wall, not just to satisfy
  exterior facade rhythm.
- **Traditional-architecture window-rhythm convention**: historically,
  facade windows were spaced at equal intervals in a straight row ("like
  a row of soldiers") — the interval between a room's own window and its
  neighboring room's window matched the room's own internal window
  spacing. Recorded as historical context, not a modern hard rule.

## Quantities / Measurements — Historic Window-Height Formula (New)

A traditional-architecture geometric method for sizing a bedroom window's
height, tied to room depth — **not previously recorded in this store**:

1. Take the room's depth (worked example: 4m).
2. Set the window sill at **0.9m above finished floor** (a separate,
   already-corroborated figure elsewhere in this store for switch
   height, here applied to sill height instead).
3. From the room's far bottom corner (opposite the window wall), draw a
   line at a **30° angle** up toward the window wall.
4. Where that line intersects the window's vertical plane sets the
   window's **top edge** — for the 4m-depth worked example, this yields
   a window height of **roughly 1.5m**.

The speaker recommends **not deviating from this proportion** for a
standard/average house, on the reasoning that it's a centuries-tested
formula every furniture-maker and tradesperson already understands
("проверены столетиями"). `single-account`, no external code citation —
architect's own stated convention, though framed as long-established
practice rather than a personal invention.

## Rules / Heuristics — Functional Zoning Model for a Modern Bedroom

States an explicit "maximum desired" functional-zone checklist for a
contemporary bedroom (context: private house, master-bedroom tier):

- Bed zone (double/king bed)
- TV zone (a TV viewable while lying in bed)
- Wardrobe/dressing zone
- Own ensuite bathroom ("свои удобства")
- **Soft/lounge zone** — armchair(s), ideally two, plus a coffee table,
  for dressing, reading, or tablet/phone use away from the bed itself

**Historical aside, new to this store**: 18th-19th century (Georgian-era
English architecture specifically named) bedrooms instead included a
**fireplace zone** — visible on Georgian building facades as unusually
tall, prominent chimneys. A single pair of chimney flues could heat up
to **16 bedrooms** across two floors via distributed flue routing to each
room, one fireplace per bedroom. Recorded as an obsolete historical
precedent (central heating has replaced this entirely), not a modern
technique, but genuinely new context for this store.

## Rules / Heuristics — Door Placement and Swing

- **Bedroom door recommended to swing inward, not outward** —
  corroborates the existing 12-item-sequence source (`hVFmcw1H2Rk`)
  independently, from a different practitioner. Stated reasoning here is
  distinct and additive: a shared hallway serving multiple bedroom doors
  typically doesn't have room for two outward-swinging doors to open
  without colliding — outward-swing would require a corridor at least
  **~1.85m wide** to avoid interference between adjacent doors' swing
  arcs; inward-swing avoids this and allows a narrower corridor. Notes
  explicitly that client preference varies here ("но тут разные точки
  зрения... заказчики просят делать по разному"), so this is a
  recommendation, not an absolute rule.

## Rules / Heuristics — Bed Placement Relative to Windows

- **Ideal**: position the bed so the **light source is not behind/at the
  head of the bed** — avoid a headboard directly under or against a
  window.
- **Acceptable fallback**: window to the **side** of the bed rather than
  behind the headboard. In this configuration, **minimum clearance from
  the bed to the side wall: 800mm**, reducible to **700mm** if 800mm
  genuinely doesn't fit. This numerically corroborates (independently)
  the existing 12-item-sequence source's bed-clearance figures
  (50-60cm minimum/ideal side clearance in general, though that source's
  figure is about walkway clearance rather than this source's
  wall-adjacent minimum specifically — related but not identical
  measurements, both recorded distinctly).

## Rules / Heuristics — Master Bedroom Layout (Wardrobe + Ensuite Split)

- **Standard master-bedroom partitioning approach**: a single partition
  wall splits off a combined wardrobe+ensuite block into two doors side
  by side — one door to the wardrobe (hanging storage), one door
  (recommended to also open inward) to the ensuite bathroom.
- **Ensuite fixture sequencing preference**: entering the ensuite should
  give access to the **sink first** (recommends the door swing/layout
  support this), then toilet along a far wall, then bathtub/shower.
- **⚠️ Critical soundproofing rule for ensuite placement**: never place
  bathroom plumbing fixtures against the **thin partition wall shared
  with an adjacent bedroom** — a standard 120mm brick or 100mm
  gypsum-block (пазогребневые) partition has effectively no sound
  isolation, so plumbing noise (flushing, running water) would be
  clearly audible in the neighboring bedroom. Where two bedrooms with
  ensuites are adjacent, prefer routing the noisy fixtures against a
  **load-bearing 380mm brick wall** instead — thick enough to also carry
  ventilation/utility routing cleanly, with genuine sound isolation as a
  side benefit. A concrete, checkable placement rule not previously
  recorded in this store's plumbing/soundproofing content.
- **Master bedroom bed placement**: push the bed as close to the window
  as practical (freeing floor space on the entry side of the room for a
  desk or lounge zone), leaving the room's entry-side zone free for
  either (a) a work desk with **daylight falling over the left shoulder**
  for a right-handed occupant (the speaker explicitly flags this is
  reversed for a left-handed client) — the same left-daylight/right-hand
  logic already recorded elsewhere in this store for kids'-room desks,
  now corroborated for an adult bedroom desk too — or (b) a soft lounge
  zone (coffee table + two armchairs).
- **TV placement in a long/elongated master bedroom**: when the room is
  long enough, a **decorative drywall partition box** (typically finished
  to match the room's interior design) is built mid-room to both mount
  the TV and visually/acoustically divide a "public" zone (TV/lounge,
  lit by one window) from a "private" sleep zone (lit by a second,
  separate window) — a room-zoning technique using a non-structural
  partition specifically to split window light between two functional
  zones, not just for privacy.

## Rules / Heuristics — Suite Bedroom (Distinct From Master Bedroom)

New distinction, not previously recorded in this store: a **"suite
bedroom"** (from hospitality/hotel terminology, e.g. "family suite") adds
a genuinely separate living-room-like space to the bedroom — sofa,
armchair, sometimes a mini-kitchen — functioning as a self-contained
mini-apartment a guest/occupant "never has to leave." Distinguished
explicitly from a master bedroom by this extra room, not just extra
square footage. Framed as a premium-segment/hospitality-grade layout,
relevant mainly to high-end private houses or hotel-style guest
accommodation.

- **Typical suite layout for an elongated space**: divided into three
  zones — (1) an entry zone functioning as a mini living room (TV, sofa,
  possible small work area), (2) a short connecting corridor with the
  ensuite bathroom along one side, (3) the large bedroom zone itself
  (king bed + bench/ottoman + its own soft seating).
- **Suite bathroom note**: a **double sink is described as the absolute
  minimum standard for a master/suite-tier ensuite bathroom** — ideally
  also both a bathtub AND a separate shower cabin (for choice), plus
  toilet and bidet. The wardrobe/dressing area in this configuration is
  often **P-shaped/U-shaped** ("п-образная"), positioned as the
  connecting element between the suite's entry zone and its bathroom.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Bad and "Tolerable" Bedroom Layouts

- **⚠️ Pass-through wardrobe directly connecting bedroom to bathroom via
  two side-by-side doors is flagged as genuinely bad, not just
  suboptimal** ("дико неудобная вещь" / "wildly inconvenient thing") —
  the speaker states he has never encountered a real house where this
  layout worked well. **Preferred fix**: make the wardrobe itself the
  pass-through space leading to the bathroom (one continuous route:
  bedroom → wardrobe → bathroom), rather than two separate doors
  branching directly off the bedroom. If the entry constraint forces two
  separate doors off the bedroom anyway, use only **one** wardrobe (not
  two flanking the bathroom entry) and keep the bathroom door itself
  clearly separated from the bed's sightline/position, extending the
  single wardrobe along the wall rather than duplicating it.
- **⚠️ "Exotic" bad layout: a windowless, spotlight-only wardrobe zone
  running the full depth of the room**, positioned so it becomes a dark,
  unlit-by-daylight band on the building's own facade (an obviously
  awkward, elongated dark zone visible from outside). Recommendation:
  plan the wardrobe as its own separate room (possibly windowless by
  design, since it doesn't need daylight) rather than letting it become
  an accidental dark extension of the bedroom's own footprint.
- **⚠️ Tight-squeeze wardrobe-at-entry mistake, with a quantified fix**:
  fitting a sliding wardrobe directly beside the bedroom's entry door in
  a small bedroom can leave as little as **0.4-0.5m** of clearance next
  to it — "looks acceptable on the floor plan, is genuinely uncomfortable
  in reality." **Fix specific to new-build private houses** (not
  retrofit-friendly for an existing apartment): recess/build the wardrobe
  into the wall itself, recovering roughly **70-80cm** of usable
  clearance versus a surface-mounted unit. General takeaway: avoid
  placing a wardrobe immediately beside the bed itself when avoidable; a
  built-in wardrobe positioned right at the entry (rather than
  bed-adjacent) is called out as a comparatively good alternative.
