## Planning Rules — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Kitchen-living-dining minimum area, stated as a personal standard**:
  a combined kitchen-living-dining space of 24m² is "always" called
  insufficient by this practitioner — his stated minimum is "from ~30m²"
  for the three functions (cook, dine, lounge) to coexist comfortably.
  A distinct, more specific numeric threshold than the ~25m² figure
  already on file from Karen Karapetyan (`YT_NWLckaXH1_U`) for a
  "full furniture set" — both point the same direction (developer-
  standard combined kitchen-livings often undersized) but with somewhat
  different thresholds, worth citing both rather than collapsing to one.
- **⚠️ Two-bathroom rationale (corroborates Karapetyan, `YT_frt8tQOrjiM`,
  same session)**: with only one bathroom, a growing family (here:
  planning for a future child) will queue to wash — split into two
  spaces (one with a full bath, one with a shower) specifically to avoid
  this. Worked example: moved a partition to enlarge a 2.4m² WC into a
  space fitting a shower pan (curtain or glass), toilet, and an 80cm
  sink; the original bath moved slightly toward the entry hall to keep
  the primary bathroom's remaining footprint (bath + sink + a large
  storage cabinet).
- **Washing-machine-in-bathroom critique, with a fallback technique**:
  a washing machine inside the bathroom is criticized as making the
  room purely utilitarian rather than a genuine relaxation space; stated
  preference is removing it from the bathroom entirely if any other
  placement is possible. When no alternative placement exists (as in
  this project), the fallback is concealing it behind a curtain rather
  than a hinged door — curtains avoid the swing-clearance problem a
  hinged door has near an adjacent fixture (here: a door would hit the
  sink) and are cited as a broader concealment technique (searchable
  under "shelves/appliances hidden behind curtains").
- **80cm sink recommended over standard narrow developer sinks**,
  specifically to have usable counter edge/rim space around the basin —
  standard developer-installed sinks are called out as too narrow for
  this.
- **Shallow (20cm-deep) recessed wall cabinet technique**: a narrow
  20cm-deep cabinet run along a bathroom wall conceals supply/drain
  pipework to the toilet behind a fixed panel section, while the
  remainder of the same run opens as real storage (bathroom supplies,
  ironing equipment) — a slim-depth storage technique for a space too
  tight for a standard-depth cabinet.
- **⚠️ Entry-hall storage sizing rule, shoe cabinet**: a narrow shoe
  cabinet **35cm deep** fits one row of shoes per shelf (bottom shelves)
  with upper shelves usable for bags/hats/scarves/gloves — stated as the
  standard useful depth for footwear storage specifically.
- **Entry-hall storage sizing rule, coat/outerwear closet**: a **60cm-
  deep** closet opposite the entry, with two hanging rods (upper and
  lower level), is the standard depth needed to hang outerwear on
  clothes hangers without the door catching.
- **Flush/handleless door technique for entry storage** — both the shoe
  and coat closets built with doors flush to the wall plane (same
  material as the wall where possible, no visible handles/hardware) to
  achieve a minimalist, "nothing protrudes" look explicitly requested by
  this project's clients.
- **⚠️ Hallway mirror as a deliberate visual-distraction device**: placing
  a mirror along an unavoidable corridor stretch (this practitioner
  states he generally dislikes corridors but couldn't eliminate one in
  this layout) gives the eye something decorative to focus on,
  offsetting a corridor's visual monotony/multiple-door clutter, rather
  than being purely a functional grooming mirror.
- **Entry pouf/ottoman with a small ledge**, for sitting to put on shoes
  and for setting down keys/gloves/a bag on arrival — called a near-
  mandatory entry element for this reason, not purely decorative.
- **⚠️ Bedroom/kids-room placement swap, age-dependent rule with stated
  mechanism**: place the **master bedroom closer to the living/common
  area** and the **child's room farther away**, specifically because
  sound passes easily through thin interior partitions — a parents'
  evening gathering/party near the living room is less likely to disturb
  a sleeping/settling child if the child's room is the more distant one.
  **Explicit caveat for teenagers**: once children become teenagers, the
  practitioner's stated preference flips — placing their room closer to
  the entry is then preferred, so they and friends don't have to
  traverse the whole apartment to reach it. A genuinely age-staged
  placement rule, not a fixed default.
- **Built-in wardrobe wall-uniformity technique**: when a partition is
  reconfigured to fit a deep (600mm) hanging wardrobe against a bedroom
  wall, extend the wardrobe's facade the full length of that wall (even
  past the actual storage cavity) so the wall reads as one monolithic,
  uniform surface — directly serves a "keep everything visually calm and
  uncluttered" client brief.
- **Console/vanity-table-facing-window technique**: placing a slim
  console/makeup table with a small mirror directly opposite a window
  creates layered depth of view (console → window → outside view) and
  benefits from the window's natural light on the table surface.

## Kitchen-Living — Durable Facts & Rules

- **⚠️ Corner ("Г-образная"/L-shaped) kitchen critique, with a concrete
  alternative**: a corner kitchen layout is called one of the least
  favored configurations — described as reading visually as "stacked
  boxes" that press on the room with their bulk, and its corner storage
  mechanisms (carousel/lazy-susan-style pull-outs) are called both
  inconvenient in practice and expensive. **Alternative offered**: a
  single linear kitchen run along one wall, paired with a separate
  free-standing "dresser"/sideboard ("комод") element as secondary
  storage/counter space — avoids the dead corner entirely, at lower
  hardware cost.
- **Kitchen-near-window placement rule, with rationale**: place the
  kitchen work zone as close to the window as possible, since it's the
  most constantly-used zone in the space — critiques the common
  developer default of placing the kitchen at the darkest, window-
  furthest end of a combined kitchen-living room (forcing artificial
  light nearly always on).
- **Dresser/sideboard as a secondary kitchen-storage element, worked
  technique**: a free-standing dresser (visually distinct finish from
  the kitchen cabinetry) holds overflow items that don't fit the main
  cabinet run, doubles as counter space for small appliances (kettle,
  coffee machine, toaster), and stores bread/napkins/dishware — framed
  as both a functional fix and a positive aesthetic variety element
  (deliberately not matching the kitchen finish).
- **⚠️ Axis-alignment layout technique**: align a kitchen dresser and the
  dining table on one visual axis extending into the adjoining corridor,
  to visually elongate the space; a second axis perpendicular to it
  crosses at the dining table, marking where an overhead light fixture
  should hang — a compositional device for tying a layout together, not
  purely functional.

## Living Room — Durable Facts & Rules

- **No corner sofas in a small living space** — stated flatly: corner
  sofas "eat all the air and openness" of a compact room.
- **⚠️ Deliberate airiness technique, "leave some space unused"**: use
  visually light furniture (no bulky legs/solid bases) so sight-lines
  pass under/around pieces, and pull the sofa away from the wall to
  leave a gap — explicitly recommends *not* using that gap for more
  storage, instead placing plants, a leaning picture frame, or a small
  bookshelf, or leaving it genuinely empty — framed as a deliberate
  rule-break against the instinct to maximize every corner, in service
  of a feeling of spaciousness.
