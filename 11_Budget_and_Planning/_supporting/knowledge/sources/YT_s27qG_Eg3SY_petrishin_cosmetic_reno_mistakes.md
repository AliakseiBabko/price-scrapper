## Mistakes / Warnings — Real Hidden-Defect Discoveries on This Object

1. **Developer protective film hidden behind curtains — real unplanned
   labor cost**: window film left by the original developer, coated in
   accumulated dirt, paint, and filler dust, took roughly 4-5 unplanned
   hours to clean across 3 windows in one room alone — a real hidden-scope
   labor-hours data point most estimators wouldn't catch without removing
   curtains first.
2. **Window-reveal ("откос") micro-cracks at the plastic-frame junction**
   found only after curtain removal — remediation sequence: open up the
   crack, fill, wait to dry, sand, possibly re-fill, then paint — framed
   explicitly as added time, not just added material.
3. **Baseboard-replacement footprint mismatch, only discoverable after
   removal**: removing an old thick wooden baseboard revealed a 7mm-1cm
   gap between wall and floor that the old baseboard's thicker profile had
   been hiding; the planned replacement (MDF or polyurethane) is
   significantly thinner and won't hide the same gap — an invisible sizing
   problem until the old baseboard is actually removed, with no fix
   decided yet in this video.
4. **Door-frame foam-gap defect with a numeric correction rule**: an
   existing door frame's foam gap measured 7-8cm, when the correct spec is
   1-1.5cm max — excess foam lets the door leaf loosen/rattle over time
   from drafts and use. **Rule stated**: a door opening should be only
   8-10cm larger than the door leaf itself. Fixing an oversized opening
   requires narrowing it with drywall, local plastering/filling, and
   repainting, and risks the new door casing ("наличник"/"обналичка") not
   fully covering the narrowed opening — a real added-scope example.
5. **Ceiling molding/cornice crack found only by physical inspection**: an
   already-cracked polystyrene ("пенопласт") cornice was found by climbing
   a ladder and testing by touch/sound, not by a visual walkthrough alone
   — can mean re-installing cornice throughout the whole apartment, an
   easy-to-miss cost driver at estimate time.
6. **Wall-filler ("шпаклёвка") multi-cycle drying requirement for deep
   holes**: deep old picture-hook holes (found hidden behind removed
   paintings) can require multiple fill-dry-sand cycles depending on
   depth — genuinely time-consuming even on a small area, and painting
   can't proceed until every layer has fully dried and been sanded.
7. **UV-degraded plastic window hardware and door-draft whistling**: window
   fittings cracked from prolonged UV exposure, plus a whistling draft
   noise when the entry door opens — both required a specialist plastic-
   window technician to replace weatherstripping/seals, lubricate
   mechanisms, and replace handles — a real added cost/time line item
   easy to miss without physically testing hardware.

## Mistakes / Warnings — General Secondary-Housing ("вторичка") Capital-Renovation Risk Checklist

8. **Door-frame demolition plaster-crack risk, with a pre-contract
   self-check recommendation**: removing screwed-in door frames often
   causes surrounding plaster to crack/detach from vibration — especially
   in Stalin-era and Khrushchev-era buildings — potentially forcing a full
   re-plaster the original estimate never accounted for. **Rule**: tap-test
   plaster around door frames yourself (or have your own inspector do it)
   before signing a smeta, to avoid this becoming an unplanned cost.
9. **Tile-adhesion tap-test, with a stated professional practice of
   bringing a rotary hammer to the estimate visit**: tile that looks
   fine can still be detached from its substrate underneath — tap-testing
   reveals a hollow sound where adhesion has failed, distinguishable from
   the solid sound of well-bonded tile. The source's own practice: bring a
   rotary hammer/hammer drill specifically to test-drill in suspect areas
   during the estimate visit, to avoid an unpleasant surprise for the
   client later — in this specific apartment, detached tile was
   deliberately left unaddressed since the scope was a minimum-budget
   cosmetic job, but the source is explicit this is a judgment call, not
   a rule to always skip it.
10. **Mixed aluminum-copper wiring connection risk from a prior partial
    electrical renovation**: when only some rooms in a unit previously had
    their wiring replaced, undiscovered aluminum-to-copper splices can
    exist behind walls; demolishing/disturbing a supposedly untouched
    circuit risks losing power to that room mid-project. **Key principle
    stated explicitly**: a renovation can only genuinely be called
    "cosmetic only" if a full capital renovation (including electrical,
    plumbing, and heating replacement) was already done at least once in
    that unit's history — attempting a cosmetic-only renovation on a unit
    that never had one carries real hidden-systems risk.
11. **Corroded mixer/faucet wall connection ("водорозетка") failure
    mode**: an old, corroded/seized wall connection point can itself
    break when removing an old mixer/faucet, especially with no isolation
    valve/cover plate present — remediation can require removing tile and
    re-piping (a full resolder if the line is polypropylene) — described
    as a genuinely large, easy-to-underestimate cost/scope item.
12. **Ceiling whitewash ("побелка") vs. paint — a diagnostic distinction
    for old (Stalin/Khrushchev-era) ceilings**: chalk-based whitewash
    cannot simply be painted over directly the way an existing painted
    ceiling sometimes can — it must be fully washed off, and the old
    filler layer underneath it often needs removing too. Relevant when
    assessing whether a ceiling can be "just recoated" versus needs full
    rework.
13. **Furniture-packing/moving as its own explicit cost/scope line item**:
    clients commonly underestimate what furniture will actually remain
    during a renovation (initially say "nothing stays," then a couch,
    wardrobe, and chairs turn out to still be present) — recommends
    explicitly defining and pricing a furniture-packing-and-moving line in
    the smeta upfront with the client and estimator, to avoid a later
    conflict over unplanned labor.

## Design Concept — Window-Reveal Color, an Open Stylistic Question (not a settled rule)

14. **White vs. wall-color window reveals — a real design-preference
    discussion, not a technical rule**: the practitioner's own historical
    client-facing pitch was that a white reveal makes a window look
    larger; he notes a roughly 8-year Moscow market trend toward reveals
    matching the wall/paint color instead, and the crew (including the
    designer, Natasha) debate the tradeoff on camera without settling on
    one answer for this particular room — framed explicitly as a matter of
    current taste/fashion, not a durability or technical consideration,
    and the video asks viewers to vote in the comments.
