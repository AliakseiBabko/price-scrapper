## Mistakes / Warnings — Avoid Curvilinear/L-Shaped Room Geometry

**⚠️ Explicit, direct architect statement**: an L-shaped or otherwise
curvilinear bedroom (or any room) is "дико неудобно" (wildly
inconvenient) — not just for furniture placement, but for interior
design generally: working with angled corners and uneven lighting
distribution across a non-rectangular footprint is described as a
known, common pain point every interior designer recognizes. Rectangular
rooms (the video's own bedrooms are drawn as clean rectangles, marked
with a diagonal cross specifically to visually demonstrate "proper
geometry") are recommended by default; avoid non-rectangular room shapes
whenever the floor plan allows a choice. A genuinely new, direct
recommendation for this store — corroborates, from the opposite
direction, why this store's existing irregular-geometry *coping*
techniques (see
[[06_Small_Bedroom/analysis/Space_Recovery_and_Proportion_Technique|Space
Recovery and Proportion Technique]]) are corrections for a suboptimal
starting condition, not a neutral design choice.

## Quantities / Measurements — Door-to-Partition Offset

**Minimum door-to-partition-corner offset**: at minimum, the door-leaf
casing's own width (typically 60-70mm) — but the architect's own
practice defaults to roughly **100mm** for margin, and states that
following "the book"/classic convention specifically calls for **120mm**,
since that figure is a multiple of standard brick module dimensions. A
concrete, checkable clearance figure for positioning a door near an
adjoining partition corner, not previously recorded in this store at
this specificity.

## Rules / Heuristics — Corroboration: Walk-Through Wardrobe as the Bathroom's Own Access Route

**Directly corroborates `AcibYG4jtbs`'s recommended fix for the "bad
pass-through wardrobe" mistake** (two separate doors off the bedroom):
this video explicitly praises a wardrobe that **is itself** the
pass-through route from bedroom to ensuite bathroom (a single continuous
path: bedroom → wardrobe → bathroom, one door off the bedroom) as "no
less good a solution than a separate wardrobe with its own entrance."
Stated reasoning, genuinely new detail beyond video 1's version of this
rule: **fewer doors preserves more usable wall length** for furniture,
art, or lighting fixtures that a door swing zone would otherwise block,
and a **single centered door** creates visual/interior-design symmetry
(furniture/art can be placed symmetrically around it) that multiple
doors can't. This is real corroboration from the same channel/architect
(not an independent second source — same practitioner, two videos in one
series), but adds concrete reasoning the first video didn't state.

## Rules / Heuristics — Client Communication: Showroom-Scale Distortion Caution

**⚠️ A concrete client-communication technique, new to this store**:
clients often fall in love with an oversized bed or sofa in a furniture
showroom (a real example cited: a genuinely huge room, "340 m²") without
registering that the same piece will look and feel disproportionate once
placed in their actual bedroom (a real comparison made: a 15-18m²
bedroom). The architect's stated practice: **show the client the true
furniture footprint honestly and early** (e.g. draw a true king-size bed
at its real 2m×2m-or-larger footprint, not a compressed schematic
rectangle) specifically to prevent this scale mismatch from surfacing
only after purchase. A reusable planning/communication heuristic for
anyone selecting furniture before a room is finished, not house-specific.

## Rules / Heuristics — Built-In Furniture Consolidation

**Built-in furniture (встроенная мебель) is called out as still
under-used in Russia despite solving many layout problems** — consolidating
several functions (TV zone, storage, wardrobe/pantry-style shelving) into
one unified furniture block against a single wall reduces the number of
separate doors and clutter fragments a room accumulates otherwise. Framed
as a general design-efficiency principle, applicable to any bedroom
regardless of house/apartment context.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Don't Add an Unjustified Fixture

**A guest bathroom/shower cabin should only be built if a genuine,
justified guest-room need exists** — the architect explicitly
second-guesses his own worked example's guest shower cabin, stating that
without an actual guest room in the plan, that shower stall isn't
functionally justified and the square footage would be better spent
simply enlarging the adjacent bathroom instead. A reusable decision
heuristic: don't add a fixture/room because a floor plan "has space for
it" — justify it against an actual, named use case first, or reclaim
the area for an already-justified space.

## Rules / Heuristics — Lighting-Direction Standards Disagreement (New, Unresolved)

**⚠️ A genuine, source-flagged tension between two lighting-direction
conventions**, worth recording as an open disagreement rather than a
settled rule: the "classic" convention (natural light falling from the
**left** for a right-handed occupant, already recorded on this page from
video 1 of this batch and independently from this store's kids'-room
desk-placement rule) is contrasted here against an **older Soviet
building-standard convention** the architect explicitly names, which
instead calls for natural light to fall from the **right** side for
(implicitly) the same right-handed-majority reasoning. The architect
notes this Soviet-standard convention is worth remembering even though
it conflicts with the "classic" rule he otherwise follows in his own
practice. **Not resolved either way** — flagged here as an unresolved
disagreement between two named conventions (a modern/international
"classic" rule vs. an older Soviet regulatory convention), not a
correction to the existing left-daylight rule.

## Rules / Heuristics — Bathroom-Over-Kitchen Utility Stacking (Corroboration)

**Vertical utility-stack alignment**: this worked example deliberately
places the second-floor bathroom directly above the ground-floor
kitchen, and the master ensuite above the boiler-room/technical zone —
stated reasoning is that water supply, drainage, and ventilation runs
all share the same vertical technical channel this way. A general
plumbing/HVAC-routing efficiency principle already established elsewhere
in this store's engineering content (not a new fact on its own, recorded
here only as an independent-episode corroboration, not a new store
entry).
