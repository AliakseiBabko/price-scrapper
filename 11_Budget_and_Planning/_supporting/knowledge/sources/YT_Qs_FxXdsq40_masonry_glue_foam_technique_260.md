## Materials / Walls-Ceilings — Foam-Glue Block Masonry Technique

- **Foam-glue block bonding vs. classic mortar bonding**: one foam-glue canister covers roughly the same wall area as 1-2 bags of cement-sand mortar mix, but is far less logistically burdensome — 100 canisters fit in a car trunk, whereas 100 bags of mortar need a van, paid hoisting/movers, and building-management-mandated stairwell cleanup. `single-account`, company's own stated adoption trajectory (self-promotional framing).
- **First course is always laid on cement-sand mortar, never foam-glue** — even in a foam-glue-bonded wall — because raw floor slabs are never perfectly level; the first course must be leveled on a mortar bed to establish a true reference plane before switching to foam-glue for all subsequent courses. Corroborates/extends this store's existing "first course on cement-sand mortar" rule.
- **Verify the first course (level, vertical plane, and 90° corners with a framing square) even if a senior/experienced person laid it personally** — never skip verification regardless of who did the work.
- **Material takeoff**: calculate exact block count from the project's wall area, add a 10% waste/buffer margin.
- **Offset (running-bond) coursing is the most stable pattern** — requires starting every second course with a half-block (pre-cut in advance; measure and cut at the half-length mark, then de-dust the cut face).
- **Wall-tie/hanger spacing to an adjoining structural wall: every second course** — too infrequent leaves the wall wobbly; too frequent wastes time/screws for no added benefit.
- **Use flexible ties/hangers to an adjoining wall, never rigid angle brackets** — flexible ties absorb minor building settlement movement without cracking the partition; rigid brackets don't.
- **Chisel a groove into the block face for a surface-mounted hanger's screw heads** (trace the hanger outline in pencil, cut with hand saw + hammer + chisel, then de-dust) — with a thin 1-2mm foam-glue joint, protruding screw heads would otherwise prevent the next block from seating flush. Consistently cut into the half-block specifically (since it recurs every other course in this bond pattern).
- **Clean dust from and prime both mating surfaces (block-row top and adjoining wall) before laying each course** — skipping primer means the blocks won't bond.
- **Foam-glue working-time constraint: ~3 minutes** to correct/adjust a misplaced block before it sets — the foam expands and cures fast under a block's weight. Shake the canister before dispensing.
- **Check each course's plane with a level and correct with shims** — block-to-block manufacturing tolerance can vary a couple mm even within the same batch; remove leveling wedges only after the foam-glue has fully cured.
- **Corner-joint technique, two-step per course**: (1) dry-fit — align a whole block flush with the wall's outer edge (not the corner itself, to preserve coursing/vertical alignment), measure the remaining gap to the corner, cut a block to that exact size, trial-fit and level-check it; (2) only then lay both blocks for real with foam-glue.
- **Where partition walls intersect, interlace ("bond") the block courses between them every other row** for added structural strength at the junction.
- **Top-of-wall-to-ceiling gap treatment**: if the gap between the finished partition top and the structural ceiling is ≤2.5cm, foam it directly with standard mounting foam (**explicitly distinct from block-glue-foam** — don't confuse the two products). If larger, cut blocks down to reduce the gap to that tolerance first, then foam it. Also foam any other visible gaps around the partition's perimeter (e.g. against a structural column).

## Planning Rules / Client-Facing QC Acceptance Checklist (24 hours after build)

- **Verify square corners in at least 3 places with a framing square**, once the adhesive has cured (~24 hours) — the corner adjacent to where a kitchen will stand is flagged as one of the most important corners in a unit, needing a true 90°.
- **A non-90° corner is not automatically a masonry defect**: if the developer's own original walls aren't parallel to begin with, a wall built between them will necessarily land non-90° at one end regardless of build quality — that specific deviation gets corrected later during plastering, not blamed on the masonry crew.
- **Wall-flatness tolerance, explicit numbers**: check with a straightedge in multiple spots. **Gaps up to 5-7mm are within acceptable tolerance** (correctable during plastering). **Gaps of 15mm or more should never be accepted — insist the crew rebuild that wall section.** `confirmed`, concrete client-facing acceptance threshold.
- **Check wall plumb (lean) with a bubble level**, reading which direction the wall leans by which way the bubble shifts.
- **Do not sledgehammer-test a finished wall's strength** — explicitly rejected as a real QC method despite being a commonly-requested demo. Judge strength qualitatively instead: the wall stands straight, has no cracks or visible damage, and shows visible foam-glue joints between blocks.
