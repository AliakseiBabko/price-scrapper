## HVAC / Ventilation — Whole-Building Airflow-Balance Design Methodology

- **⚠️ Core design starting point: size total required airflow using single air-exchange-per-hour ("однократный воздухообмен") for residential comfort** — the entire room volume should turn over once per hour. Worked example: 3m ceiling height × 87m² ground floor ≈ 260 m³/h; 50m² second floor × 3m ≈ 150 m³/h; **whole-building total 410 m³/h**. `confirmed`, general sizing heuristic, checkable arithmetic.
- **⚠️ Never tie a kitchen hood's dedicated exhaust riser into the same natural-ventilation stack as the kitchen's general/background exhaust** — code prohibits it even though many installers do it anyway. The general kitchen natural-exhaust stack and the toilet/bathroom natural-exhaust stack are, by default code convention across Russian apartment buildings, two separate risers ("2 канала"), each serving only its own room type — a hood-specific riser must be a third, independent run. `confirmed`, code-adjacent rule, `single-account` (not cited to a specific code number).
- **⚠️ Airflow-balance table methodology — the actual design technique, worked in full**: build a room-by-room supply/exhaust spreadsheet before drawing any ducting. Rule: "dirty zone" rooms (bathroom/toilet, kitchen, utility rooms) get exhaust only; "clean zone" rooms (bedroom, living room) get surplus supply air, sized so their supply exceeds their own exhaust need by exactly the amount the dirty zones need to draw from adjacent rooms — this produces a pressure gradient (slight negative pressure in dirty zones, slight positive in clean zones) so that air flows from clean into dirty zones, containing cooking/bathroom odors rather than letting them spread into living spaces. Worked numbers for the example: combined bath/toilet 50 m³/h exhaust (natural riser); kitchen natural exhaust 60 m³/h (local range-hood exhaust excluded from this balance since it runs rarely); bedroom 100 m³/h supply / 50 m³/h exhaust; walk-in closet + utility room 30 + 20 m³/h combined; living-room+kitchen combined zone gets 160 m³/h supply / 50 m³/h exhaust (no separate kitchen supply needed since the open-plan zone distributes air adequately and a slight supply surplus there also creates a positive-pressure "barrier" against cooking odors migrating room-to-room). Everything reconciles to the 260 m³/h ground-floor total. `confirmed`, complete worked methodology, directly generalizable and checkable.
- **⚠️ A fan's rated airflow is not a fixed number — it's a curve against duct-network resistance ("сопротивление сети"), measured in pascals**: resistance depends on duct length/geometry, the count and type of fittings (bends, tees, transitions), and air velocity inside the duct. Worked example from a real unit's spec curve: rated 400 m³/h at ~100 Pa resistance, but only ~300 m³/h at 150 Pa resistance — the same physical fan delivers meaningfully less air as the duct network gets more restrictive. **Practical implication: route ducts with as few bends/branches as possible and keep the run as flat/simple as possible**, both to preserve real delivered airflow and to minimize ceiling-height loss. `confirmed`, core mechanism, generalizable to any forced-ventilation system regardless of scale.
- **⚠️ Duct velocity should stay ≤4-5 m/s in main trunk runs, and the reason is non-linear, not just noise**: resistance rises with the *square* of velocity (doubling velocity roughly quadruples resistance) — worked example: a 160mm duct carrying 300 m³/h (~4 m/s) vs. the same duct carrying 600 m³/h (~8 m/s) shows a measured 4x resistance difference for just one meter of straight duct in an online calculator. At recommended low velocities, a single meter of straight duct might cost only ~5 Pa, while the whole system's fan curve only has ~100-150 Pa of resistance budget to work with — meaning careless long/complex/high-velocity duct runs can easily exceed what any reasonably-sized fan can overcome. `confirmed`, quantified mechanism with worked comparison, generalizable and checkable.
- **Standard round galvanized-steel duct sizing convention**: manufactured in 3m lengths, standard diameter series 100/125/160/200/250/315mm etc. — a specific-enough spec to be a genuinely reusable reference point for planning duct routing/procurement. `confirmed`.
- **Exterior supply and exhaust termination points should be physically separated as far apart as the building envelope allows** (code guidance cited: ≥3m apart for larger systems, though the speaker notes even 5% air recirculation is not a serious problem for a small residential system with tight physical constraints) — minimizes exhaust air being re-drawn back into the fresh-air intake. `confirmed`, design rule with an explicit residential-scale caveat on how strictly to enforce it.
- **Grille/diffuser selection is a genuine sizing calculation, not just an aesthetic pick**: manufacturers publish grille performance tables (by grille length/width for rectangular, and by diameter for round diffusers) giving resistance (Pa) and face velocity (m/s) at a given airflow. Worked example: a 1m-long, 2-slot linear grille rated for 140 m³/h at 7 Pa / 0.2 m/s (measured 0.8m out from the grille face) scales roughly proportionally when halved to 0.5m length at half the airflow (70 m³/h) — the practitioner accepted a slightly higher-than-table airflow (80 m³/h through the halved grille) as acceptably "not critical." **Round diffusers have the added advantage of being adjustable in place** (twist to open/close) as a secondary balancing mechanism beyond the whole-system dampers. `confirmed`, sizing technique with worked numeric example.
- **⚠️ Exterior wall/roof termination grilles carry disproportionately high resistance and often need duct upsizing just before the wall penetration**: worked example — a standard round exterior grille at 300 m³/h through a 160mm duct shows ~40 Pa resistance (a large fraction of a small system's total pressure budget); upsizing the duct to 200mm just before the exterior grille (with a 200mm grille) drops that same run to ~15 Pa. The supply side of the same system (410 m³/h full flow) needed a similar upsize, ending at ~25 Pa. **Practical design implication: exterior weather-grille resistance is often the single largest resistance point in an otherwise well-designed system and should be checked/sized explicitly, not treated as negligible.** `confirmed`, quantified mechanism, directly generalizable to any exterior vent termination.
- **⚠️ Fan/unit selection rule: size the whole system around the single most heavily-loaded duct path** (from exterior grille to the single most distant/most-resistive room grille — usually, but not always, the physically longest run) — not an average or a shorter/simpler path. Worked full calculation for this example: exhaust branch (410 m³/h total, minus 50+60 natural-riser exhaust = ~300 m³/h forced-exhaust load) totals 45 Pa once optimized (initially higher on 160mm duct, dropped by downsizing an underused segment to 125mm, illustrating that oversized ducts aren't automatically "safer" — right-sizing per actual segment flow matters); adding the 15 Pa exterior grille and diffuser resistance gives **~67 Pa total for the exhaust branch**; the supply branch totals **~57 Pa** (25 Pa duct run + grilles). The selected unit's spec sheet was then checked against both figures at their respective required flows (410 m³/h supply, 300 m³/h forced exhaust) to confirm it could deliver both simultaneously — supply checked out close to exact, so the exterior supply grille was upsized one step further specifically to shave its 25 Pa contribution down, since it represented nearly half the branch's total resistance. `confirmed`, complete worked selection methodology, directly generalizable and checkable arithmetic throughout.
- **⚠️ Post-installation manual balancing via duct-mounted dampers ("дроссель-клапан") at every grille is a required commissioning step, not optional** — a semi-residential-grade ventilation unit's onboard automation typically cannot independently regulate supply and exhaust to two different target flow rates; if dampers aren't manually set after installation, the exhaust side (aggregated with the natural-riser exhausts) risks pulling more total air than the supply side delivers, degrading the natural exhaust risers' performance and risking **backdraft** (reversed airflow) through them — described as "completely unacceptable." `confirmed`, commissioning/QC step with explicit failure-mode reasoning, directly generalizable and a genuinely checkable acceptance-test item for any similar system.
