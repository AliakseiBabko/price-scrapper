## Bathroom — Tub-to-Wall Joint: Two Methods Compared

- **Method A — tub pushed flush against already-finished tile ("приставная ванна")**: explicitly framed as a functional, not aesthetic, choice — recommended only for a rental unit or a maximally cheap renovation, not a design decision on its own merits.
  - **Pros**: fully DIY-installable (anyone can run a sealant bead around the perimeter once tiling is done); the tub can be swapped later without disturbing the tile at all — a real advantage specifically for a rental unit where a damaged tub needs quick replacement; doesn't require the room/tub dimensions to match exactly, since any gap is simply sealed over; works with an inexpensive clip-on plastic apron/screen.
  - **⚠️ Cons, with a named failure mechanism**: the perimeter gap is necessarily large, requiring a substantial bead of sealant to close it — that sealant **yellows over time and eventually grows mold**, requiring repeated cleaning and periodic resealing. Because the tub has rounded edges, a tiled apron can't be built cleanly against it — a plastic screen is the only realistic screen/apron option with this method (aesthetic ceiling, not a technique failure).
- **Method B — tile laid over the tub's rim ("монтаж в три стены," tub installed against three walls)**: the source's own stated exclusive practice on every project, business/premium segment only.
  - **⚠️ Pros, with a precise new numeric spec**: produces a flush, visually clean line (aesthetic advantage); the resulting vertical gap between tub and tile is only **1-2mm**, sealed with a thin sealant bead — genuinely new precision for this store, which already records the general tile-over-rim sequencing principle (`YT_hvPddB5Lc1s`, `zems_b028_door_bdc70cee`) but not this specific gap width. Because the gap is this narrow, water has nowhere to pool inside it, so — per this source — the joint **never grows mold and never needs resealing**, unlike Method A.
  - **⚠️ Cons, with a real cross-referenced consequence**: requires a genuinely skilled tiler and specific tools — a lower-skill installer risks a poor result; **the tub cannot be replaced later without demolishing the surrounding tile** — and if that exact tile is no longer sold (a real risk this round's video 6, `Zd6yryCjyhM`, covers in detail — discontinued/clearance tile lines), full retiling may be required just to replace the tub. Also more expensive labor than Method A, consistent with this method not being recommended for a rental or minimum-cost renovation.
