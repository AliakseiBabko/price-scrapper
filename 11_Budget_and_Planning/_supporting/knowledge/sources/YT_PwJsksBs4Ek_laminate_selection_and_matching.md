## Flooring — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's core sequencing rule: choose interior doors first, then laminate — never the reverse.** His stated reasoning: the pool of doors that are both visually appealing and within budget is, in his estimate, roughly 100× smaller than the pool of acceptable laminate options — so fitting laminate to an already-chosen door is easy, while fitting doors to an already-chosen laminate floor is "practically impossible." Restates and reinforces this store's existing door-before-flooring sequencing theme (already recorded from other Zemstandart sources) with an explicit magnitude claim (~100×) not previously captured.
- **Zemskov's baseboard rule: choose the baseboard immediately after the doors, in exact color match to the doors — not to the flooring.** His reasoning: the baseboard should read as a continuation of the door casing, not the floor; matching baseboard to floor color instead of door color is common but, per Zemskov, looks visibly worse — he recommends buying the baseboard from the *same manufacturer/collection* as the chosen doors specifically because "wenge" (or any named color) varies enough between manufacturers that even a careful cross-brand match will read as subtly, distractingly off.
- **Zemskov's three stated reasons flooring color is now commonly chosen in contrast to doors, not matched to them**: (1) finding a true color match is harder than finding a pleasing contrast; (2) even a well-matched color will still show a texture mismatch, since laminate and doors are made by different manufacturers with different surface textures — and laminate lies horizontal while a door stands vertical, so the same nominal color reads differently under the room's actual light; (3) once you're choosing floor/door colors, it's also worth considering furniture, which is best matched to the doors — but matching three separate categories (floor, door, furniture), each made by different manufacturers, to one exact color is per Zemskov essentially guaranteed to fail somewhere, so a deliberate contrast for the floor sidesteps this compounding-mismatch problem entirely.
- **Zemskov's "saturation matching" rule**: keep the saturation/vividness level roughly consistent across an interior's main components (a "gold oak" — natural, saturated, vivid tone — paired with a "bleached oak" — artificial, desaturated, muted tone — is, per Zemskov, an almost-guaranteed clash) — pick components of comparably natural/saturated or comparably artificial/muted character, not a mix.
- **Zemskov's laminate-board-sizing rule**: prefer laminate milled to dimensions closer to a genuine solid-wood board's smaller size (his cited classic laminate dimensions: ~130cm × ~19cm, vs. solid wood board sizes roughly 1.5× smaller — solid wood is kept smaller because larger boards warp more under humidity swings and waste more material in cutting/laying). His reasoning: laminate exists specifically to imitate solid wood/parquet's *look*, and a board sized closer to real solid-wood dimensions reads as more convincingly premium than an oversized laminate board, even though the underlying decorative pattern printed on the surface has zero connection to the material or structure underneath.
- **Zemskov's chamfered-edge (fascia/bevel) requirement — flagged as safety-relevant, not just cosmetic.** His claim: any laminate, however well made and carefully maintained, will eventually develop small gaps between boards; a chamfered edge hides this gap, while a non-chamfered (square) edge leaves the gap visible, catches a cleaning cloth, collects dirt, snags socks/tights — and, most seriously, at any crack the board edges can lift slightly at the corner, which Zemskov states is a real trip/cut injury risk for anyone walking barefoot. He also states genuinely premium flooring (solid wood included) always has a perimeter bevel, and a boardless (square-edge) product cannot achieve a premium look regardless of price.
- **Zemskov explicitly debunks the "dirt collects in the bevel groove" objection** — he calls this a myth invented by manufacturers of non-chamfered laminate specifically because their product wasn't selling, and states that in his own experience chamfered floors clean perfectly normally with no unusual dirt accumulation in the bevel line.
