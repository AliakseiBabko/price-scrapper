## Bathroom — Structure, Framing & Partition Materials (routed directly to `07_Bathroom/analysis/Structure_and_Framing.md`)

- **A different, heavier partition-block product from this store's existing silicate-tongue-and-groove-board entry**: a **tongue-and-groove ("пазогребневый"), moisture-resistant, high-density silicate BLOCK** (distinct from the thinner ~8cm "ПСП" tongue-and-groove *board* already in this page) — real dimensions **497mm (L) × 248mm (H) × 100mm (W)**, two tongues on top / groove on bottom (interlocks like Lego, tongue-and-groove on the end faces too). High compressive load capacity and good fastener-holding (rated for a heavy wall-mounted item like a kitchen cabinet). **Weighs 22.5 kg per block** — explicitly called viable for a monolithic-frame building or a private house, but **too heavy to be a realistic choice for an older khrushchevka-type panel building** (floor-load concern, not stated as a hard code prohibition). Chosen over brick specifically because brick's small unit size makes wall-building far slower for the same wall area.
- **Room-resize numbers for this specific bathroom/WC remodel, staying within the pre-existing outer wet-zone footprint**: the toilet/bath dividing wall moves 20cm, shrinking the toilet room and enlarging the bath; **1 meter is cited as the minimum comfortable toilet-room width** with a small sink and a hygienic shower fitted; net internal usable area gain **+1 m²** overall, with the outer boundary against adjacent living rooms left unchanged.
- **A concrete planning-rule threshold, corroborating this store's existing small-apartment-replanning caution from a different Sidorik source (`yt_0sJPlpi8I2U`)**: as long as a wet-zone resize stays within its pre-existing outer footprint (only moving the internal split between rooms), no formal replanning project or hidden-works-act paperwork is triggered; **only expanding the wet zone's footprint into an adjacent living room's space** requires the formal replanning project + acts-of-hidden-work process. A practical decision lever for anyone weighing whether to expand a small bathroom.
- **Foam-adhesive block-laying method (first-time use by this practitioner)**: polyurethane adhesive foam (клей-пена, brand "Irfix" — needs a dedicated professional foam gun, not a straw-nozzle canister) instead of mortar for all courses above the first. Real advantages cited: faster/no wet trades, and **no thermal bridging**, relevant specifically for exterior-wall applications. **The first course is still set in traditional masonry mortar**, specifically to level out an uneven floor slab before switching to foam for all subsequent courses. One 30-second-plus can-shake before each use is needed for full foam yield. Correction window: ±5mm horizontally within ~3 minutes of placement; a vertical shift beyond 5mm requires re-applying adhesive entirely.
- **A real, if informal, bond-strength test the practitioner ran on himself**: standing his own 95kg body weight on 3 silicate blocks glued end-to-end (foam, ~5-minute cure gaps between each) with no sign of failure; a separate test on aerated-concrete blocks glued end-to-end broke the block itself, not the foam joint, when pulled apart. Presented as a rough, non-laboratory confidence check on the foam-block bond, not a certified test.
- **Openings up to 208cm tall don't need a structural lintel when built this way**: temporary wood-board formwork supports cut blocks laid on foam directly over the opening; the foam bond alone was judged sufficiently rigid without a lintel, though the speaker still adds reinforcement "for extra safety" — see the rebar technique below.
- **Door-opening reinforcement technique, ~15 minutes per opening**: cut a groove above the door opening with a wall chaser (штроборез), embed 12mm rebar in mortar within the groove, trim flush with the block face. Stated benefit beyond safety margin: the opening can still be widened a few centimeters later by cutting into the block, since the rebar sits fully embedded in mortar rather than in the block itself.
- **Partition-to-structural-wall tie-in**: every third course is tied to the building's own exterior/structural walls using metal angle brackets (6×40mm fasteners), at three tie-in points along each partition run bordering the monolith.
- **Ceiling gap detail**: leave a 1-1.5cm gap between the top block course and the ceiling, and fill it with foam only, never mortar — mortar can't be properly packed into a gap this shape/size and will crack over time, while foam gives both rigidity and an airtight seal at that junction.
- **Cutting technique for this silicate block**: use a large angle grinder with a vacuum attachment, not a reciprocating/sabre saw (explicitly called a poor choice for this material) — cut from both faces since the blade doesn't reach full depth in one pass, and tune the grinder's cut depth to leave a few mm uncut in the center to reduce dust. Set up a dedicated cutting station in an enclosed space (this practitioner used the loggia) to contain dust to one area.
- **Layout/marking technique for precise 90° partitions**: use a 3-axis laser level with a built-in 90° reference; additionally check the laser beam against the apartment's *existing* walls with a large carpenter's square and a laser rangefinder, so new partitions land parallel/perpendicular to the existing structure too, not just square to each other. Mark chiefly on walls and ceiling (floor marks get covered by the first course) and trace lines with a straightedge for clarity; mark door-opening locations on the floor early, before the first course goes down.
- **Uneven-floor leveling technique for the first course**: set the laser level at 1m height, then use a tape measure to sound the floor at multiple points — a *smaller* reading than the 100cm base point means the floor is higher there, a *larger* reading means lower; start the first block course at the floor's highest point found this way.

## Cost Drivers / Material Prices — Silicate Block Sourcing

- **Buy blocks by the full pallet directly from the manufacturer's own website, not from a retail building-supply store** — real price comparison stated directly: **1 BYN 14 kopecks per block** (Belarusian ruble, explicitly named) factory-direct vs. **roughly 2× that price** at a retail building-supply store. Condition: factory sale is pallet-multiples only; a crane/manipulator delivery truck is needed. This project used 2 block types across 5 pallets total. USD-equivalent: ≈**$0.46/block** (trailing-6-month BYN/USD average before 2020-09-30, 2.4721 BYN/USD per `tools/pricing/currency_converter.py`; bucket-rounding convention not meaningful at this sub-dollar scale, exact figure kept). Region: Belarus level 1 (Minsk). Year: 2020.
- **Practical delivery/handling advice**: hire movers experienced with heavy material handling to carry blocks up into the apartment rather than doing it personally (the speaker frames this as standard advice he gives clients, not just his own choice) — combined with the DIY hand cart from episode 2. Also: don't stack all delivered blocks in one spot — split the pile across multiple locations in the apartment (an implicit floor-point-load consideration, not stated as a hard rule).
- **Regional demolition-labor cost driver, stated as a general observation**: demolition/rough-work labor pricing varies significantly by city tier within the same country — cited as highest in Moscow, lowest in a small provincial town, "standard" in Minsk. `single-account`, directional/qualitative only, no absolute figures given.
- **A methodological caveat on this practitioner's own cost estimates worth flagging generally**: responding to viewer disagreement over whether his estimates read as too expensive or too cheap, the speaker states he does **not** include the value of his own labor time in his on-screen materials/labor "смета" for a self-executed project — meaning any total sourced from this channel's self-managed cost figures likely understates true all-in cost by the value of the owner's own labor hours, a caveat worth carrying forward to any other Sidorik-sourced self-managed total in this store (including the khrushchevka finale case study, `yt_0sJPlpi8I2U`, worth a light cross-check).
