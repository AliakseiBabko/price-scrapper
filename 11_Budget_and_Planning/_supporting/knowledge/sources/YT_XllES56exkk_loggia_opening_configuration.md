## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

### If NOT joining the loggia — never remove the sill wall (подоконная часть)

- **Zemskov's central claim: removing the solid sill/knee wall below a balcony door-and-window unit (to install a second clear glass door purely for light) does not actually make the interior brighter.** His reasoning: the loggia's own parapet/railing (where the glazing sits) is itself opaque/non-transparent and blocks almost all the light that would otherwise pass through that lower section — so the sill wall's removal changes interior light levels by an amount the occupant won't even notice.
- **Real costs, per Zemskov, with no offsetting benefit**: two clear glass doors cost significantly more than a standard balcony-block (door + window) unit; removing the sill wall itself in a panel building (high-strength reinforced concrete) takes 2-3 days and costs tens of thousands of RUB.
- **The sill wall typically houses the radiator** — removing it forces a relocation, which costs real money (riser shutoff fee plus labor, ≥10,000 RUB with central heating) and requires **justifying the shutoff request explicitly** — Zemskov notes this rule exists because some people claim a simple valve swap but actually install an illegal water-based underfloor loop, risking flooding neighbors below. Any future inspection catching an undocumented radiator relocation means either reverting it or paying heavily — **no authority will ever issue a paper legitimizing a moved radiator**, so the exposure is permanent.
- **With collector-based (not standard riser) heating distribution, the radiator problem is worse** — no sill wall forces a floor-standing radiator instead (per a companion video, not in this batch), which then has nowhere good to go: in front of the window (blocks the light gain you were trying to achieve) or to the side (blocks curtain access, forcing curtains to shift inward and shrink usable room area).
- **Physics consequence**: moving the heat source away from enlarged glazing causes window fogging/condensation; **in-floor convectors are explicitly not used in modern renovation** (per a separate Zemskov video). Also, an enlarged glazed section leaves the frame's middle unsupported, causing more deflection/sag over time than a frame braced by a sill wall (durability tradeoff, per a companion video).
- **Zemskov's conclusion**: removing the sill wall when the loggia stays unjoined is all downside, no upside — never do it.

### If joining the loggia — opening width and radiator relocation

- **Zemskov's practical clearance figures for a widened apartment/loggia opening**: 60cm minimum to pass through comfortably; 70cm for a larger-bodied person; 80cm to carry two plates of food through; 90cm lets two people pass each other; beyond ~1m he considers excessive for any normal use case.
- **Widening the opening to the full wall width gives essentially zero added functional benefit, per Zemskov**, and (per the section above) minimal brightness benefit either.
- **The radiator-relocation problem when widening**: can't stay in its original position (blocks the opening); mounting on either side of the opening pulls warm air from the glazing (fogging risk, as above); relocating it onto the loggia itself eats into the loggia's own limited floor space **and is, per Zemskov, essentially impossible to get approved — one of the violations an inspection will almost always force you to redo, even with a bribe** — meaning the apartment becomes permanently harder to sell or re-register once done this way. **Alternative**: mount a frost-resistant radiator on the door/window reveal — but this requires an expensive cold-rated unit, and (per a companion video also referenced here) rules out putting a light switch or underfloor-heating thermostat on that same reveal, since the reveal can be narrowed to as little as 25cm.
- **Podium/threshold consequence**: a wet-screed loggia floor needs ≥15cm buildup for insulation — if the apartment's own screed is only ~5cm, the loggia floor ends up higher, creating a podium. **Threshold length scales directly with opening width**: 60-80cm for a modest partial opening, up to **2.5m if the opening is widened to the full wall** — and Zemskov notes a longer threshold strip is more prone to popping loose under repeated foot traffic.
- **Zemskov's furniture-creep argument**: any real use of a joined loggia (a treadmill needing a phone-charging nightstand, a desk needing extra surface, a hookah needing a side table) inevitably adds small furniture pieces that reduce the opening's effective usable width anyway — undermining the case for maximizing opening width up front. These added pieces are also inherently less stable than the solid, load-bearing sill wall that was removed (which could safely be sat or stood on) — Zemskov cites a real accident scenario (a hookah with hot coals knocked off a wobbly side table by a caught foot) as the kind of risk this creates.
- **Zemskov's central legal/practical point on inspections — a real quantified cost difference**: joining a loggia is always technically unregistrable, **but the practical consequence differs sharply depending on whether the original opening configuration was preserved or altered.** If you joined the loggia **without widening the original opening**, defeating an inspection is trivial and cheap — buy the cheapest available plastic frame and install it in the (unaltered) opening; an inspection commission works only from the floor plan (a top-down view) with no wall-elevation drawing and no record of the balcony unit's original left/right door configuration, so this passes easily, at a typical "signing fee" of **up to ~10,000 RUB**. **If you widened the opening**, you must first fully restore the sill wall, put the radiator back, and only then install the cheap frame — and officials who sign off on this paperwork know exactly how expensive that reversal is, so the fee for getting a widened/altered opening signed off starts at **~200,000 RUB**, not 10,000.

### Reconfiguring the opening without widening it — legal and effectively free

- **Zemskov's rule for regular window openings (non-load-bearing exterior walls only)**: widening these does genuinely improve interior light, unlike the loggia sill-wall case above. On fire-safety framing for the sill-wall stub some codes reference: Zemskov argues the traditional strip rarely saves anyone in practice — a fire extinguisher kept on the loggia is the thing that actually helps — so many modern designs minimize the stub to 10-15cm per side, just enough to form a proper reveal.
- **Caveats on how far to widen**: leave ≥60cm of wall stub if the loggia will be a home office (otherwise you'll see the back of the monitor and dangling cables from inside the room) or if a wardrobe will sit there (otherwise you'll see its side panel). **Don't let a widened sill section exceed 2.5m in length** — becomes an awkward, hard-to-reach long surface; reconfigure into a **T-shaped opening** instead once you'd otherwise exceed that length, which is also useful for a symmetric/mirrored layout (e.g. two identical wardrobes or two identical workstations on the loggia).
- **"Mirroring" technique**: a balcony-block unit has its door fixed to one side (a "right" unit has the door on the right); **flipping this configuration (installing a mirrored unit) can solve a real layout problem** — Zemskov's example: a bedroom where the loggia exit sits diagonally opposite the room's own entry door, forcing an awkward bed placement no matter where you put it. Mirroring lets the bed go against the far wall with a TV zone near the entry, giving both a straight, convenient path to the loggia and a cozier bed placement.
- **Legal note on mirroring, and its one real precondition**: mirroring the door side needs **no approval at all** — the same inspection-commission blind spot applies (floor plan only, no elevation drawing, no record of original door side). **But this is only safe with underfloor (not standard riser) heating.** With standard central-heating risers, mirroring would require routing the risers down into the screed and back up on the door's new side — equivalent to a radiator relocation, and categorically prohibited for the same reasons as above.
