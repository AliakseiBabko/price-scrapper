## Rules / Heuristics — New Detail

1. **Monolith chase-depth limit is about rebar cover, not a blanket
   chase prohibition**: chasing into monolithic concrete is fine up to
   roughly the depth of the first rebar layer (~2-3cm) — the real, hard
   rule is that **cutting/damaging the rebar itself is technologically
   prohibited outright**; if it happens, the building's technical
   supervision ("технадзор") will fine the contractor and force the
   rebar to be re-welded. This refines this page's existing "no
   recessed panel niches in a load-bearing monolithic wall" rule with a
   more general chase-depth mechanism (rebar-cover depth is the real
   limit, not the material itself).
2. **Foam-block covering over exposed monolith areas doubles as
   soundproofing, not just a chase-avoidance technique**: bare
   monolithic concrete is described as acoustically "very resonant" —
   vibration and noise travel readily through it — so covering
   outlet-box/cable-routing areas with foam block instead of chasing the
   monolith directly gives an incidental soundproofing benefit, improving
   living comfort, on top of avoiding the rebar-damage risk above.
3. **Real time-cost comparison, foam-block route-around vs. monolith
   drilling**: installing an outlet-box group in foam block plus
   finishing took about 10 minutes; drilling the equivalent into
   monolithic concrete with a granite-based aggregate took nearly an
   hour with a core bit.
4. **Diamond core-bit + trickle water-feed technique for hard
   granite-aggregate monolith**: carbide ("победит") bits wear out fast
   on this specific hard aggregate; a diamond bit run at low RPM with a
   manual spray-bottle water trickle (the same water-cooling principle
   tile-setters use for hard-material cuts) extends bit life.
5. **Flush-mount outlet boxes as a courtesy to the plastering/painting
   crew that follows, with the failure mode named directly**: an outlet
   box left protruding even slightly causes a visible bump ("шишка")
   defect once plastered over — framed as a common electrician
   carelessness issue, not a rare mistake, and as directly affecting how
   much the finishing crew has to fight the substrate later.
6. **Enlarging a drilled box-mounting hole to improve long-term
   fixation, specifically against being pulled out by cord-tugging**:
   in a thin partition wall with weak fixation, an outlet box can be
   pulled out of the wall by a cord being yanked (children and adults
   named as the realistic culprits) — drilling/enlarging the hole
   slightly beyond the box's own footprint gives the mounting
   adhesive/plaster more surface to grip, meaningfully increasing
   pull-out resistance. A specific hazard-avoidance framing: this
   protects against a real shock-risk outcome from an exposed, torn-out
   box.
7. **Phase-loss/neutral-disconnection protection device, with a
   concrete failure mechanism explained**: at a client's specific
   request, a protection unit was installed that automatically
   disconnects (or switches to a backup phase) if a phase is lost.
   **Mechanism stated**: if an electrician elsewhere in the building's
   shared wiring accidentally disconnects a neutral ("0"), the full
   380V line-to-line voltage can appear across single-phase 220V loads
   throughout the building, "seeking out" the weakest/lowest-consumption
   device on that circuit (a named example: a TV) and destroying it via
   overvoltage — a real building-shared-infrastructure failure mode this
   project hadn't previously documented, distinct from a simple breaker
   trip or short-circuit protection.
8. **Leave slack in a cable run when the exact final fixture location
   is still genuinely uncertain**, rather than cutting to a design
   drawing's stated dimension: a real anecdote where a design drawing's
   under-cabinet lighting position conflicted with the as-installed
   range-hood location (a coordination gap between the designer and the
   kitchen layout) — the electrician's own practice of leaving visible
   spare cable length at the outlet meant the fixture could be
   relocated later without redoing the run; without that slack, a
   miscoordinated cable gets cut short, hidden, and forgotten as a
   problem for whoever discovers it later.
9. **Call the fixture supplier before wiring a complex dimmable
   lighting circuit to confirm real cable requirements**: a client's
   specified dimmable LED under-cabinet lighting needed a 5-conductor
   cable even though only 4 of the 5 conductors are actually used — the
   practitioner phoned the supplier directly before installation to
   confirm this, framed as routine due diligence to avoid installing the
   wrong cable count and having to redo the run (or, per the video's own
   aside, risk burning out fixtures from a wiring mismatch).
