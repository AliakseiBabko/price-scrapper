## Design Concept — Durable Facts & Rules (General Living-Room Layout)

- **Define the room's purpose before placing furniture**: a living room
  can serve rest, entertainment, work, or socializing — the intended use
  should drive furniture selection/placement, not the reverse. In the
  designer's own home, the stated purpose (a gathering/rest space for
  family and friends) is why both sofas are sized to the room and
  seating capacity was prioritized.
- **Establish one central focal point per room** (fireplace, large TV,
  dining table, or even a window view counts) and arrange furniture so it
  visually draws attention there without anything blocking it — in the
  designer's own living room, the fireplace zone is the focal point, and
  the poufs placed near it are positioned so they don't obstruct it.
- **Organize the room around the soft-seating zone (usually the sofa)**
  for comfortable conversation and TV viewing; in a larger living room,
  the space can be divided into sub-zones, but **walkways/circulation
  paths must never be blocked** by that zoning.
- **Match furniture scale to the room's actual size** — don't overload a
  smaller living room with oversized furniture. The source notes the
  kitchen-living combination trend is increasingly common (cross-
  reference only, not elaborated in this video).
- **Lighting should use a minimum of three layers**: **general** (even
  ambient illumination of the whole room), **decorative** (table/floor
  lamps aimed at specific tasks like reading), and **accent** (directional
  fixtures/art-lighting highlighting specific details) — more layers can
  be added beyond these three, but these three are treated as the
  non-negotiable minimum. **Corroborates** the layered-lighting structure
  already recorded from `YT_TnYExlfWw9c` (Diskill/Nadia Karmin,
  primary/accent/decorative) and `YT_hW1hlDoqDR0` (LightLab/Voronov,
  decorative-accent/functional/perimeter-backlight) — a third independent
  specialist source converging on the same 3-layer minimum, again under
  its own layer names (general/decorative/accent here).
- **Rearrangement-planning caution, genuinely new**: if you plan to
  periodically rearrange furniture (the source's own stated
  recommendation, to refresh a space), be aware that fixture wiring and
  outlet positions are fixed at construction time and won't move with the
  furniture — **if you're the type of household likely to rearrange
  often, plan only the basic general-lighting layer at construction time**
  and add accent/decorative layers later with more flexible fixtures
  (lamps, not hardwired spots), rather than over-committing to fixed
  accent positions that a later rearrangement would strand.
- **Furniture must be functional, not just arranged for looks**: place
  side tables next to seating for drinks/books, and ensure adequate
  storage so the room can stay tidy.
- **Rugs are a genuine zoning tool, not just decoration**: beyond comfort
  underfoot, a rug delineates a zone. **Furniture should ideally not sit
  fully on the rug — leave some clearance/gap** between a rug's edge and
  the furniture on it. In the designer's own home, two separate rugs
  independently delineate the dining zone and the living/sofa zone within
  one combined room — a real applied example of rug-based zoning rather
  than a physical partition.
- **Maintain balance between paired elements** (sofa+art, sofa+chair,
  sofa+table) and keep color palette/furniture style harmonious across
  the room; avoid overloading a room with too much furniture regardless
  of room size.
