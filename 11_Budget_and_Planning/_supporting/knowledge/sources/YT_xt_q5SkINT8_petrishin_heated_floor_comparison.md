## Heating — New Legality Nuance, Economy Data, and Mechanism Detail

2. **New legality exception for water/liquid underfloor heating,
   beyond this page's existing "under 1% of cases" ground-floor-unit
   exception**: water floor is also permitted in **a new-build ("новостройка")
   specifically designed with separate dedicated risers for underfloor
   heating** — distinct from the existing individually-metered-ground-
   floor-unit exception already on this page. **Mechanism for the
   illegal-hookup consequence, more specific than the existing "large
   fines" framing**: connecting a water floor to a shared central-
   heating riser without authorization also disrupts the **hydraulic
   balance of the entire riser line** serving every connected unit, not
   just a paperwork violation.
3. **⚠️ The single most important nuance, per this source's own framing:
   installation cost vs. running/usage cost are separate questions,
   and running cost is what people forget to weigh.** Water floor is
   the most expensive to install (boiler, manifold, pumps, pipes,
   expensive labor) but, if run on mains gas or a wood boiler,
   **roughly 5× cheaper to operate than any electric floor** (cable or
   film alike), because electricity tariffs are the most expensive
   energy source in this comparison. **Real payback figure**: in a
   permanently-occupied private house, the water floor's higher
   install cost is recouped in roughly **3 years** via gas savings, after
   which it saves "tens of thousands of RUB" annually. Electric floor
   is cheaper to install but more expensive to run — framed as
   acceptable for a weekend/seasonal dacha, financially ruinous for
   permanent heating of a full house.
4. **Bitumen-insulated-contact mechanism for film's wet-room
   prohibition**, more specific than this page's existing "exposed
   electrical connections" framing: film underfloor heating's
   electrical contacts are protected only by bitumen insulation — water
   ingress causes a short circuit and real shock risk, the specific
   mechanism behind the existing categorical wet-room ban.

## Planning Rules — Sequencing and Thermostat Savings

5. **Planning-sequence rule**: always start with a furniture-placement
   floor plan *before* designing the heated-floor layout — draw where
   furniture will sit first, then lay out the heating zones around it,
   not the reverse.
6. **Thermostat energy-saving figure**: using a thermostat rather than
   running any heated floor at a fixed constant setting saves **up to
   30%** of energy consumption.
7. **Room-by-room combination strategy for an apartment or electric-only
   home**: film floor under floating coverings (laminate, engineered
   board, linoleum) in living spaces (living room, bedroom) for fast,
   on-demand comfort heating; electric cable floor under tile in wet/
   utility zones (bathroom, kitchen, balcony) — a concrete, practical
   room-by-room split, not just an abstract "use film here, cable there"
   rule.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Thermal-Conductivity Mechanism for Wood-Based Coverings

8. **⚠️ New general mechanism, broader than the already-known
   "film can't go under laminate" rule**: laminate ("essentially
   compressed cardboard"), engineered board, and solid wood all have
   inherently poor thermal conductivity (the company expert's own
   analogy: a heated nail feels hot almost instantly because metal
   conducts heat well; a lit match can be held far longer because wood
   doesn't) — heating *any* underfloor-heating type (water, cable, or
   film) under a thick wood-based covering is largely pointless, since
   the covering itself blocks most of the heat from ever reaching the
   surface, regardless of which heating technology sits beneath it.
   **Explicit recommendation**: pair heated flooring with tile,
   porcelain tile, quartz-vinyl, or linoleum — not laminate, engineered
   board, or solid wood — and always confirm the covering manufacturer's
   own stated heating-temperature limit before combining the two,
   given real disputes exist over exceeding an unstated limit.
