## Planning Rules — Pre-Purchase and Pre-Keys Design Decisions

1. **Consult a designer before finalizing an apartment purchase, not
   after** — the source frames this as the single biggest lever a buyer
   has: a designer can flag layout problems (see item 6 below) before
   the purchase is locked in, when they're still avoidable rather than
   something to work around.
2. **A design project commonly starts 5-6 months before the developer
   hands over keys**, using only the developer's own measured floor plan
   (overall unit dimensions) as the reference — **partition wall
   positions on that pre-keys plan are not guaranteed as-built**; treat
   them as an orientation reference, not a confirmed layout, until keys
   are actually received and the unit is inspected in person.
3. **Recognize developer's drywall (`гипсокартон`) partition walls as a
   demolition-and-rebuild candidate by construction type**: in this
   project, every developer-built partition was gypsum-board, and all of
   them were demolished and rebuilt in 10cm foam block (`пеноблок`) —
   the material identification itself (drywall vs. masonry) is what
   signals whether a wall is a cheap, low-risk one to remove and replace
   with a heavier partition type.
4. **Avoid a layout change that would require formal replanning
   legalization (`узаконивание планировки`) unless the client is
   committed to it.** Worked example: the client initially wanted an
   isolated home office walled off from the living room, but once keys
   were received and the designer assessed the two new walls that would
   require, the decision was reversed and the original as-delivered room
   footprint was kept specifically so the measured/registered plan would
   not need to be re-legalized. **Decision heuristic distilled**: weigh
   a desired layout change against the legalization cost/complexity it
   would trigger, not just its own design merit.

## Family Requirements / Preferences — Space-Planning and Room-Selection Cautions

5. **Two exterior walls with windows in the same small room (a caution
   for evaluating a floor plan before buying)**: in this project's kids'
   room, two window walls plus floor-standing radiators under both
   windows left almost no usable wall length for furniture — a real
   example of how a seemingly attractive "extra window" room feature can
   actually be a furniture-placement constraint once floor radiators are
   accounted for.
6. **Corridor-into-kitchen-living sightline caution**: the developer's
   layout put the entry hallway on a path that flows directly into the
   kitchen-living room, so a visitor sees the kitchen-living space
   immediately on entry — the designer states she'd flag this as a
   negative if a client asked her to evaluate the unit before purchase,
   even though it was designed around successfully after the fact
   (color-zoning the hallway, using tall cabinets as a visual partition).
7. **Laundry-room-in-hallway placement, not bathroom/WC**: placing the
   laundry machine/utility zone in a hallway niche instead of inside the
   bathroom or WC is called out explicitly as a way to avoid consuming
   bathroom/WC floor area with laundry equipment — a placement principle
   independent of the room's specific decor.
8. **Towel-warmer-near-toilet placement mitigation**: when a towel
   warmer must be located near a toilet, position it so it is not
   reachable directly by hand from a seated position at the toilet
   (achieved here via the fixture layout, not extra distance) — contrasts
   with this same project's own primary/master bathroom, where the
   towel warmer sits directly beside the toilet with no such
   mitigation, flagged by the designer as the less-preferred version of
   this placement.

## Design Concept — Material Selection and Decor Technique

9. **Humid-zone furniture facade material rule**: bathroom furniture
   facades in this project use enamel (`эмаль`), not laminate/foil
   finishes, specifically because bathrooms are a high-humidity
   environment — wood-look accent inserts (a decorative laminate,
   `Egger`-type) are used only outside the wet-splash zone.
10. **Entry "dirty zone" flooring should match a durable wet-tolerant
    material already used elsewhere, not the main flooring run**: the
    entry hallway's floor tile was chosen to match the kitchen
    backsplash tile specifically because the apartment's main flooring
    (engineered/parquet board) is not moisture- or dirt-resistant enough
    for a shoes-on entry zone — repeating an already-selected tile here
    avoids introducing a third flooring material.
11. **Reversible kids'-room wall decor for a growing child**: rather than
    real wallpaper, the kids' room walls are painted, with printed wall
    decals applied over the paint — the decals are described as
    removable once the child outgrows the theme, turning the room into a
    plain painted (relettable/re-themeable) space for a future teen
    redecoration without a repaint or wallpaper strip-out.
12. **Lighting-preference conflict resolved via backlit wall panel
    instead of a pendant/sconce compromise**: in the master bedroom, one
    spouse wanted a hanging pendant light and the other did not want one
    at all — the designer's resolution was a stone-texture (`скала`) and
    wood-panel accent wall with integrated LED backlighting behind the
    panel on one side, paired with the requested pendant on the other
    side, so each side of the bed got its preferred lighting type without
    either party's preference being dropped.

## Rules / Heuristics — Vetting a Designer, and Working the Budget

13. **Vet a prospective designer by asking for photos of realized
    (built) past projects, not renders** — the source's stated reasoning:
    "anyone can draw a pretty picture; whether it gets built the way it's
    drawn is a separate question" — a concrete due-diligence question a
    self-managed client can ask before hiring, distinct from asking about
    style/portfolio alone.
14. **Ask a prospective designer what a design-project deliverable
    actually contains** (which drawing sheets, what's covered) before
    signing, per the source's own "top 3" advice list — framed as a
    common gap where clients don't know what they're paying for until
    partway through.
15. **In-store material verification even before a collage/moodboard is
    formally approved**: the designer has the client physically visit a
    store to see a tile in person under real lighting once it's
    tentatively selected, because in-store lighting materially changes
    how a tile/finish looks compared to a rendered visualization —
    practiced at the tentative-selection stage, not only at final
    sign-off.
16. **Budget adherence method**: the designer works to a client-stated
    ceiling by building and pricing collages/visualizations room by room
    (not the whole project at once), checking each room's running cost
    against the budget incrementally rather than pricing the full design
    only at the end — while still flagging optional upgrades (e.g.
    "eco-friendly"/solid engineered or parquet board flooring) as a
    choice the client can decide to save toward rather than a forced
    substitution.
17. **Remote consultation as a fallback when a client skips the full
    on-site design-supervision (`авторский надзор`) package**: the
    designer stays reachable and gives remote guidance during
    construction even without the paid supervision add-on, framed as
    protecting the design outcome's fidelity rather than a pure upsell.

## Mistakes / Warnings — Unauthorized Replanning

18. **Illegal replanning (`незаконная перепланировка`) framing, per this
    studio's own account**: roughly 99% of this studio's projects use
    legally registered layouts; where an unregistered change occurs, it's
    described as limited to changes that don't affect shared/common
