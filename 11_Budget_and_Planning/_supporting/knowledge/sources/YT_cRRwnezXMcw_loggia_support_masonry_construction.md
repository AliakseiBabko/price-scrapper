## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's tool list**: angle grinder (metal-cutting disc), rotary hammer with 6mm bit, driver with PH2 bit, a hacksaw/reciprocating saw for cutting blocks, an impact-rated bubble level (can be tapped with a hammer), a small flat pry bar (~100mm blade), tape measure, plumb bob (~2m string), tin snips, a water level or long laser level, mixing buckets (10-20L) and a mixer paddle, PPE (glasses, gloves, plus a winter hat if working in cold weather).
- **Zemskov's core distinction: "support masonry" (опорная кладка) is fundamentally different from an ordinary interior gas-block partition wall** — it's a thin-joint structure that doesn't settle under the new frame's weight, and must resist tensile and shear loads, not just compression. **Must be built from gas-concrete block specifically, never foam-concrete block** (standard size 600×100-250mm) despite the common informal habit of calling any light block "пеноблок."
- **Positioning rule**: the masonry must sit exactly in the plane where the new frame's top and side fixing points will land. Top fixing point ≈6cm from the outer edge of the upper slab, offset inward specifically to avoid the slab's own reinforcing rebar (typically ~4-5cm from the slab's outer edge, roughly finger-thick) — drilling into or outside the rebar risks the slab chipping at that spot. On a non-standard loggia, drill exploratory test holes first to find the actual rebar position before committing to the masonry's final placement. Mark the frame's top fixing point on the slab, then drop a plumb line to establish the masonry's centerline below it.
- **Base-prep rule**: knock off every protruding concrete stub/burr that would stop the first course sitting flat; remove all debris; dry-fit the first course (no adhesive) and confirm every block sits stably without rocking before proceeding — carve notches for any un-removable protrusions, but **don't pre-fill low spots** — the thick first-course adhesive bed fills those during setting.
- **Row-sizing rule**: never fill a leftover gap with straight adhesive (explicitly forbidden), and never leave a tiny offcut specifically in the top (fixing) row's last position, since a small unstable piece there is a weak fixing point. **Correct method**: trim a bit off the second-to-last block so the final piece ends up a reasonable length (Zemskov's own rule of thumb: at least 10-15cm, never a thin sliver).
- **Cutting technique and safety rule**: always cut any detail notches into a block **while it's still full-size**, before cutting it down to its smaller final piece — cutting a notch into an already-small offcut is imprecise and, per Zemskov, a real finger-injury risk from trying to hold a small piece steady while cutting it. Mark cut lines against the block's actual installed neighbor, not by pre-measuring on paper; account for roughly two ~0.5cm adhesive joints when marking. **Saw technique**: light pressure cuts effectively — pressing harder doesn't cut faster, it just makes the cut wander; keep the saw perpendicular to the block face in both directions.
- **Debris-management rule for a long loggia**: create one temporary debris pile per ~2-2.5m of loggia length while working, cleared only at the end — not one single pile at one end of a long run, which Zemskov calls a beginner mistake that wastes real time on longer loggias (e.g. 3 piles for a 6m loggia).
- **Adhesive mixing rule**: mix progressively — start liquid to eliminate dry lumps, then add dry mix gradually to reach final consistency; let it rest ~15 minutes before use. **The first course's mix must be noticeably thicker than every subsequent course's** — thick enough to fill base irregularities without slumping. Clean the base thoroughly before applying — squeezed-out excess adhesive gets scraped up and reused for later, thinner courses, and any grit/pebbles picked up from a dirty base will cause real problems once embedded in a later thin joint.
- **Adhesive application technique**: apply as a small pyramid/mound, never spread thin across the full contact face — the block's own weight when pressed down spreads it correctly into voids; same technique on the wall-contact side. **Never economize on adhesive** — cheap material, and excess squeeze-out is harmless, but too little risks a real void (even pen-diameter) that becomes a genuine problem later. Give extra adhesive at any structural extrusion/fixing lug on the neighboring precast slab, where the gap is naturally deeper.
- **Seating technique ("притирка")**: press each block down into its thick adhesive bed and wiggle it slightly until resistance stops (bed fully seated), then slide it firmly against the previous block/wall in one precise final motion. **Tapping technique**: when using a hammer to seat a block, press firmly on the block while tapping, don't just strike it loose — striking alone risks cracking it.
- **Zemskov's single most important, most counter-intuitive rule: never level courses horizontally by varying adhesive-joint thickness to compensate for an uneven base.** He explicitly calls this the common-but-wrong method ("takes 2 days instead of 2 hours, and still comes out worse") — stepped-thickness joints act as a compressible cushion that lets the wall settle unevenly under the frame's later weight, eventually forcing a full teardown and rebuild. **Correct method**: lay every course thin-joint, directly following the base's true unevenness straight up through the stack; **only the very top (last) course gets individually cut level/horizontal**, after dry-fitting — which is needed anyway, since 5 standard courses (~25cm each ≈125cm total) typically exceeds the target masonry height (110-115cm), so the top row always needs trimming regardless of base unevenness. This produces a faster, simpler, and genuinely stronger wall, since every block bears directly and tightly on the one below with minimal compressible material between them.
- **Course-to-course bonding ("перевязка") rule**: start each new course from the end where the previous course finished (alternating direction each course, boustrophedon-style — also a debris-management efficiency point); offset each course by roughly half a block so vertical joints stagger — the first block of course 2 should reach to about the midpoint of course 1's first block. **Precision here doesn't need to be exact** — an eyeball mark with a dirty trowel is fine; ±3cm tolerance is acceptable at a 60cm block-bonding scale.
- **What makes it genuinely "thin-joint"/load-transferring, demonstrated by a knock-test**: a properly-seated block (tight, thin adhesive film) sounds dull/deadened when tapped, like knocking on solid concrete — direct load transfer, no cushioning. A loosely-set block (inadequate adhesive contact) rings/resonates when tapped — meaning a compressible adhesive gap exists that will settle under load over time. This directly demonstrates why the leveling method matters (see above).
- **Final-course height and slope rule**: the top course must sit at a height such that, after the frame and its external drip-cap/water sill (водоотлив) are installed, the drip-cap rests on the old loggia railing at roughly a 15° downward slope. **Formula: raise the new masonry ~1cm above the old railing for every 5cm of the future drip-cap's width** (e.g. a 15cm-wide drip-cap needs the masonry raised ~3cm above the old railing; a 30cm-wide drip-cap needs ~6cm). **Always measure from the old railing's highest point, not its lowest** — referencing the low point makes the drip-cap sit wrong wherever the railing rises above that reference elsewhere along its length.
- **Practical sequencing rule**: never glue the top course before cutting it to final height — cutting after gluing risks tearing the fresh joint apart; dry-fit, mark, remove, cut, then install and glue. **If the loggia's exterior railing itself isn't installed/welded yet, don't guess the top-row cutting height in advance** — wait until the real railing height is fixed, then trim the top row against the actual as-built reference.
- **Joint tuck-pointing (затирка)**: after laying, work a looser adhesive mix by hand/trowel firmly *into* any remaining gaps between blocks — genuinely fills voids and adds bonding strength, not a cosmetic surface smear.
- **Fixing-course pre-treatment ("imitation plastering")**: coat the course that will receive the frame's fixing screws with the same specialized block adhesive (never tile adhesive or Rotband, consistent with this store's existing block-adhesive-selection content) from both directions, so it soaks in and hardens the block's surface — prevents a chunk breaking off when a fixing screw is later driven in, which otherwise means re-cutting and re-gluing a whole replacement block in front of the client.
- **Three explicit reinforcement mistakes to avoid, per Zemskov**: (1) **never embed metal mesh in the horizontal joints** — adds zero strength, defeats the whole thin-joint tight-bearing principle, and creates a direct thermal bridge straight through the wall; (2) **never embed a long metal rod/rebar in a carved channel** — gas-concrete block is too brittle to hold the rod snugly (always some play), so it provides no real flex resistance against wind load, and it also worsens thermal performance the same way mesh does; (3) **never try to reinforce/pin the wall's outer edges** — the edges already carry the load directly through the frame's own anchor fixings and don't flex; the wall's actual weak point under wind load is its **unsupported center span**, not the edges.
- **Zemskov's correct center-reinforcement technique**: a horizontally-tensioned perforated metal strap, stretched taut (slightly elongated under tension) and screwed to every course with self-tapping screws — minimum 2 screws per 60cm block, 4 is fine, 6+ is excessive. After screwing the strap to all courses, slightly loosen and re-tighten its own end-tensioning screws, causing the strap to contract a small amount and gently cinch the courses together — producing genuine (if sub-millimeter) compression that helps the wall's center resist cracking under wind-load flex, unlike a rigid embedded rod or mesh.
