## Balcony / Loggia — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's technique: thin the wall between the room and the loggia (from the loggia side) to reclaim usable interior width** — a distinct method from the companion video's railing-removal approach, and per Zemskov worth more: **up to ~30cm gained**, versus ~15cm from the railing method — "twice as much, and 15cm was already a lot."
- **Which loggias this applies to**: (1) new-builds with a "combined" load-bearing wall (structural inner layer + non-structural external insulation layer under anti-vandal render) — the insulation layer can simply be removed with zero impact on load-bearing capacity; (2) apartments with self-supporting (non-load-bearing) exterior brick walls; (3) apartments where the wall between room and loggia is a plain infill partition made of gas-concrete block, not structural.
- **Thinning method by wall type**: combined wall — just remove the external insulation layer and its render; brick wall — mechanically reduce thickness (grind/knock off the excess); gas-concrete block wall — in practice, per Zemskov, it's easier to **fully demolish and rebuild the wall thinner while preserving its exact original opening position and radiator location**, rather than trying to shave down individual blocks, since the specialized penetrating block adhesive bonds blocks together far more strongly than ordinary cement-sand-mortared brick.
- **Zemskov's central numeric rule: minimum final wall thickness between room and loggia is 25cm; 30cm is better.** His reasoning differs by whether the loggia is joined:
  - **If NOT joining**: the new balcony-block frame installs centered in the remaining wall thickness, and needs reveals (откосы) formed on both sides. At only 10cm remaining thickness, the frame itself would fit but the reveals would not — even the thinnest 58mm PVC profile plus cladding would leave only ~3.5cm per side, nowhere near enough for a proper reveal on both faces. **Minimum 250mm, better 300mm** is needed for the frame plus workable reveals on both sides.
  - **If joining**: the thinned wall section becomes a countertop/desk surface — usable minimum countertop width (enough to hold something without it falling, without the surface itself failing under edge pressure) is 40-50cm; at 10cm wall thickness the countertop would overhang ~20cm per side and fail immediately when pressed near an edge, since the fixing/support area is too small — and radiator-mounting brackets also won't hold reliably in a 10cm-thick wall. Same 25cm-minimum/30cm-better rule applies here too.
- **Zemskov's single most consequentially-stated rule in this source, directly bearing on whether this technique should be used at all**: **only thin this wall if the loggia will genuinely be insulated and heated afterward.** If the loggia is *not* going to be insulated, thinning this wall is explicitly forbidden by Zemskov's own rule — the wall will definitely freeze, develop condensation, and grow mold, because **the cold/warm boundary line must always face outward toward the street, never toward the apartment interior.** Thinning converts this wall from "the cold boundary between a warm apartment and a cold loggia" into "a purely formal partition between two now-both-warm zones" — but that reasoning only holds once the loggia side is actually warm; otherwise the thinned wall becomes a direct cold bridge straight into the living space.
- **Approval question, two cases**: if not joining, an inspector sees a properly-glazed warm loggia and never even asks about the interior wall's thickness. If joining, the inspector's only question concerns the missing glazing contour between apartment and loggia (per the companion opening-configuration video) — wall thickness itself is irrelevant to any inspection either way.
- **Header/lintel restoration**: required after thinning — in most cases the whole upper wall section is removed and a small drywall-framed box built in its place instead. Zemskov explicitly flags (without elaborating here) that a single continuous ceiling spanning both the room and the loggia should never be built — reserved for a separate video not in this batch.
- **The "quarter" (четверть) rebate question — the highest-value content in this source.** A "четверть" is a masonry return/ledge (~5-6cm, named for being roughly 1/4 a standard brick's length) at a window or balcony-block opening's street-facing side, normally there to give the window frame's foam joint better thermal protection at that edge. **If joining**: no rebate is needed at all — wallpaper the room side, frame/clad the loggia side, trim with reveals, done. **If NOT joining, Zemskov states approximately 100% of people make the same mistake**: after thinning the wall and necessarily removing the original rebate, they try to painstakingly rebuild it before installing the new glazing. **His explicit rule: never do this.** Two reasons given: (1) rebuilding a rebate around a full opening perimeter is genuinely difficult, especially since post-demolition opening edges are always uneven/ragged; (2) it creates a much harder downstream problem — **interior reveals work fine either way** (cut a channel, insert a panel, angle it, fix the back, close with a trim profile), but **exterior reveals specifically require tucking the panel behind the frame's edge**, which a rebate physically blocks (it covers the first foam joint). Without a rebate, this simple tuck-in method works; with one, installers must instead use a much harder "glued reveal" (клеевой откос) technique that, per Zemskov, "practically nobody can do properly nowadays."
- **Zemskov's core insight on what a rebate is actually for**: **purely thermal protection of the window-to-opening junction — not structural (preventing the window falling out) and not UV protection for the foam joint.** His proof: loggia glazing universally has no rebate at all — the frame sits in a straight opening, secured by anchors (won't fall in) and covered by the drip-cap (won't get pushed out from outside) regardless. Since a properly glazed, insulated, heated loggia's opening doesn't need thermal protection from itself, rebuilding a rebate there is pure wasted effort with zero benefit, while actively creating the harder exterior-reveal problem above. **Contrast case**: building an actual house from scratch, a rebate at a *regular* window opening genuinely is worth including, since it protects against real freezing/condensation there — the "don't rebuild it" rule is specific to a loggia opening that's already being properly glazed, not a general anti-rebate rule.
