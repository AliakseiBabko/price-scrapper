## Durable Facts (Level 1, `single-account`/`unverified` throughout)

- **Developer-delivered walls with depressions of several centimeters (up to 3-5 cm cited) are described as a common defect, not a bonus**: the speaker's framing is that a developer is contractually/structurally obligated to deliver a wall in one flat plane, and an owner should treat an uneven wall as a defect to correct, not as "free extra floor area" to preserve by leaving it recessed.
- **Common owner mistake — leaving the recess unfilled to preserve a few cm of extra depth for storage**: doing so creates non-standard segment widths (rarely a multiple of 10 cm) while off-the-shelf furniture/cabinet modules are standardized in multiples of 10 cm, making it impossible to fit standard cabinetry into the resulting space without a custom order. It also introduces extra inside/outside corners into the flooring plinth line, baseboard run, and stretch-ceiling contour that otherwise wouldn't exist, adding complexity, cost, and (for a kitchen specifically) awkward, harder-to-use cabinet layouts.
- **Mistake 1 — filling the depression purely with thick gypsum plaster**: gypsum plaster is reliable only up to roughly 5 cm of buildup; adding the ~3 cm base-wall plaster thickness on top of a 4-5 cm recess fill pushes total thickness to 6-8 cm, which reliably delaminates/falls off over time. Also noted as expensive at that volume — comparable to the plaster needed for an entire room.
- **Mistake 2 — mixing cement-sand fill under gypsum finish plaster**: some DIYers, having read that thick fill layers should use cement-sand mix (a legitimate rule for deep fills generally), then apply a gypsum finish coat directly over the cement-sand layer to match the rest of the wall. **Gypsum plaster and cement-sand mixes are explicitly stated as incompatible when layered directly on top of each other — doing so reliably causes delamination between the two layers.**
- **Mistake 3 — filling the recess with drywall on a metal stud frame**: the recess depth is often barely enough to fit the profile + drywall sheet with no room left for shimming/leveling, so the result just crudely restates the original wall's unevenness rather than correcting it. This approach is also flagged for two secondary problems: (a) the drywall-filled section ends up poorly sound-isolated compared to the solid wall around it, even with batt insulation added inside the cavity; (b) it's structurally weak for mounting heavy items (e.g., kitchen wall cabinets) directly to it, requiring extra plywood/lumber blocking behind the drywall — described as adding cost and complexity disproportionate to the benefit.
- **Correct method**: fill the depression with aerated-concrete blocks in a thickness (5, 7, or 10 cm) chosen to match the recess depth. Bond the blocks to the back wall with block adhesive or gypsum plaster, and additionally anchor each block mechanically with plastic mushroom-head dowels (dübel gribok) — described as important, not optional. Then level the combined original-wall-plus-infill surface together using plaster guide rails (маяки) and finish-plaster it as one continuous plane. Result: a flat wall matching the sound-isolation performance of the surrounding solid wall, strong enough to mount kitchen cabinets and route cable chases through, unlike the drywall alternative.
