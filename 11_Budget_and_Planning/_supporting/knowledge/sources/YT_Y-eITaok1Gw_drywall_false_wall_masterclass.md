## Walls — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Zemskov's case for building a false wall over exposed utility pipes**, even at the cost of a small floor-area loss: (1) enables clean, technologically correct baseboard/flooring and stretch-ceiling-track junctions — neither can be neatly terminated against a round pipe, always leaving a dirt-collecting gap; (2) restores the room to a clean rectangle so furniture can sit flush against any wall; (3) bonus: often yields a wider countertop, itself usable as a desk/study surface. Cost in his example: a floor triangle roughly the size of an A4 sheet.
- **Prep sequence**: thoroughly clean the area to be enclosed *before* building (far easier than after); prime/treat the main wall with antiseptic primer using a sprayer, not a roller or brush (roller/brush application is called "extremely ineffective" for this purpose) — apply generously enough that it visibly runs down the wall (a true soak, not a surface coat). Quality primer absorbs almost instantly, so don't worry about needing to re-treat an earlier section that's already dried by the time a later section is finished.
- **Layout/marking method using a laser plane-builder**: find the single most protruding element to be hidden, project that point onto the floor; the drywall's inner face should clear that point by 3cm; measure the resulting offset distance from the main wall (worked example: 16.5cm); transfer that same distance onto the perpendicular walls at each end; align a laser osepostroitel's bottom marker to the floor mark, switch to vertical-plane mode, and adjust its leveling screws until the projected plane matches the wall-mounted reference point — producing a true vertical reference offset the correct distance from the main wall for the whole run. **If the main wall is significantly bowed**, pressing one continuous track straight against it will deform the track — cut it into segments first rather than forcing a single piece to bend to match a wavy wall.
- **Main-opening verticals need much higher precision than side verticals**: level roughly with a magnetic level first, finalize with a laser — because these later serve as the guide rails for plastic window-reveal installation, and the angle between wall and future reveal must be exactly 90°; an error here (track pushed too far out or in) forces knocking down or building up material later at the loggia-joining stage.
- **Zemskov's rule: join framing elements with a professional crimping tool (просекатель), not screws — calls "screws are stronger" a myth.** Once drywall is installed, profile joints of either type become essentially immovable, and any screw head (even a flat one) protrudes slightly and locally deforms the drywall surface at that point. He concedes screws are sometimes genuinely unavoidable, but the crimper should be used wherever possible. **Ordinary crimping tools just puncture/tear the metal; a professional one actually folds/interlocks it** — demonstrated with a destruction test where a professionally-crimped joint essentially cannot be torn apart by hand or even its own weight, unlike an ordinary-crimper joint.
- **Convector-niche framing rule for a balcony-block's lower section**: build it from one long, continuous bent piece rather than joined segments, for maximum rigidity, since it must also support a future countertop. **Niche sizing must be exactly 5mm larger (length and height) than the convector grille's own mounting dimensions**, or the grille won't fit. **Positioning rule, flagged as a common mistake**: don't try to visually center the niche symmetrically top/bottom — the lower gap gets partly covered by baseboard anyway (breaking any equal-gap illusion), and centering also tends to leave awkward, hard-to-reach access to the thermostat/valve regulator behind it. **Correct rule: fix 10cm of clearance above the niche, let the remainder fall below.** **Leveling-sequence mistake to avoid**: independently leveling both the top and bottom profile with a spirit level — instead, level one, then set the second purely by tape-measure distance from the first, which is more reliable in practice.
- **Framing spacing rule**: with 12mm moisture-resistant drywall, the frame's grid cell must not exceed 60×60cm — **but this doesn't mean studs must always sit exactly 60cm apart**; for a standalone section between 60cm and 1.2m wide, simply splitting it in half is better than forcing a rigid 60cm module.
- **Zemskov's rule: use guide-track profile (направляющая) throughout the frame, not stud profile (профиль), even for elements that would normally be studs.** Stud profile's inward-folded edge adds rigidity but blocks freely inserting cross-pieces between two studs — cross-pieces would then need special connector clips ("crabs"/краб in trade slang) instead of simply slotting in, and at the point where the false wall meets plastic window reveals, those crab connectors would have to be cut in half anyway since they protrude past the installation plane. Track profile avoids this entirely.
- **Cross-piece height rule**: fill left/right sections with cross-pieces at 60cm intervals, with their *centers* specifically at 60/120/182/240cm from the floor — not simple even spacing.
- **A common mistake at this stage**: leveling only the cross-pieces (auxiliary elements) and forgetting to re-check the main vertical — an overly-long cross-piece can silently bend the vertical during installation. **Correct practice**: when installing guide verticals, place the level against the vertical itself, not the cross-piece. If cross-pieces also need leveling, a magnetic level is sufficient — 1-2mm deviation is acceptable there, since the only real purpose of leveling an auxiliary element is so a screw through the drywall doesn't miss it.
- **Top/bottom horizontal elements at an opening have different requirements**: the top one must be placed with extreme precision (hard to fix if wrong, and it anchors the metal corner-guide angle used for reveal installation) — level with a laser, check from multiple angles. The bottom one must be very rigid, since a person's full body weight will step on that edge — build it from one continuous bent piece (corners folded, not jointed) and reinforce with an added vertical support.
- **Bracing rule**: a stud/track frame alone has fairly low rigidity — brace it to the main wall with fabricated angled brackets, fixed to the wall then screwed to the frame. **Caution**: it's easy to deform the frame slightly while fixing each brace — re-check verticality with a level throughout the bracing process, not just once at the start. **No fixed brace count** — use enough that the frame doesn't wobble under a shoulder-lean or wallpapering pressure; Zemskov's own project used 14.
- **Zemskov's drywall-sheet cutting method: cut sheets in place ("по месту"), not by tape-measure-and-pencil.** Hold an oversized sheet against the frame, fix it at a couple of points, mark by pressing/creasing directly at the frame edge, then snap/fold/cut along that crease. His stated reasoning: the tape-measure method requires the same eventual snap-fold-cut steps anyway, with extra wasted motion first (measure, transfer to two points on the sheet, find a straightedge, align, mark) — framed as "10 extra actions" versus zero for the in-place method.
- **Screw-driving tooling**: use a depth-limiting screw-gun attachment (or an automated screw-feeding tool) that prevents driving screws deep enough to tear through the drywall's paper face.
- **Screw spacing rule for fixing drywall sheets to the frame**: roughly the length of a human palm apart (~20cm).
- **Stretch-ceiling backing-block reminder**: a common oversight while sheathing is forgetting to install a backing block (закладная) for the stretch ceiling's own perimeter track — without it, the track can tear loose from the drywall when the membrane is tensioned during installation. This specific backing block is the one place the crimping tool can't substitute for screws — but the resulting local drywall deformation isn't a concern there, since the screw lands on the block itself and the area is later fully covered by the stretch ceiling.
- **Explicit safety warning**: never brace a screw with a bare finger behind the drywall as a "stop," a common beginner habit — with a 35mm screw this can drive nearly straight through the finger, and removing it afterward is described as "practically impossible." Always wear protective gloves and safety glasses for this work.
