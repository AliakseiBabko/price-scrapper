## Flooring / Materials — Why a Self-Leveling Finish Over Tile Adhesive

- **The problem being solved**: a thick cement-sand screed poured
  between beacons inevitably settles/sags slightly in the strips between
  beacons, leaving the beacons themselves protruding above the settled
  screed surface — an expected imperfection at this pour thickness, not
  a mistake. Since floor height is planned to the millimeter, this
  needs a finishing leveling layer *before* the beacons are pulled.
- **Two finishing options compared, with a clear reasoning gap between
  them**: **tile adhesive** levels the floor but leaves it visually
  rough/unattractive (coarser aggregate); a **thin-layer self-leveling
  compound** achieves both level *and* smooth/attractive, because its
  aggregate is much finer than tile adhesive's and the cured material is
  inherently stronger. Thin-layer pours also use minimal material by
  volume. **The speaker states directly he won't go back to tile
  adhesive for this purpose after trying self-leveling compound.**
- **Named product**: Vetonit 3000 — a thin-layer self-leveling compound,
  pourable 1-5mm thick, compatible with underfloor heating, producing a
  strong, smooth finish. Previously used by this practitioner with good
  results.

## Rough Plumbing / Rules — Primer Step, Explicitly Flagged as Critical

- **Priming the screed before pouring self-leveling compound is not
  optional** — the primer creates a surface film that makes the
  substrate weakly absorbent, letting the compound spread/flow properly.
  Without it, the substrate pulls water out of the fresh mix, risking
  delamination, cracking, or poor flow of the finish layer.
- **Named primer and dilution schedule**: Vetonit MD16 concentrate, ≥40%
  dry residue, suited for substrate prep ahead of leveling compounds and
  self-leveling floors specifically. **Two-coat dilution per the label's
  own table**: first coat at 1:5 (primer:water), second coat at 1:3,
  applied 2 hours after the first. Applied with a roller and extension
  pole.

## Materials / Technique — Mixing, Pouring, and Working-Window Tricks

- **Mixing ratio**: 5.2 liters of water per bag of self-leveling
  compound; mix for 1 minute before pouring.
- **Pour and spread technique**: pour, spread evenly with a
  trowel/spatula, then drag flat with a straightedge (правило). A whole
  room took roughly 30 minutes and 2 bags of mix at this thickness.
- **Working-window trimming trick**: while the compound is still fresh,
  any ridges/bumps can be trimmed off with a spatula; once it's cured
  and gained strength, it can no longer be worked by hand.
- **A practical two-strip pour sequencing technique for a single worker
  (or two-person team)**: pour two strips first, deliberately skipping
  the edge strip; once those two strips have set enough to walk on (~1
  hour at 3mm thickness), stand on them to pour the remaining edge strip
  — avoids having to crawl backward off wet compound and drag the
  straightedge awkwardly toward yourself. Any seam ridges between
  strips can be cleaned up afterward. **Best done as a two-person job**
  — one mixes, one screeds.
- **Cure/walk time at 3mm thickness: walkable after about 1 hour.**
- **Pre-pour surface prep**: grind the cured screed with a diamond cup
  wheel to remove debris, cement laitance/bloom ("цементное молоко"),
  and protruding stones/aggregate that would otherwise catch the
  straightedge — but **explicitly avoid grinding down the beacons
  themselves.**

## Rough Plumbing / Planning Rules — Convector Final-Leveling Sequencing

- **A genuinely reusable sequencing principle**: don't finalize the
  height-critical leveling of a height-sensitive fixture (here, a
  heating convector recessed into the floor) until you have an actual
  physical sample of the finished floor covering on-site — not just its
  spec thickness. The speaker explicitly explains why he didn't finish
  leveling the convector during the screed stage itself: without a
  sample strip/board of the real floor covering in hand, it's too easy
  to get the flush height wrong, especially with mixed-height,
  multi-material floor transitions between rooms.
- **Named floor covering**: an "engineered stone-polymer" plank —
  described as superior to wood/laminate on wear resistance, 100%
  moisture resistance, and minimal linear expansion; oak-look with a
  realistic wood texture/feel and an integrated underlayment on the
  back.
- **Final leveling technique**: floor-panel samples placed on both sides
  of the convector as a height reference, adjusted via leveling bolts,
  then locked in place with side brackets (bolts driven downward and
  sideways into the convector body).
- **Thermostat control-cable routing, with a reconsidered design
  choice**: the temperature-control dial connects via a steel cable to a
  thermo-head. Two install options exist — one that allows the
  thermo-head mechanism to be swapped later, one that doesn't. The
  speaker initially installed the simpler non-replaceable version, then
  reconsidered and redid it with a corrugated conduit sleeve specifically
  so the mechanism could be serviced/replaced later. **A reusable
  planning-flexibility lesson**: route a control cable/mechanism with a
  service sleeve even when a simpler direct routing looks fine initially.
- **Convector embedding technique**: wrapped in damper tape (2 layers)
  before final screed/mortar embedding; sealed additionally with tile
  adhesive on the window side.

## Mistakes / Warnings — A Forgotten Leak-Protection Cable, Caught Before Finishing

- **A genuine "caught before it was too late" mistake**: the signal cable
  connecting the Neptun leak-protection inlet valves to the control unit
  had been forgotten during the earlier plumbing stage and needed to be
  retrofitted through the corridor screed before finishing the floor.
- **Retrofit routing technique**: routed along the screed's perimeter,
  taking advantage of the 10mm perimeter damper-tape gap (already in
  place per the episode #18 screed build) — only needed to chip out one
  corner to route the cable through, rather than cutting a fresh chase.
- **Cable spec**: two multi-strand 0.75mm² conductors, 12V signal from
  the control unit that triggers the Neptun valves to close. Routed
  through corrugated conduit under the screed in the toilet, hidden
  behind a pipe with cable clamps in the technical cabinet, then through
  the screed via the toilet to the control unit's location; the chase
  was then patched over.
