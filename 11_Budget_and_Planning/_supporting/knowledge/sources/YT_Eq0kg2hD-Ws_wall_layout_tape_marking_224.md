## Walls / Ceilings — Layout Marking Technique

- **Painter's-tape marking is the only correct method for temporary first-course layout marking — not pencil, not chalk-and-string, not spray paint.** Explained mechanism: whatever marks the floor for the first-course mortar bed will be *covered* by that cement-sand mortar once the first row of blocks is laid — a pencil line or a chalked/painted line is illegible under a mortar layer, but a strip of tape stays visible in outline even once mortar is dumped on top of it, because the mortar doesn't need to bond to the marking (it needs to bond to the floor/waterproofing beneath, and doesn't care whether that's exposed subfloor or a taped-over strip). Two parallel tape strips define each wall's footprint; the installer aligns the first course visually against the tape edges once mortar is placed. `confirmed`, explicit mechanism, genuinely new marking-method rationale for this store (previous sources recorded laser-projection+chalk-line as the layout method, not a discussion of *why* tape specifically beats chalk/pencil/paint for this step).
- **Squaring/perpendicular-layout method using a self-leveling laser + tape measure (no laser-projected corner method)**: place the laser unit *along* the existing wall you want to build perpendicular to (not against the target wall), roughly in the position the perpendicular line should cross; enable the vertical axis; use the laser's fine-adjustment screw to rotate it until the beam-to-wall distance is equal at two measured points along that wall (confirmed with a tape measure at both points) — this makes the laser's vertical plane parallel to the reference wall. The laser unit's own construction guarantees its two beams (front/back) are exactly 90° to each other, so once one beam is parallel to the reference wall, the perpendicular beam is a true perpendicular by construction — no separate right-angle check needed.
- **Point-transfer technique from an established perpendicular plane**: measure from the room corner to the laser's beam-line to get one offset (worked example: 4205mm), then measure from that line to the desired construction-start point (worked example: 440mm) — sum/subtract as needed, then physically walk that same 440mm offset out from the beam line with a tape measure to place the actual start point, tape from there.
- **Practical layout worked examples from the source's own project** (kitchen wall + adjoining WC/shower layout): a 600mm clearance offset from a load-bearing column, held specifically to leave room for a floor-standing trash bin under the future kitchen counter; a 100mm-thick partition offset 900mm from a column to leave an 800mm alcove for a concealment cabinet hiding utility runs; bathroom layout driven by only 2-3 key dimensions (a 1300mm corridor/future-bedroom clearance, then 1200mm for the WC, remainder becomes the shower) rather than the "dozens of dimensions" the source claims typical uncurated floor plans specify for the same small area — framed as the deliberate advantage of a project with few, load-bearing dimensions vs. many, all nominally "critical."
- **Timing claim**: two walls (kitchen) laid out and taped in ~3 minutes on-camera (~2 minutes claimed off-camera without filming pauses); the WC/bathroom perimeter (3 walls + a duct-box jog) in ~5 minutes. `unverified` — self-reported production-speed claim by the source's own crew, no independent corroboration.
- **Corollary rule reinforced but not new**: dimensions that are truly load-bearing for fixture fit (a bathtub, a fixed-width doorway) must be held exactly; all other room dimensions can and should be treated as flexible/"rubber" and simply accepted as whatever they come out to once the load-bearing dimensions are fixed — matches this store's existing hard-vs-flexible-dimension framing from other sources in this same chunk (see `yt_sJ8UZj36TMQ`), independently corroborated here on a genuinely different (kitchen-renovation, not commercial-clinic) project.
