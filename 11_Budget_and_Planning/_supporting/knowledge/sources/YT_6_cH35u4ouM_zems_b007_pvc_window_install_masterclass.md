## Doors / Trim (window fastening is grouped here per this store's existing
Windows/Slopes convention — see Target Page(s) below)

- **Window-opening "quarter" (четверть) anatomy and thermal rationale**:
  the quarter is the reveal/ledge the frame recesses into (brick in brick
  buildings, poured concrete in panel buildings, wood in wood-frame
  buildings). A quarter improves thermal performance because the frame
  sits recessed a few centimeters, protecting the first foam joint on all
  sides — vs. a flush/direct opening where the frame simply abuts the
  wall with only a thin foam layer, which loses heat faster. **Explicitly
  stated caveat**: a quarter gives no fastening advantage — the same wind
  load acts on the fasteners either way. `confirmed`, mechanism-based.
- **Correct vs. incorrect frame fastening — direct fastening only**:
  frames must be fastened with drilled-through holes using an anchor bolt
  or a screw/dowel ("нагель") directly through the profile into the
  structural opening. **Fastening (mounting) plates alone cannot resist
  wind loads and must not be used as the final structural fastening** —
  legitimate use is preliminary/temporary positioning only. Frame drain
  holes near the fastening points are a deliberate manufacturer feature
  (to prevent condensate accumulation, offset so snow can't enter) — not
  a defect, addressing a common misconception. `confirmed`, stated as
  long-held practice ("since 2012").
- **Mounting wedges (монтажные клинья), never wood scraps, for leveling
  and load-bearing support**: wedges are cheap, never rot, and level
  reliably. Using cut pieces of old wooden window frame ("дрова") as
  shims is called out as a bad, historically-common practice ("maybe
  acceptable in the 1990s"). **One narrow legitimate exception**: when the
  foam joint gap exceeds **80mm**, treated hardwood timber (not scrap
  softwood, not construction-site offcuts) may be used — never as a
  load-bearing shim substitute, always alongside proper wedges.
  `confirmed`, numeric threshold given.
- **Balcony-block door+window joining procedure** (joining a separate door
  and window frame into one balcony block before installation):
  1. Drill the window frame on the side opposite the hinges, hole spacing
     ≤60cm (a standard window length fits ~3 holes), drilled from the
     outer face of the frame to avoid damaging the gasket with the drill's
     rotating shaft, centered on the frame's fastening reinforcement
     channel.
  2. **Two-stage foam-joint technique for the door/window sill gap**:
     apply foam along the joining edge with the hinge-side frame face-down
     on the floor, wait for the foam's **primary cure/skin** (becomes
     slightly springy with a non-tacky surface) before assembling —
     disturbing uncured foam collapses its expansion and leaves a weak,
     eventually-freezing joint. Remove the protective film from the
     connector profile before install (otherwise the profiles can't
     properly clamp). Apply a **second, smaller foam bead** on top of the
     nearly-cured first bead to reactivate/re-bond the two sections for a
     stronger final joint.
  3. Join the two frames with self-tapping screws long enough to pass
     through the first frame and seat in the second frame's internal
     reinforcement — **explicitly do not over-tighten**, this deforms the
     profile.
- **Three-stage perimeter foam-seam technique (not a single application)**:
  large gaps/voids must be pre-filled first; then the continuous perimeter
  seam is formed **filling only ~25% initially**, so the foam expands
  properly without over-thickening and losing polymerization quality;
  finally the cured excess is trimmed flush. A joint formed "in one pass"
  at full thickness cannot cure properly at that thickness and reliably
  develops voids/fistulas ("свищи") — even a 3-4mm void (about the
  diameter of a standard screw) is enough to defeat the window's thermal
  benefit entirely, and an improperly-formed seam typically has several
  dozen such voids. **Mastic/pre-installing sashes and glazing units
  before foaming is called out as a workaround some installers use to
  limit frame deformation from over-thick foam application — it may
  reduce deformation but does not fix the underlying poor cure/void
  problem.** `confirmed`, mechanism-based, with a stated failure-mode
  timeline (visible ice chunks on the upper slope appear "within 2-3
  years" if the seam was rushed in one pass).
- **Tape/multi-point window hardware (ленточная фурнитура) quality
  checklist** — described as "the single most important detail" of a PVC
  window:
  1. Should offer **at minimum 4 positions**: closed; standard open;
     tilted (откинуто); and **trickle/slot ventilation** (щелевое
     проветривание) — a distinct locked-but-not-fully-sealed mode that
     lets air circulate around the sash perimeter while keeping all locks
     engaged (demonstrated with a banknote test: firmly pinched when
     fully closed, freely movable in slot-ventilation mode, sash still
     cannot be opened).
  2. Reliability of individual mechanism components — cites the "tilt
     scissor" hinge mechanism specifically as a marker of build quality
     (should be substantial/robust, not flimsy).
  3. Low-maintenance — good hardware only needs a few drops of oil in a
     dedicated port periodically.
  4. **Foolproofing** — should physically prevent an inexperienced user
     from combining an open+tilted state simultaneously (handle can't
     rotate to the tilt position until the sash is fully closed).
  5. **Anti-burglary resistance** — quality hardware's engagement/keeper
     hardware is visibly substantial even in base configuration (without a
     dedicated anti-burglary kit) vs. generic hardware that can be pried
     apart by hand and defeated with a light strike.
- **Water sill (отлив/window sill flashing) quality checklist**:
  1. Must be **factory-manufactured** on precision equipment, not
     hand-bent on-site from sheet metal at a market stall — hand-bent
     sills have rounded/imprecise edges and dimensional variance of
     **2-3mm** between nominally identical pieces, causing visible gaps
     when butting sections together (a genuine factory sill's edge-to-edge
     tolerance is under **1mm**).
  2. Must ship with a factory-applied **protective film**, and that film
     **must be removed immediately after install** — leaving it on
     "to keep it looking new" is a documented bad habit even among
     professional window-company installers; UV and thermal cycling
     permanently bond parts of the film to the sill while other parts
     peel and bubble within a few years, becoming impossible to fully
     remove without replacing the sill.
  3. Must have a factory-applied **three-layer sound/vibration-damping
     backing pad** (adhesive layer + vibration-damping layer + sound
     insulation layer) — without it, rain and wind-induced sill flex
     generate substantial noise; a pad applied by hand on-site tends to
     delay/peel, unlike one applied at time of manufacture.
- **Opening-widening procedure for an oversized standard balcony-block
  door** (worked numeric example): standard new-build balcony-block door
  openings are often oversized (door leaf ≈85cm from a 90cm frame width)
  — called out as bulky and swinging into roughly half the kitchen when
  open; the speaker's own comfortable/optimal balcony-door width is
  **60-65cm** (states he passes through his own 60cm door without
  difficulty despite not being "skinny"); a wider/heavier door is also
  less durable (more hinge load, more self-weight deformation over time).
  Reducing door width also reclaims wall depth on the other side of the
  opening — in the worked example, ~50cm of depth (too narrow even for a
  fridge) is widened to fit a 65cm-deep refrigerator by moving the
  balcony-block frame over with a false-wall/framed-out extension:
  - Target dimension: door/window frame should recess ~3cm into the new
    quarter, leaving a ~17cm margin on the narrow side (worked example
    figures).
  - Measure the required quarter extension (distance from old quarter to
    new frame's center) — worked example: 17.5cm, achieved with two
    lumber pieces (10cm + 7cm thick).
  - **Antiseptic treatment method**: apply antiseptic to the lumber with a
    sprayer/paint gun rather than a brush — stated to use the **same
    total product volume** either way (a brush doesn't save product), but
    a brush takes longer and creates more mess; **cure time ~24 hours**
    before the lumber is workable (no longer tacky to the touch).
  - Foam must be driven **at least 3cm deep** into gaps between the new
    lumber framing and the structural balcony beam, not just surface-
    applied; irregular small gaps between the lumber pieces themselves are
    better sealed by wrapping insulation material around the assembly
    first, foam second.
  - **Lumber-offset rule to avoid a common beginner mistake**: the thinner
    outer lumber piece should be set back ~7cm from where the frame's
    edge will land (not flush to it) — flush placement is easier to
    fasten to but forces cutting an angled notch into the corner later to
    fit slope/reveal panels properly; the recessed approach avoids that
    rework.
  - Cure time before trimming excess foam on this framing: **~47 minutes**
    stated for the primary cure to finish (foam no longer tacky).
  - **Fastening plates ARE the correct method specifically at the top of
    a widened opening** when the header material (e.g. foam-block/plaster
    infill) can't hold direct fasteners: install the frame, level/true it
    up on one side first using plates, only then fasten the plate on the
    second side — fastening both sides before leveling risks a permanently
    bowed ("house-shaped") frame.
- **On-site installation sequencing and QC checks** (final assembly):
  set frame in opening, temporarily fix and level using a spirit level
  and mounting wedges (wedges stay permanently as load-bearing pads — a
  wood shim used the same way is explicitly forbidden even though
  visually similar). **Temporary support-block technique** to avoid
  bending the frame while torquing a dowel/anchor: use a temporary support
  block so the fastener can be fully torqued without deforming the
  profile. **Correct dowel-torque technique**: many installers stop
  tightening as soon as the fastener head first touches/dents the plastic
  — this under-tightens and holds nothing; correct technique is to
  continue until the head passes through the plastic and seats against
  the frame's internal reinforcement, then back off half a turn once
  deformation is first visible.
