## Walls / Ceilings

- **Apartment has 5 distinct substrate types needing different prep**:
  AAC/aerated-concrete block partitions (most crack-prone); silicate-brick
  block bathroom walls (visually similar to but distinct from AAC —
  explicitly flagged as a common confusion); monolithic load-bearing
  concrete (including ventilation-duct surrounds, a frequently problematic
  smooth surface where plaster debonds); partially-retained developer
  plaster (kept where flat, needing correction only); and expanded-clay-
  concrete block sections where developer plaster had already failed,
  leaving only a thin spatter-bond ("обрызг") layer. **Material joints
  between any two of these types are the highest crack-risk zones**, along
  with corners generally.
- **Developer plaster requires mechanical resurfacing even when visually
  flat**: developer plastering crews often skip the final "float and
  smooth" step correctly, or over-wet already-drying plaster to force a
  smoothable finish ("wet-on-wet" - "мокрое на мокрое") — this leaves a
  weak surface skin ("гипсовое молоко"/gypsum laitance) that later coats
  (putty/plaster) can bond to unreliably and delaminate from later.
  **Fix**: sand the entire surface with a long-reach drywall sander
  ("жираф"-type, e.g. named budget model DLT R 7232) fitted with 60-80
  grit vacuum-hole sandpaper, connected to a shop vacuum — the vacuum
  suction also stabilizes the sander against the wall (less bounce,
  less arm fatigue) versus doing it dust-free by hand. Manual scraping is
  explicitly called inferior to machine sanding ("night and day"
  difference). Real per-wall time: ~15-25 minutes. Rounded corners
  (unreachable by the sander disc) are corrected with a chisel plus a
  sanding block. **Governing rule: a leveling/skim coat can only be applied
  over a *stronger* substrate, never the reverse** — sanding exposes a
  rougher, sound surface that a subsequent coat bonds to properly.
- **Primer sequencing and double-priming rule for absorbent substrates**:
  deep-penetrating primer (named: Ceresit CT 17 Pro, ~2hr dry time, yellow
  pigment indicates coverage) applied by airless sprayer normally (garden
  pump sprayer or roller as budget alternatives). **Highly absorbent
  substrates — AAC block walls specifically — get primed twice**: first
  pass diluted 1:1 with water, second pass (after the first fully dries,
  ~2hr) applied undiluted — reduces substrate absorbency, which matters
  because plaster losing moisture too fast to an over-absorbent substrate
  is a direct crack-formation mechanism.
- **Chase (штроба) filling rule, and a real named misconception**: chases
  for electrical cable must always be filled/patched before plastering —
  a "plaster will just cover the cable" assumption is **explicitly wrong**,
  since plaster doesn't reliably achieve full depth coverage everywhere; an
  unfilled chase risks a visible surface groove later, since plaster
  applied thicker there settles/sinks differently. Cables are bundled and
  tucked into back-box holes (stuffed with crumpled paper as a temporary
  cavity filler if needed) before chase-filling.
- **Reinforcing mesh (fiberglass, facade-type, alkali-resistant so
  cement-based adhesive doesn't degrade it, 160g/m², 5×5mm cell) is
  mandatory for AAC block partitions specifically** — AAC is the most
  deformation-prone substrate in this apartment. Silicate block, expanded-
  clay-concrete block, and brick are explicitly *not* mandatory to mesh
  (lower deformation risk) but meshing them anyway is stated as
  strictly beneficial, never harmful. **Mesh is not a guarantee against
  cracking** — a serious building-settlement event can still tear the mesh
  — but is described as the only technique that actually meaningfully
  resists ordinary crack formation.
- **Mesh application method 1 (classic, adhesive-embedded)**: named
  product Ceresit CT 85 (micro-fiber-reinforced adhesive suited to a base
  plaster coat) is trowel-applied in dabs then leveled, mesh is pressed
  into the wet adhesive, then a second thin coat covers/embeds it fully
  (adhesive squeezes through the mesh weave as a visual check that
  embedding is complete). Adjacent mesh strips overlap by 10cm, and mesh
  is run 10cm onto adjoining corners/ceiling for continuity. A pre-planned
  cut list (measure wall run lengths first) avoids wasting mesh rolls.
- **Mesh application method 2 (embedded directly in plaster body, not
  glued)**: mesh is temporarily screwed to the wall (into the foam
  perimeter gap between wall and ceiling) *before* beacons (маяки) are
  set, positioned so it can be folded aside during plastering, then folded
  back and pressed into the wet plaster coat once applied, followed by a
  second plaster coat over it and leveling — the mesh ends up embedded
  inside the plaster body itself rather than under it. Demonstrated on a
  silicate-block wall (not mandatory for that substrate) purely to show
  the technique.
- **Three joint/crack-control techniques at dissimilar-material seams, in
  ascending sophistication**: (1) classic — fill/foam the joint gap if it's
  mortar-filled, then apply adhesive/plaster, embed mesh, cover with a
  second coat; (2) same as (1) plus a drywall-profile foam sealing tape
  laid directly over the seam line before the adhesive/mesh layer — a
  crack that eventually forms stays trapped under the tape rather than
  reaching the surface; (3) same mechanism as (2) but with a stretchable
  composite tape (named: Straight Flex, color-agnostic — red/green/orange
  are equivalent) instead of the profile sealing tape.
- **Monolithic-concrete/precast-panel prep for gypsum plaster**: grind the
  surface with an angle grinder + diamond cup wheel to remove cement
  laitance ("cement milk") and open surface pores — cement laitance is
  described as the weak link that a concrete-bonding primer can peel away
  along with if not removed first. Then prime with deep-penetrating primer,
  foam-fill gaps around ventilation-duct penetrations, trim cured foam, and
  mesh-reinforce that seam.
- **Concrete-bonding primer ("бетон-контакт") technique and a named
  mistake**: Ceresit CT 19 concrete-contact primer must be mixed
  thoroughly before use (a hex-bit substituted for a proper mixer paddle
  when one wasn't on hand); apply by brush/roller/texture-sprayer in a
  **deliberately thick coat that dries with a "fur"/nap texture, not
  rolled flat** — rolling it thin ("to zero") leaves too little exposed
  quartz sand on the surface, and **quartz sand, not the pink acrylic
  binder, is what actually anchors the subsequent gypsum plaster coat** —
  the acrylic is only a binder, not the bonding mechanism itself. Dry time
  ~3 hours; speaker recommends applying well ahead of when it's needed.
  Named mistake to avoid: skipping cardboard floor protection underneath
  (leads to a messy floor cleanup afterward).
