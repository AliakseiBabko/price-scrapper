## Durable Facts

- **Toggle-position consistency**: throughout an apartment, all *standard* switches (not two-way/проходной, not intermediate/перекрестный) should rest in the same physical position for OFF across the whole home — top half protruding = OFF, top half pressed flush = ON. This rule explicitly does **not** apply to two-way or intermediate switches, whose position alone doesn't indicate on/off state (it depends on the paired switch too). `unverified`
- Reasoning given for the OFF/ON convention: entering a dark room, you reach *upward* toward the switch to turn light ON; leaving a lit room, you reach it from a lower/different angle to turn it OFF — the two reach angles differ, which the speaker uses to justify designing for protruding-top = OFF. `unverified`, speaker's own reasoning.
- Height/offset repeated from elsewhere in this batch: ~90–95 cm from finished floor, ~15 cm from the doorway's rough edge (same channel, not independent corroboration — see `YT_l_rjjPlPkRo_switch_height_offset_rules.md`).
- **Gang/key-count preference**: a 2×1-gang arrangement (two separate single-key switches) is always preferable to one 2-gang (2-key) switch; likewise 3 separate 1-gang switches are preferable to one 3-gang switch. Reasoning: narrow multi-key switch plates are easy to mis-press (hitting the wrong adjacent key). `unverified`, ergonomic opinion.
- **Switch position should signal which circuit it controls**: e.g. over a bed, the switch nearest the door/entry side should control the main/ceiling light (matching the switch used on entry); switches farther out should control each side's own nightstand light. Explicitly warns against using one switch for the main light and a second single switch for BOTH nightstand lights combined — occupants have to memorize an arbitrary mapping and commonly press the wrong one. States (unsourced) that in an estimated "95% of clients," partners have different sleep/wake times, so each side needs its own independently switchable nightlight. `unverified`, speaker's own estimate/opinion, no data source given for the 95% figure — treat as anecdotal, not measured.
- When a tall headboard forces switches onto the wall above the nightstands (instead of at room entry): "textbook" guidance is to place the nightstand-light switch nearer the sleeping position and the main-light switch farther away; the speaker states his own default (from "years of practice") is the **opposite** — main-light switch closer to the sleeper (as the two-way switch, since main light is used far more often than the nightlight) and the nightstand switch farther. He recommends confirming this preference per-client rather than assuming either default. `unverified`, speaker explicitly flags this as differing from the textbook.
- **Adjoining-room switches**: switches for two adjacent rooms should be on the SAME side of their respective doorways as the door handle, so the hand motion that closes one room's door continues naturally to the next room's switch. Exception: if two separate doors both open into one shared hallway/foyer, placing each door's switch on its own convenient side (rather than forcing hand-side matching) is acceptable and, per the speaker's practice, often preferred. `unverified`
- **Future-door contingency**: if a room currently has an open doorway (no door) but might get a door installed later (example: a kitchen pass-through sized to match a standard interior door opening), never place a switch inside that door's future swing zone — retrofitting the door later would require relocating the switch (opening finished walls/tile, replastering, re-wallpapering), a cost the speaker frames as far larger than the door itself. `unverified`, concrete and checkable anti-pattern.
