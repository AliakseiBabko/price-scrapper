## Mistakes / Warnings — Plaster Acceptance Technique and Failure Modes

- **⚠️ 2-meter straightedge ("правило"), 2mm gap tolerance — the actual code-referenced acceptance test, not a subjective "looks flat" judgment**: a 2m straightedge held against the wall; any gap exceeding 2mm along its length is out of tolerance ("не допуск") and the wall section fails. The speaker's own crews plaster tight enough that the straightedge shouldn't visibly rock at all.
- **⚠️ Straightedge must be applied in a full pattern, not wherever the crew points**: horizontal, vertical, and diagonal, repeated roughly every meter (matching typical beacon/маяк spacing of 1–1.5m, since gaps concentrate exactly where a beacon was removed) — plus a mandatory separate pass specifically along any wall zone where **baseboards** will run and along **door/window opening returns**, since these are where sag/bow is both most common and most visible once trim is installed. The subscriber's own self-acceptance had skipped the baseboard-zone and door-jamb checks entirely, and both failed once actually measured (34mm and more found in a baseboard zone against the 2mm norm).
- **⚠️ Named mechanism for baseboard-zone bulging**: crews plastering to finish quickly often don't bother re-trimming excess material as it sags down the wall while curing, leaving a bulge concentrated near the floor — invisible to a general glance at the wall but fatal to a rigid baseboard (MDF/polymer) trying to follow that surface; a cheap flexible plastic baseboard can mask it, a rigid one cannot, forcing a sealant-filled gap that likely won't even color-match.
- **⚠️ 90° corner check with a right-angle tool ("угольник"), at multiple points per corner, is a separate test from flatness** — required wherever built-in furniture, wall-hung fixtures, or fitted cabinetry will land (wardrobe corners, kitchen cabinet returns, bathtub-to-wall corners): a factory-square appliance/fixture pressed into an out-of-square corner leaves a visible gap on one side or simply won't fit a non-custom unit. In this inspection, a walk-in closet's corners, a kitchen cabinet corner, and both bathroom corners (behind an acrylic tub and a washing machine) all failed a 90° check that had never been performed at acceptance.
- **⚠️ Raking/grazing light ("лампа Лосева") inspection reveals surface micro-texture invisible to the naked eye or to a straightedge check alone**: placing a lamp at a shallow raking angle against the wall throws long shadows across every small bump/dip in the plaster surface — used specifically to verify a "smooth as an eggshell" finish before high-quality paint (where every texture flaw will show under normal room light), and treated as less critical (though still a quality signal) under wallpaper, which itself hides minor texture. No inspector normally checks with this lamp at handover — it is presented as an extra, non-standard diagnostic the crew doesn't expect.
- **⚠️ Gypsum plaster beacons ("маяки") must be physically removed before finishing with cement plaster — leaving them is a code violation ("нарушение технологии"), not a shortcut**: when a crew leaves gypsum beacons embedded and closes over them with cement-based plaster/adhesive (common in economy-segment bathroom work, on the reasoning that "the tile will hide it"), the two materials react and the gypsum degrades, which can crack the plaster and, in a tiled wet room, take the tile down with it. The removal requires cutting out plaster to the full width around each beacon and re-filling with cement-based material — confirmed in this case by physically digging into a wall and finding a metal beacon glued in with gypsum.
- **⚠️ Beacon-removal is bundled into a flat per-m² plastering price, so it's invisible on a quote and easy to skip unnoticed**: the speaker states a typical quoted rate like "350 RUB/m²" for plastering already includes beacon installation *and* removal as part of that single line item — an economy-segment crew skipping the removal step is pocketing that included labor time rather than passing savings to the client, and the client has no way to see this from the quote itself.
- **⚠️ Protect fire-alarm sensors, the electrical panel, and any exposed pipe insulation *before* plastering, not just clean them after**: this crew plastered directly around an un-removed smoke detector and an open electrical panel, coating both in plaster/gypsum dust, and left pipe thermal-insulation sleeves caked in the same debris. Correct sequence: detach/cap the sensor and pull its cover, plaster around the opening, then reinstall — otherwise the sensor may stop functioning correctly (or false-trigger) and someone has to disassemble, clean, and reseal it after the fact, adding real time no one budgeted for.
- **⚠️ Protect finished floors during plastering, or pay for it later in sanding**: this apartment's floors were left uncovered during wall plastering; the resulting gypsum dust bonded into the concrete and must now be fully sanded off before any self-leveling floor compound can be applied — self-leveling compound cannot bond reliably over gypsum residue. Quoted cost for this specific case: **≈20,000 RUB (≈$220) for a 63 m² apartment** to sand the floors clean, purely because plastic sheeting wasn't laid down during the plastering stage.
- **⚠️ Plumbing rough-in should default to manifold/collector ("коллекторная") routing over branched-tee ("тройниковая") routing specifically to bound leak risk under a finished wall**: with tee routing embedded in walls, every one of the many tee joints (the speaker estimates 30–50 across an apartment) is a potential leak point that stays invisible until water actually appears — often only when a downstairs neighbor complains. Manifold routing concentrates all joints at one accessible manifold location, so a leak is visible immediately at that single point rather than hidden inside a wall for an unknown period. Tee routing is described as still functionally workable, just carrying materially higher hidden-defect risk once sealed behind finish.
- **⚠️ Installation-frame (инсталляция) concealment box height must clear the cistern/actuator-panel by more than the frame's own quoted height** — in this case the installed box was built to 120cm total, but the concealed frame/cistern assembly needs roughly 20cm of clearance above its own top for panel access, leaving under 100cm actually usable and no way to reach the panel without opening the box; the plumber who installed it never asked what would sit above or how it would be accessed later, and simply "did the toilet" with no follow-up questions about the surrounding built-in structure.
