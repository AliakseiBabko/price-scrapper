## Walls / Ceilings — Bathroom-Specific Plastering: 90° Method + 4 Substrate-Prep Methods

- **Why bathroom plastering gets extra scrutiny, stated reasoning**:
  bathroom finish work (tile + tile labor) is typically the single most
  expensive finish stage in an apartment renovation — a later plaster/tile
  delamination failure is correspondingly the most costly mistake to have
  to redo. Beyond level/flatness, bathroom walls specifically need
  verified **90° corner geometry**, driven by built-in fixtures/furniture
  fit.
- **90°-corner technique for a bathroom, extending ep.12's general method,
  specifically solving for equal door-reveal length**: requires a laser
  level with **two mutually perpendicular 360° beams** (a specific tool
  capability, not just any laser). Mark equal offset distances (e.g. 7cm,
  arbitrary but must match) on both sides of the door-opening wall from
  the corner; place the laser at those marks so its beam matches both —
  this captures the exterior wall's already-plastered level plane.
  Switch on the laser's perpendicular beam and mark points along it, then
  move the laser inside the room and re-set it against the interior wall,
  offset from the highest point by the beacon's own thickness (6mm in
  this job) — this keeps the interior reference plane strictly parallel
  to the exterior one, which is specifically what guarantees the future
  door's reveal ("откосы") comes out equal length on both sides once
  installed. From there, the same straightedge-thickness offset marking
  and beacon-installation method from ep.12 applies to every bathroom
  wall.
- **Beacon-prep details, corroborating and extending ep.12/13**: cut
  beacons (bought in 3m stock) with an angle grinder + metal-cutting disc,
  never metal shears (shears crimp/deform the beacon profile); trim ~2cm
  off factory ends since they're often bent/damaged in shipping; length =
  ceiling height minus 1cm. Eye protection required when grinding.
- **Beacon-setting adhesive, a real product-driven technique change**:
  sets beacons using the same cement-sand wall plaster itself (named:
  Holcim) rather than gypsum plaster — states this specific cement-sand
  product's stickiness is now close enough to gypsum that he's stopped
  reaching for gypsum plaster just for beacon-setting.
- **Core substrate-prep problem this episode addresses**: most
  cement-sand plasters (unlike gypsum) aren't inherently sticky and risk
  delaminating on smooth block substrates specifically — though some
  modern polymer-modified cement-sand plasters have enough
  plasticity/stickiness to behave closer to gypsum. Speaker still preps
  the substrate regardless of the specific product's stated quality, as
  insurance against the high cost of a later tile-plaster delamination
  failure.
- **Method 1 (bonding coat of the same plaster)**: notched-trowel a thin
  skim coat of the same cement-sand plaster onto the wall first, then
  throw dabs onto that pre-coated area — works especially well paired
  with an already-sticky (polymer-rich) plaster product.
- **Method 2 (tile adhesive as bonding layer)**: named product Ceresit
  CM16 (acknowledged as possibly overkill — any C2-class tile adhesive
  works) combed on horizontally with a notched trowel, left to cure a
  full 24 hours into a hard, textured substrate that grips smooth block
  surfaces far better than bare block. **Sequencing caution**: plaster
  only opposite/parallel walls in one working pass, not two adjacent
  walls — working a fresh corner between two just-plastered adjacent
  walls risks damaging the corner edge with the straightedge; do adjacent
  walls on separate days instead.
- **Method 3 (dedicated bonding additive, spatter-coat texture)**: named
  additive Ceresit CC81 mixed per its own package ratio table into a
  cement/sand/water slurry, brushed on as a rough spatter-coat texture
  onto a lightly dampened (not wet) substrate; cures ~24h before plaster
  goes on; produces a substrate the speaker states is "already good for
  tiling" even before the plaster coat.
- **Method 4 (same bonding additive, wet-on-wet application)**: same
  CC81 slurry as method 3, but applied as a continuous coat and plastered
  *immediately* while still wet — a technique normally used for bonding
  thin-layer screeds/pours, which the speaker experimented with applying
  to wall plaster instead. Reports a strong, well-adhered result — "the
  additive literally glues the plaster to the substrate."
- **Cement-sand plaster consistency check**: draw a trowel through the
  mixed plaster — correct consistency holds the groove shape without
  flowing back/slumping.
- **Named hopper-gun product and real working notes**: "Рик Сталь," a
  Belarusian (Gomel-manufactured), stainless-steel hopper gun, described
  as ideal specifically for cement-sand plaster (also usable for gypsum).
  Needs only a modest air compressor (his: 50L tank sufficient). **Lets a
  genuinely untrained helper apply plaster competently on a first
  attempt** — demonstrated live with his assistant successfully using it
  the first time. Markedly faster than hand-throwing plaster.
- **Cement-sand plaster cure/trim timing, contrasted directly with
  gypsum plaster**: trim after ~5 hours of set time — far more forgiving
  than gypsum's ~40-50 minute window (ep.13); can even be left overnight
  and trimmed the next morning while still workable. Stated directly as
  genuinely easier to work with in this respect than gypsum plaster.
- **Deliberate wet-room chase-fill choice**: fills every beacon-channel
  chase flush in the bathroom specifically (rather than leaving it, an
  option a non-wet-room wall might tolerate) because the later
  waterproofing membrane needs a continuous flat substrate to bond to
  correctly.
