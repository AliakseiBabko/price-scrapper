## Durable Facts

- **Purpose/value of temporary electrical**: installing temporary lighting+outlets at the start of a renovation is claimed to shorten the overall renovation timeline by roughly a month versus working without it, particularly relevant in winter when daylight is short. `unverified`, single-account claim, no measurement methodology given.
- **Cable spec — permanent vs. temporary wiring**: for permanent electrical, the company's stated standard is 3×1.5 mm² cable for lighting circuits and 3×2.5 mm² for outlet/socket circuits (both include a ground conductor). For **temporary** construction-stage wiring, they use 2×2.5 mm² cable (no ground, since it isn't required for the temporary setup) with lighting and outlets sharing a single circuit. `unverified`, single-account stated practice — not a code citation, and worth flagging that no-ground temporary wiring is a construction-stage-only practice, not something to carry into a permanent installation.
- **Materials list for temp wiring**: 3-outlet power strip blocks, self-clamping (push-in/spring) terminal connectors rated for stranded wire, self-adhesive cable-mounting pads, plastic cable ties, cable, light-bulb sockets/holders and bulbs.
- **Bulb quantity rule of thumb**: buy at least 2x the number of rooms in bulbs, since bulbs are treated as a consumable that regularly burns out in construction/dust conditions, plus expect to buy more during the renovation itself. `unverified`, single-account rule of thumb.
- **Temp light placement**: mark future light positions as close to the geometric center of each future room as possible — placing them by even spacing *before* interior walls exist looks even at that stage but ends up misplaced once walls are actually built. Precision doesn't need to be exact — a tolerance of about ±0.5 m from the room's eventual center is called sufficient; no need to spend time on precise diagonal-based centering. `unverified`, single-account guidance.
- **Sequencing rule**: install temporary wiring only *after* the ceiling has been primed, not before — priming after wiring would mean priming around/over cables and lamp holders, creating extra difficulty; priming first avoids this. `unverified`, single-account sequencing rule but internally logical.
- **Mounting-pad spacing**: self-adhesive cable-mounting pads placed along a laser-line (or a chalk-snap string line if no laser tool is available) at roughly 60 cm intervals — described as "about half a meter plus a bit," not precisely measured with a tape. Spacing pads too far apart causes visible cable sag; spacing them too densely is called wasted, unnecessary work. Adhesive pads are reported to hold securely for a couple of months until the permanent electrical rough-in replaces the temporary run — contrasted favorably with an older method (dowels + perforated mounting tape) described as noisy, messy, and much slower; each mounting point with adhesive pads is claimed to be roughly 10x faster than the dowel method. `unverified`, single-account, both the multiplier and the multi-month adhesion claim.
- **Cable prep workflow**: cut cable into ~1.5 m lengths (one length per room) from the coil, and strip both ends of each length while working on the ground/table — explicitly warned against the common mistake of hanging cables first and then stripping them while standing on a stepladder, which is described as much less efficient than doing all prep work at ground level first, then hanging/connecting.
- **Bulb-to-outlet clearance rule**: keep at least 1 meter of distance between a temporary light-bulb socket and an outlet block. Two reasons given: (1) bulb heat near a cord can melt/damage cord insulation; (2) plugging power tools into a nearby outlet causes repeated cable-tugging that knocks the bulb loose. `unverified`, single-account, but the underlying heat/tugging mechanisms are plausible and generalizable cautions.
- **Drop-cable length rules**: keep the light-fixture drop cable short (~20 cm) — cited mistake of making it too long is that the bulb ends up hanging low enough to glare in your eyes or get bumped by your head repeatedly. Outlet-block drop cables should be long enough (~1.5 m total lead length from the main run to the outlet block) that the outlet block hangs low enough to reach without a stepladder — cited mistake is making outlet drops too short, forcing workers to fetch a stepladder every time they need to plug in a tool.
- **Wire-joining method**: self-clamping/spring-type connectors for stranded wire are described as reliable over 10+ years of the company's own use; twisted joints, even taped, are described as having repeatedly failed for them, especially with black tape (vs. blue tape, which is described as somewhat less unreliable but still inferior to spring connectors). `unverified`, single-account.
- **Panel connection safety step**: when connecting the main incoming cable into the temporary/electrical panel, the corresponding breaker must be switched off first before making the connection. `unverified` but a standard, generalizable safety practice.
- **On-site-crew tip**: add a separate outlet group on its own dedicated breaker for workers living/staying on the job site — so that turning off the lights at night doesn't also cut power to phone chargers and other devices on the same circuit. `unverified`, single-account practical tip.

## Numeric Data

- Permanent wiring: 3×1.5 mm² (lighting), 3×2.5 mm² (outlets), both grounded.
- Temporary wiring: 2×2.5 mm², ungrounded, lighting+outlets on a shared circuit.
- Bulb quantity: ≥2× number of rooms, plus reserve during renovation.
- Room-center placement tolerance: ±0.5 m.
- Mounting-pad spacing: ~60 cm.
- Cable-length cut per room: ~1.5 m.
- Minimum bulb-to-outlet clearance: 1 m.
- Light-fixture drop cable: ~20 cm. Outlet-block drop lead: ~1.5 m.
- Claimed timeline benefit of temp wiring: ~1 month faster overall renovation (unverified, no methodology given).
- Claimed speed multiplier of adhesive pads vs. dowel method: ~10x (unverified).
