## Kids Room — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Room-swap as an alternative to moving or major construction**: when
  a kids' room becomes too small for a needed furniture upgrade (here: a
  bigger bed), swapping which room the children occupy vs. the parents
  (give the kids the larger room, parents take the smaller one) can solve
  the space problem in a fraction of the time/cost of moving or a full
  renovation — this real case was completed in **3 days** (repainting,
  furniture relocation, wall decoration).
- **Furniture placement by natural light gradient**: identify a room's
  darkest corner and darkest-but-brightest zones before placing
  furniture — put something that doesn't need reading/task light there
  (a bookshelf) rather than a bed or desk; reserve the room's brightest
  spot for the desk specifically.
- **Two children's sleeping "houses" placed at opposite ends of the
  room, wardrobe as the physical divider between them**: each child gets
  a distinct sleep zone (styled as a small "house"/loft structure) at
  opposite corners, with a shared clothing wardrobe placed at the
  midpoint between them acting as a soft physical anchor/divider — frees
  a large shared central zone (rug-floored) for joint play, rather than
  hard-partitioning the whole room.
- **Warm, insulated balcony used as overflow wardrobe/storage and play
  space** for one child specifically — the balcony in this case was
  already insulated with heated flooring, making it comfortable enough
  for a child's wardrobe and occasional play, not just storage.
  Deliberately used to relocate a bold/bright color scheme (bright pink)
  away from the main shared room, keeping the main room in calmer tones.
- **Dual-height design consideration**: build genuinely kid-height
  details (low shelves, low stools, low decorative elements) that
  children can notice/reach on their own scale, while keeping a separate
  layer of adult-eye-level design detail — both age groups get something
  to appreciate in the same room rather than designing purely to one
  eye-level.
- **Keep genuinely fragile/breakable decor (figurines, sentimental
  objects) up at height, out of small children's reach**, rather than
  avoiding such items altogether — lets the room still display
  sentimental/decorative pieces without needing every low-level item to
  be child-proof.
- **Overall room color restraint, corroborating the "avoid overloading a
  small/shared room with saturated color" principle already recorded
  from `YT_Cr1vew4sA9g`**: the household kept the shared room itself in
  calm blue/gray/green/wood tones, deliberately routing the one bold
  color choice (bright pink) to the balcony rather than the main room.

## Design Concept — Durable Facts (Decorative Technique)

- **Building a 3D drywall decorative feature to hide a leftover baseboard
  notch/cutout**: after removing a dresser that a previous baseboard run
  had been cut/notched around, rather than patching the baseboard flush,
  the designer built a decorative drywall "mountain" relief feature over
  that section of wall (hand-painted by the child) — a reusable technique
  for concealing an awkward wall-baseboard artifact behind a deliberate
  decorative build-out instead of a flush repair.
- **Reusing existing familiar objects (an existing pendant/paper lamp
  shade, an existing LED string light) when relocating a room**, rather
  than buying entirely new fixtures — noted in this case as both
  budget-conscious and comfort-preserving for the children (familiar
  objects moved with them into the new room). Practical caution: a pull-
  cord light fixture is one of the first things children will physically
  test/pull on on moving into a new room — expect it to get used/handled
  immediately, not treated delicately.
