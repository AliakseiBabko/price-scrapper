## Mistakes / Warnings — Designer-Project Defects, Mechanism-Explained

- **WC/bathroom finished-floor level must match the rest of the apartment's floor exactly, no threshold step** — the designer failed to account for the thickness difference between the hallway's laminate and the bathroom's tile, producing a sharp-edged raised threshold at the WC doorway. **Injury mechanism explained**: a step this small doesn't register as an obstacle to the brain's normal walking pattern (it reads as "flat floor"), so the foot doesn't lift for it — repeated, painful toe injuries result, especially barefoot. `confirmed`, genuinely new safety mechanism for this store.
- **Electrical panel placement inside a closet must be co-designed with the closet itself, not retrofitted** — the designer apparently ordered/paid for the closet first and only afterward remembered the panel needed to go somewhere inside it, resulting in the panel partly blocked by a horizontal shelf (breakers become inaccessible without partial disassembly) and the lower breaker row reachable only through a 55cm-deep narrow gap.
- **Only standard doors with an easy-to-clean, hard-to-stain finish are appropriate for a modern renovation — never "paint-grade" doors finished with decorative (non-washable) plaster.** Mechanism: in real use, occupants grip and push/pull the door slab itself (not just the handle) to open/close it — a paint-grade decorative-plaster door develops permanent, unremovable smudge/grip stains at the push point that can't be cleaned or touched up, forcing a full redo (which itself only lasts about a month before recurring).
- **Laminate underlayment must be the manufacturer-specified product sold with that laminate, never leftover packaging offcuts** — a real defect found: installers used cut-up pieces of the laminate's own shipping cardboard as underlayment, placed near floor-to-ceiling glazing where condensation regularly accumulates — that cardboard will soak through in the first winter and then rot/mold in the enclosed space beneath the flooring, a real structural and air-quality risk.
- **Switch groups must be mounted horizontally, at exactly 90cm from the floor, so any switch can be found by feel without looking.** A vertical switch-group arrangement is flagged as a defect on two counts: no switch in the group sits at the correct blind-reach height, and — critically — a vertical group standing next to a normal horizontal single switch on the same wall makes accidental simultaneous activation of the whole group much more likely.
- **Door swing-limiter (stopper) placement: 5-10cm from the door slab's edge on the handle side, never near the hinges.** Explained mechanism: a stopper placed close to the hinge side (real defect found: 20cm from the hinge) puts it on the short end of the door's own lever arm, so the door's full swinging weight bends or tears the stopper out on the very first hard opens — leaving no functional stop at all, and the door then repeatedly strikes and damages the wall behind it.
- **Continuous flooring (no thresholds/seams between rooms) is required at Comfort-class and above — quartz-vinyl tile is the stated optimal material for this.** Mixing tile (in an entryway) with laminate elsewhere is flagged as an outdated (2000s-era) practice with three concrete downsides: tile costs more per unit and more to install than quartz-vinyl; tile grout joints darken and degrade over time; and the tile-to-laminate seam repeatedly opens as the laminate expands/contracts seasonally.
- **Bedroom lighting controls must never be positioned so reaching them from bed requires an awkward stretch/twist** — a real defect found: all bedroom lighting control was placed behind the headboard, unreachable without significant contortion while lying down.
- **Wardrobe/closet length should stop ~30cm short of its opening's full width** — this specific offset is not arbitrary: at 30cm short, once the door casing and the wardrobe itself are both installed, a light switch fits exactly centered in the remaining gap between the casing and the wardrobe edge. A wardrobe built 13cm shorter than this target (real defect found) leaves a functionless leftover strip between switch and wardrobe that wastes usable interior storage space for no aesthetic or functional benefit.
- **Finish plumbing fixtures (faucets, sinks) should avoid black finishes** — a real observed failure: black fixtures on this project, despite the renovation being fairly recent, already showed visible residue streaks, smudges, wear marks, and baked-on mineral deposits that can't be cleaned, polished, or touched up. The only real fix once this happens is full fixture replacement — and a black replacement will show the same defect again within a couple of months. `single-account`, but a specific, checkable durability caution not previously recorded in this store.
- **A hallway/entry zone should always include a pass-through (two-way) switch**, so the main light can be turned off on the way out without walking back into the "dirty" (unclean-shoes) zone. A defect found: no pass-through switch existed, forcing occupants to first test multiple switches to find the right one, walk back across the dirty floor to the front door to turn the light off, then walk back through in the dark past shoes stored at the entry — a real daily-friction failure mode.
- **Never place a washing machine on laminate flooring** — any water intrusion into the flooring's joints (лаги/locking edges) causes swelling, forcing a full re-lay. A defect found: despite already tiling the entry zone (at real cost), the designer reverted to laminate specifically in the utility/closet zone and placed a washing machine directly on it — a code violation per the source, compounded by the additional defect of omitting a leak-detection sensor for that machine, which the source frames as an inevitable-eventually-leak risk that a management company will ultimately force the owner to remediate once a downstairs neighbor is affected.
- **Entryway lighting should be limited to one main light plus, optionally, a night/duty light and a mirror/makeup-zone light only** — a defect found: the designer installed three separate main-lighting sources in a 2m² entryway (a bright LED strip, several spotlights, and a second LED strip inside a closet), each on its own switch, producing seven possible lighting combinations for a space that only ever needs one — a real, avoidable cost multiplier (fixture cost, wiring, installation, connection, all ×3) with no functional benefit.
- **Outlets should be planned to a specific, deliberate count — never scattered without a stated purpose.** A defect found: an extra outlet placed at a bedroom doorway, ostensibly "for a vacuum," while two outlets already existed at each bedside (four total near the bed) — the source frames each unexplained extra outlet as a real, compounding cost (fixture + wiring + installation + connection) with no matching use case.
- **A proper shower should have a sliding rod (not a fixed-height showerhead)** so the height can be adjusted per occupant. A defect found: a fixed showerhead mounted on one wall with the mixer/faucet on a *different* wall — the mixer positioned on the side of the tub is flagged as unacceptable on two counts: it reduces the tub's own usable interior width, and it's a real injury risk (a slip in the tub can cause direct impact against the protruding mixer hardware, bending, breaking, or tearing it off).
- **Bathroom/WC primary lighting should be a backlit stretch-ceiling panel (with side-glow), not directional spotlights.** Mechanism: a backlit ceiling gives soft, even, shadow-free light that flatters the space and properly illuminates every corner during cleaning; directional spotlights create bright hot-spots on walls (visually harsh, can shine directly in a bather's face while lying in the tub) while leaving other floor areas dim enough that dirt/dust there goes unnoticed.
- **Finish-stage electrical hardware (switches, outlets, plates) throughout an apartment should be one consistent color, brand, and collection.** A defect found: four different colors/finishes across four different zones of the same small apartment (ivory-white in the kitchen backsplash zone, dark graphite on one decorative-plaster wall, cold white at the living-room entry, light graphite at the exit) — the source's own dry framing: it looks as though whoever bought the materials grabbed whatever was in front of them at the store without any plan.
