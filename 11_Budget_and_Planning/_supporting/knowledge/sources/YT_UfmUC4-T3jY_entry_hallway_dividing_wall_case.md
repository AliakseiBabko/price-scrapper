## The Problem: Developer's L-Shaped Entry Hallway

- Base hallway width 1.56m (finishes to ~1.5m after plaster) along the main wall — too narrow for a wardrobe deeper than 40cm without the wardrobe's own end protruding into the door opening. A 40cm-deep wardrobe only fits shoes/light everyday clothing, not coats/outerwear, and barely fits luggage (which then blocks the rest of that zone from being used for hanging clothes at all).
- Deep clothing storage requires the L-shape's 60cm-deep leg — but that geometry only accommodates a **free-standing ("corpus") wardrobe**, not a built-in, which is more expensive (pays for side/back/top/bottom panels a built-in doesn't need) and, even with a rounding/corner module to soften the exposed corner, wastes roughly 30% of the wardrobe's usable length on a purely transitional, non-functional section.
- An L-shaped corner also leaves an exposed protruding corner in a high-traffic zone, and the baseboard along that run simply terminates at a cut edge/end-cap there — a cap that reliably pops off from routine contact with a mop/broom/vacuum during cleaning.

## Furniture / Built-ins — Durable Facts & Rules

- **Free-standing corner-module wardrobes waste roughly 30% of usable wardrobe length on the transitional section alone** — a concrete, quantified cost of using a rounding module to soften an exposed exterior corner, beyond the free-standing unit's baseline cost premium (paying for panels a built-in wouldn't need). `confirmed`, real project figure.
- **A short (here, 2.16m) dividing wall converting an L-shaped zone into two separate niches lets a built-in sliding-door wardrobe replace a free-standing one** — paying only for door panels + interior fitout, using full ceiling height (unlike a free-standing unit topped by a gap), with zero wasted transitional section. `confirmed`, real project, extends this store's existing built-in-vs-corpus wardrobe cost theme.
- **Framing a former open corner with a proper door opening (and door-casing/jamb-extension trim) gives the baseboard a correct termination point** instead of a bare cut edge — the baseboard's cut end now meets the door casing rather than dead-ending with a cap, producing a junction durable enough to withstand repeated contact with cleaning tools. `confirmed`, corroborates this store's existing casing-stub/baseboard-termination facts from a different context (doorless openings).

## Ceilings — Durable Facts & Rules

- **A stretch ceiling over an L-shaped (non-rectangular) room section is materially harder and more expensive than two independent rectangular sections.** A seamless single piece over an L-shape requires buying oversized material sufficient to cover the full bounding rectangle and discarding the unused offcut — which usually can't be reliably reused elsewhere, since stretch-ceiling fabric/film is cut and fitted on-site (an installer working from a stepladder cutting a precise "L" shape out of a large sheet is unlikely to preserve the offcut undamaged/unmarked). Alternatively, an L-shaped area can be split into two pieces, but any seam location will be long, visible, and comparatively short-lived (longer seams fail faster). **Splitting the L into two independent rectangular sections via a dividing wall avoids both problems** — real cited cost delta: **more than 10,000, sometimes more than 20,000 RUB** difference (labor + materials) between two simple rectangular stretch-ceiling sections versus one complex L-shaped installation. `confirmed`, concrete cost figure, genuinely new content for this store's ceiling coverage.
- **Fabric vs. film stretch-ceiling material choice compounds the L-shape waste problem**: fabric ceilings (matte, doesn't crack/yellow/fade, generally preferred in living spaces) and film ceilings (glossy, generally preferred in wet rooms for compatibility with reflective bathroom surfaces) are different materials — a leftover offcut from one room's material can't be repurposed for a different room that uses the other material, even if the offcut is otherwise usable. `confirmed`, practical material-planning detail.
- **A dividing wall creating a boxed transition also lets the wardrobe's own top rail attach directly to a purpose-built box**, giving the visual effect of the ceiling meeting the wardrobe with no gap, while keeping the two systems independently serviceable — the wardrobe (or its rail) can be removed/replaced without touching the ceiling, and the stretch ceiling can be dropped to access wiring without touching the wardrobe. `confirmed`, real project detail.

## Flooring — Durable Facts & Rules

- **An entry-zone floor covering wears out faster than the rest of an apartment even when water-resistant**, because it's a "dirty zone" (tracked-in grit, moisture) — so being able to replace flooring in just that L-shaped zone independently, without needing to also replace flooring further into the apartment, has real value. Without a dividing wall, any transition/seam in an L-shaped zone has to run diagonally or awkwardly across the corner, producing a seam that is long, visually prominent, and — because longer seams fail faster — comparatively short-lived. `confirmed`.
- **A dividing wall with a proper door opening lets a flooring transition terminate at a short (here, 80cm) transition molding anchored to the door frame, not the baseboard** — a texture/seam this short holds firmly and reads as intentional (terminating cleanly at the door frame) rather than as an arbitrary mid-room cut. **General rule implied: the shorter a flooring transition seam, the better it holds and the better it looks — route transitions through a door opening rather than across open floor whenever possible.** `confirmed`, real project detail with a specific length figure.
- **Solid/engineered wood flooring (массив) wears unevenly depending on the direction of foot traffic relative to the grain** — it holds up well when the boards run parallel to the main direction of movement, and wears out unusually fast when laid across that direction. In an L-shaped corridor, this means the two legs of the L often need flooring run in genuinely different directions to each match their own traffic pattern — which, without a dividing wall, forces an awkward, long diagonal seam at the corner where the two directions meet. A dividing wall with a door opening lets each leg have its own flooring direction, meeting cleanly at the opening's center rather than at an arbitrary diagonal. `confirmed`, genuinely new material-durability mechanism for this store.
