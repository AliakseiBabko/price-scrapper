## Plumbing

- **Radiator-to-clapan connection materials/parts, itemized**: matched-color installation bushings (4 per radiator, "футорки" — with correct left/right-hand thread direction matched per side), a Maevsky air-bleed valve ("кран Маевского") + bleed key, a blanking plug, installation tubes, and Danfoss thermostatic-regulating + shutoff valves (each supplied in two threaded parts). `confirmed`.
- **Danfoss valve sealing mechanism, confirmed from a second angle**: no gaskets/O-rings — sealing achieved via a machined brass cone; the source explicitly states this connection type is "more reliable, but you need to know how to work with it," consistent with and extending the durability claim already recorded from episode #16. `confirmed`, `single-account`.
- **Thread-sealing method choice, an explicit alternative comparison**: threaded fittings can be sealed with flax fiber ("лён") + sealing paste, or with anaerobic sealant. This job used the traditional flax+paste method by the practitioner's own preference (he states he trusts flax more, and specifically asked the hired plumber to use it despite the plumber initially proposing anaerobic sealant). `confirmed`, stated preference not a technical claim of one method being objectively superior.
- **Fastener torque discipline, general plumbing heuristic**: threaded fittings with internal rubber seals (both the Maevsky valve and the blanking plug) should be tightened only lightly by hand plus a small wrench nudge — "better to under-tighten slightly and add a touch more than to over-tighten." `confirmed`, technique/heuristic.
- **Sequencing rule, stated as a hard rule with reasoning**: mount the radiator on its brackets first, *then* fit/cut the connecting supply tubes — never the reverse. Reasoning given: if the supply tubing is fitted first, the installer will never manage to get it to sit perfectly perpendicular to the wall once the radiator is finally hung; mounting the radiator first lets the tubing be adjusted to it. `confirmed`, technique/heuristic, directly generalizable to any radiator installation.
- **⚠️ Quantified rebuttal to a viewer's bracket-safety concern (direct evidence for episode #16's bracket-load decision)**: a commenter argued a radiator needs 4 brackets; the practitioner cites the manufacturer's own instruction (radiators up to 12 sections need only 2 brackets) and states his own 3-bracket choice (documented in #16) gives 3 × 70kg = 210kg of rated capacity against a radiator that weighs only ~20–25kg — a **roughly 10x safety margin**. `confirmed`, arithmetic-checkable, corroborates and quantifies the #16 bracket decision.
- **⚠️ Quantified rebuttal to a viewer's hot-glue-safety concern (direct evidence for episode #16's fastening technique)**: a commenter argued the hot-melt/thermal glue ("термоклей") used to bed wall anchors in #16 would melt from radiator heat and let the radiator fall. Practitioner's counter-argument with figures: hot-melt glue's melting point is 130–150°C; the radiator's own surface temperature runs only ~70–75°C; the radiator hangs 30mm off the wall and the anchor itself is set 8cm into the wall, so the wall material at the anchor depth is thermally isolated from the radiator surface by both distance and mass — reaching anywhere near 100°C at that depth (his stated minimum threshold for any real risk to the glue) is "impossible" under these conditions. He adds a caveat: this was one demonstrated method he considers reliable, but for anyone unsure, a chemical anchor ("химанкер") or mounting cement is a reasonable alternative — and he states he personally wouldn't rely on the fastener alone into an aerated-concrete block ("пеноблок") without adding some kind of adhesive/anchor/cement regardless of method. `confirmed`, arithmetic/physical reasoning given, `single-account` (his own calculation, not independently lab-verified).
- **PEX press-fitting tooling and workflow, in detail**: tooling options are a manual pipe expander + manual press, or a battery-powered ("аккумуляторный") expander + press; manual tools are stated as what most plumbers still actually use today (battery tools are becoming more common but aren't yet standard), and battery tools are typically rented from the pipe manufacturer's (KAN's) own retail/representative outlets rather than purchased, for a plumber doing occasional work on this system. Expander heads come in 6-petal (widespread on newer systems, expands via three 30°-rotation steps) or 9-petal (expands fully in one motion) variants. `confirmed`, tooling reference detail.
- **PEX press-fitting process, step by step**: fit the crimp ring onto the pipe with its coned face forward/toward the fitting (blunt face backward); expand the pipe end in three steps, each rotated 30° further (6-petal expander); insert the fitting only partway (leaving roughly one ring-width of pipe exposed, which the crimp ring will press flush against the fitting once compressed); select the press jaw matching the pipe diameter; press. The practitioner states he learned to press fittings competently in about 10 minutes, and explicitly compares this favorably to polypropylene pipe welding, which he says has "far more nuances" to get right. `confirmed`, technique detail, `single-account` comparative claim (`unverified`, no controlled test shown).
- **Pipe-rotation-near-a-fitting rule**: a PEX pipe can be freely rotated up to 5 pipe-diameters' distance from a fitting without inducing stress on the joint; closer to the fitting, a pipe clip/fixator should be used instead of hand-torquing the pipe directly, to avoid inducing joint stress. `confirmed`, technique/heuristic, generalizable.
- **Screed-embedded pipe run constraint, specific to this job's noise-insulation floor buildup**: pipes could not be fixed/clipped directly to the floor at this stage because doing so would puncture the sound-insulation layer beneath — instead, pipes (with new protective corrugated sleeving fitted over the old sleeving where reused) were weighted down with adhesive-filled bags until the reinforced cement-sand screed was poured over them; no restrictions apply to the pipe/screed pour combination itself. `confirmed`, technique detail directly relevant to a project also planning floor sound-insulation + screed.
- **Convector connection correction**: the developer's original connection to the floor convector had a bend that put the fitting under mechanical stress; the practitioner extended and re-routed the pipe with proper fixators to remove that stress — a direct real-world example of the "never leave a fitting under tension" rule already recorded from episode #16. `confirmed`, `corroborates_existing` (internal, same-source-series confirmation).
- **Pressure-testing (опрессовка) procedure, full sequence with figures — genuinely new, checkable technique**: (1) open the apartment's inlet valves in the corridor distribution cabinet and fill the system; (2) bleed air from every radiator via its Maevsky valve until water flow is steady; (3) system's own working/operating pressure in this building measured at 4 bar — first visual leak check performed at this working pressure on every threaded and flax-sealed joint (re-tighten a leaking threaded joint; redo a leaking flax-sealed joint entirely, not just re-tighten); (4) **test pressure = working pressure × ~2** in this case (not exactly the "standard" 1.5x, because the installed KAN pipe is rated to 10 bar and the radiators to more — so he pumps to 8 bar, roughly double the 4 bar working pressure, rather than only 1.5x); (5) before pressurizing, the corridor inlet valves must be closed, or the whole building stairwell riser gets pressurized instead of just this apartment's branch; (6) re-check all joints again at test pressure, fix any leak found, and re-pressurize to the target after any fix; (7) hold the system at test pressure for **1 hour**; a pressure drop of up to **0.2 bar** over that hour is within acceptable tolerance — a larger drop indicates a real leak. `confirmed`, checkable numeric procedure.
- **Hot-water-cooling caveat on pressure testing, a genuinely useful gotcha**: if hot water is used to fill/pressurize the system, pressure will drop further than normal as the water cools during the hold period, independent of any leak — the practitioner's recommendation is to fill and let the water cool to ambient *before* starting the timed pressure-test hold, to avoid a false-positive leak reading. `confirmed`, technique/heuristic.
- **Finishing-stage protection detail**: named PVC masking tape brand "Blue Dolphin" used to mask all exposed valves/tubing before plastering over the wall chases, specifically because it can stay on for an extended period without leaving adhesive residue; radiators are switched off during the plastering step so the fresh plaster isn't heated while curing. Valve bodies themselves are later spray-painted to match the radiator color during the painting stage. `confirmed`, minor technique detail.

## Mistakes / Warnings

- **Plastic wrench-adapter/insert accessory issue**: a plastic bushing-gripping accessory sold with the installation-bushing kit didn't grip reliably on a slightly rounded bushing surface, causing the wrench to slip; workaround was wrapping the wrench jaws in two layers of electrical tape for grip, or simply not using the plastic insert at all and gripping the bushing directly (acceptable for a chrome towel-warmer-style fitting, per the practitioner, though a bare wrench does leave minor surface scratching). `confirmed`, minor practical workaround.
- **Timing rule for trimming excess flax/sealant**: don't trim the excess flax+paste squeezing out of a threaded joint immediately after assembly — wait until the full system has been assembled and inspected, since trimming early risks having to redo (re-pack/re-seal) that exact joint if a leak shows up later at that connection, doubling the work. `confirmed`, technique/heuristic.
