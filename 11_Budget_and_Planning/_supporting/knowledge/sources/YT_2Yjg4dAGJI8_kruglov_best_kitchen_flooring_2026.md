## Genuinely New Content

- **Established hardness ordering across all materials compared (softest to hardest underfoot)**: LVT quartz-vinyl (softest) → SPC quartz-vinyl → MSPC composite → laminate (hardest of the non-tile group) → porcelain tile (hardest overall). This ordering underlies the rest of the ranking's breakage-risk and comfort judgments.
- **⚠️ Quartz-parquet's real-world verdict, ranked worst (8th) despite premium marketing**: the thin natural-wood veneer top layer scratches quickly in practice regardless of the manufacturer's claimed protective lacquer — the "wow effect" of a natural-wood surface fades fast, especially in a kitchen's harsher daily-use environment. **New concern not previously flagged for this material**: the SPC base has minimal linear-expansion, while the glued-on natural-wood veneer has a meaningfully higher expansion coefficient — how these two bonded layers behave together long-term is explicitly called an unknown, since the material is too recently introduced to have a track record (echoing this round's own video 3 caution about the same material family from a different Kruglov video).
- **Engineered board (#7) and parquet board (#6) kitchen-specific verdicts**: most comfortable barefoot of all materials compared, but scratches/dents extremely easily from dropped objects, and is **the single worst material for staining** — spilled wine, borscht, or pomegranate juice absorb into it readily if not wiped up within minutes. No locking mechanism (only tongue-and-groove/"шпунт" glue-down joints), meaning extra labor/adhesive cost, but **being glue-down-only lets heavy kitchen cabinetry sit on it safely** (compresses slightly under weight, no real damage) — unlike a floating installation. **The specific reason engineered board ranks below parquet board despite near-identical properties**: engineered boards are typically larger-format, making any warping/deformation more visually obvious than on parquet board's smaller module size.
- **⚠️ Parquet board's floating-installation furniture rule, a manufacturer prohibition explicitly spelled out**: parquet board can be installed either glued or floating (on underlay); **when installed floating, manufacturers explicitly prohibit placing heavy furniture/cabinetry on it** — insufficient expansion clearance under a fixed heavy load can cause "tenting," seam separation, or lock breakage as the floor tries to expand.
- **Porcelain tile ranked surprisingly low (5th/worst-category) despite being the 2nd-most popular kitchen flooring choice by usage**: cold underfoot (absent underfloor heating, deliberately excluded from this comparison's scope); highest hardness of any material compared → most dish/glass breakage risk and the most dangerous surface to fall on; genuinely uncomfortable for long standing, particularly for people with flat feet/plantar issues; slip risk (anti-slip product lines exist but most porcelain tile lacks this feature — a real buyer-attention point). **A subjective but explicitly stated criterion**: porcelain tile is described as failing to convey coziness/warmth/a "home" feeling — framed as a real tension with the modern trend of combining kitchen and living-room into one open space, since a continuous tile floor across both zones reads as cold/commercial, while zoning the tile to just the kitchen creates a transition seam that itself looks dated.
- **⚠️ MSPC composite flooring, first mention of this specific material for this store**: mineral-stone composite base plus iron-ore additive, topped with a melamine-paper wear layer (the same wear-layer family as laminate, not the polyurethane layer typical of LVT/SPC quartz-vinyl) — explains why MSPC is the most scratch-resistant of the entire quartz-vinyl-adjacent family, though its rigid base means it can chip at an impact point (repairable with ordinary wood filler/putty). 100% waterproof like the rest of the quartz-vinyl family. **⚠️ Named-brand manufacturer exception, a genuinely unusual claim flagged as unique**: the "STN" brand explicitly maintains its warranty even with heavy furniture installed on a floating-installed MSPC floor — stated as the only case among all floating-floor products/manufacturers surveyed in this video where a floating installation's warranty survives heavy-furniture loading. **Visual caveat**: MSPC's edge chamfer/bevel is noticeably smaller than a standard laminate or SPC bevel — a manufacturing limitation, visible at full-room scale, treated as a minor real defect/characteristic rather than a dealbreaker.
- **⚠️ Universal quartz-vinyl-family deformation-gap rule, applying across LVT/SPC/MSPC alike**: despite "insignificant" linear expansion, every quartz-vinyl variant still requires a manufacturer-specified expansion gap to the wall. **The technically-correct-but-rarely-followed installation sequencing spelled out explicitly**: install kitchen cabinetry first, then run the flooring only up to the cabinet legs/toe-kick, concealing the required expansion gap there — in practice, almost all installers instead lay the floor continuously at one level and ignore this, a real, named gap between manufacturer spec and field practice.
- **LVT quartz-vinyl composition and profile (10-20% stone chip + polymer binder, 75-90% PVC + additives)**: the softest/most comfortable underfoot of all materials compared (least likely to break a dropped dish/cup), 100% waterproof, needs a genuinely perfectly flat substrate (an uneven base can chip the locking mechanism). **Scratch-vs-chip tradeoff, a new distinction for this store**: LVT is the *least* scratch-resistant of the quartz-vinyl family (its polyurethane protective layer scratches more easily than a melamine-paper wear layer), but has essentially no chip risk since it's a soft PVC-based product — the inverse tradeoff from MSPC above.
- **SPC quartz-vinyl composition (≈70% mineral base, ≈30% PVC+additives)**: harder underfoot than LVT but softer than MSPC; 100% waterproof; chip risk more likely than LVT but less than MSPC; scratches more easily than laminate but less easily than any real-wood-veneer product (quartz-parquet, engineered board, parquet board).
- **Cross-material visual-realism claim**: modern SPC/LVT quartz-vinyl has advanced enough in texture/color reproduction that even a flooring specialist can rarely distinguish it visually from genuine wood-veneer parquet or engineered board side by side — tactile inspection can usually reveal the difference to a professional, but an ordinary homeowner likely can't tell even by touch.
- **⚠️ Water-resistant laminate's specific rated water-contact window, first numeric figure for this store**: manufacturer-claimed continuous water-contact tolerance of **24-72 hours** for genuinely water-resistant (not merely moisture-resistant) laminate — a real numeric spec distinct from this round's own earlier general laminate-improvement narrative.
- **⚠️ Laminate's de jure vs. de facto conflict, explicitly named as such**: laminate is a floating-installed product, and manufacturers uniformly prohibit heavy kitchen-cabinetry loading on it (voids warranty if it later swells/squeaks/separates) — but **real-world field practice shows laminate installed under kitchen units performs fine despite the technical violation**, which the presenter states is the specific reason he ranks laminate his overall #1 "de facto" pick despite it being technically disqualified "de jure" by the same rule every quartz-vinyl variant is held to above.
- **Laminate scratch/chip verdict**: laminate is almost impossible to scratch compared to any quartz-vinyl variant (though not literally scratch-proof with real effort), but more scratch-prone than porcelain tile specifically — placing it correctly between the two material groups on this axis.
- **⚠️ Presenter's own explicit personal top-pick recommendation, distinct from the "de facto winner"**: despite laminate's practical #1 ranking above, the presenter states MSPC composite is, by his own criteria-based assessment, the objectively most optimal kitchen-flooring choice — flagged by him as a currently underappreciated/not-yet-recognized-by-clients material specifically because it's new to the market, not because of any real performance shortfall found in this comparison.
