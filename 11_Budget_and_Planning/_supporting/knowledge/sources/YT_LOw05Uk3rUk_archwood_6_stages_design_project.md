## Planning Rules / Design Process — Durable Facts & Rules

- **⚠️ 6-stage design-project structure**: (1) Preparation — client
  questionnaire (zones, furniture/appliance needs, family habits,
  engineering requirements like soundproofing/ventilation) + site
  measurement + documents (technical passport) + reference photos/video;
  (2) Layout — 3-4 variants developed with walls, furniture, and
  plumbing fixtures placed at real dimensions from the start (not
  approximated); (3) Design concept — mood-board research, then ONE
  "exploratory sketch" ("поисковый эскиз") developed in a single
  representative room before extending to the rest; (4) Budget — cost
  calculated mid-process against the client's actual budget, not
  deferred to the end; (5) Technical drawings — measured plan, updated
  furniture-placement plan, demolition plan, construction/build plan,
  electrical plans, floor/ceiling plans, wall elevations, custom
  furniture drawings; (6) Smeta — final material quantities and
  procurement.
- **⚠️ 70% of dimension-related renovation errors trace back to the
  measurement stage itself, stated claim**: don't economize on
  measurement, especially for irregular/old-fund apartments — a 3D
  scanner is recommended over manual point measurement when budget
  allows. **Worked example**: a seemingly fine, already-renovated
  secondary-market apartment (client wasn't planning to touch the main
  walls) turned out, once scanned, to have a **10cm wall deviation**
  invisible to the naked eye.
- **Old-fund/curved-wall apartments may require partial demolition just
  to properly measure the original structure** underneath old finishes
  (thick old plaster, wood floors on joists) before a real plan can be
  drawn.
- **⚠️ Revision-payment policy, explicit rule**: charge extra for
  revisions only when the *client* changes a prior decision (reverses an
  already-approved layout or concept) — never for the studio's own
  "not quite right yet" iterations. Stated rationale: "a design project
  is built like a house of cards" — changing an already-approved
  lower-tier element invalidates dependent work built on top of it, and
  that risk belongs to the studio, not the client, until the client
  themselves reverses something already signed off.
- **⚠️ Single-strong-concept-over-multiple-options critique**: argues
  against a studio marketing "2-3 full design variants" as a selling
  point — better to invest the same total effort into deeply developing
  *one* strong direction, with the architect actively narrowing and
  recommending rather than just presenting options ("you can't eat two
  breakfasts"). Genuinely branch into a second direction only if the
  first is fully rejected, not preemptively.
- **Sequential concept-refinement technique**: develop the exploratory
  concept sketch in exactly one representative room first — lock
  palette/style/mood there, confirm with the client, *then* extend to
  every remaining room — avoids redrawing an entire apartment's worth of
  concept work after an early-stage pivot.
- **Three-tier design-presentation format comparison, with a stated
  choice**: flat 2D moodboard/collage (found insufficiently informative
  — a client can't envision a full room from it, so this studio dropped
  it) vs. **3D collage** (this studio's chosen middle-tier default —
  faster/cheaper than full photorealistic rendering, "naglyadny" enough
  to commit to) vs. full photorealistic visualization (used as
  additional depth on top, not the primary presentation format, and
  flagged as expensive/slow if attempted as the default — needs either
  every team designer to be an expert renderer or a dedicated
  visualization specialist).
- **⚠️ Whole-project budget should include furniture from the start, not
  just construction/labor**: a common client mistake is budgeting only
  the renovation work and treating furniture as a later, separate
  expense — furniture frequently costs *more* than the construction
  itself. Recommends estimating the full budget (down to a bath mat)
  together from day one, even if actual furniture purchasing happens
  later.
- **Drawing-set consolidation technique**: put related information on
  as few sheets as practical (e.g. all electrical content on 1-3 sheets)
  rather than one item per sheet — a highly fragmented drawing set looks
  impressive to a client but confuses on-site workers, who (absent a
  dedicated site supervisor) tend to work sequentially sheet-by-sheet
  and can miss coordination requirements if related info sits on a
  different, unconsulted sheet (worked example: a flush-mounted material
  requiring a plaster-thickness step that only appears on a separate
  sheet gets missed, and the wall gets plastered flat first, requiring
  rework).
- **⚠️ Electrical-plan-freeze sequencing rationale**: outlet/lighting
  positions decided at the electrical-plan stage feed directly into
  several later drawings (ceiling niches for fixtures, wall elevations
  duplicating outlet positions) — the electrical plan should be
  finalized and not keep changing after this stage, or every dependent
  downstream drawing needs rework.
- **⚠️ Four-category lighting-scenario taxonomy, with a new technical
  sub-type**: (1) bright fill/task light; (2) dimmed accent/mood light
  (table lamps, floor lamps, bedside); (3) architectural/decorative
  backlighting (stair, niche, or curtain LED-profile lighting, night
  lighting); (4) **"technical" light — motion-sensor-triggered automatic
  interior cabinet/closet lighting** that switches on when a door/hatch
  opens and auto-shuts after. Broadly corroborates this vault's existing
  "minimum three lighting scenarios" rule
  (`12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Lighting_Design.md`) from an
  independent source, and adds the motion-sensor cabinet-lighting
  category not previously on that page.
- **Tile-layout verification technique**: check the real-scale tile
  layout (format + starting point + laying direction) against actual
  room dimensions *before* finalizing the floor/wall plan, specifically
  to avoid awkward/unattractive cut fragments at room edges — may
  require shifting a fixture (e.g. a toilet) slightly to get a cleaner
  layout. For matching tile format between floor and wall, verify grout
  lines actually align between the two surfaces.
- **Wall-elevation ("развертка") duplication rationale**: outlet/switch
  positions are deliberately duplicated onto wall elevations (in
  addition to the dedicated electrical plan) because the electrical plan
  becomes visually crowded — elevations show grouping/height more
  clearly for on-site workers. Bathroom wall elevations are called the
  single most information-dense drawing type (combined plumbing-fixture
  positions + tile layout).
- **⚠️ Two distinct supervision types, with a stated non-substitution
  position**: "технический надзор" (technical supervision — a
  third-party construction-QC firm checking finish quality) vs.
  "авторский надзор" (author's/design supervision — the original
  architect/designer periodically checking that built work matches the
  drawings dimensionally/positionally). Explicit stance: supervision
  should never substitute for a complete, unambiguous project — "figure
  it out on-site as we go" only works for a very small-volume,
  extremely experienced architect; the real standard should be a
  project detailed enough that any competent crew capable of reading
  drawings can execute it correctly without improvisation, especially
  important for a remote/out-of-region project.
- **⚠️ Tile-quantity smeta caution**: counting tile quantity as equal to
  the room's floor/wall area is "fundamentally wrong," especially for
  large-format tile — real cutting waste means the actual quantity
  needed is meaningfully more than the raw area. A full project's smeta
  is checked ~10-15 times across different stages by the team (5-6
  people work a full project), separate from client/contractor
  discussion rounds.
- **Budget-management philosophy, explicit preference stated**: contrasts
  "draw everything, then tally whatever it costs" against "set the total
  budget first, then manage/trade off within it during design" — this
  practitioner states an explicit preference for the budget-first
  approach, framing "renovation as an investment you can deliberately
  scale up or down" rather than a runaway process where money
  unexpectedly runs out mid-project.
