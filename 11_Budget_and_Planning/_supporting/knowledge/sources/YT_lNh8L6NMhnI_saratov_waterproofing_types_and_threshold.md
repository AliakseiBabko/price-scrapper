## Bathroom / WC — Waterproofing Product Taxonomy

- **Four cement/mastic waterproofing product classes, each with a narrow, non-overlapping intended use** (`confirmed`, general technique, brand-agnostic once named products are stripped out):
  1. **Brush-on mastic (мастика)** — the speaker's stated best-in-class choice for any residential wet room (bathroom, WC, balcony/loggia, terrace with a roof over it): easiest application, no separate primer/curing-layer sequencing needed, no dedicated tape required in a monolithic building with no relative wall movement. **Not** rated for constant water immersion (pools) — under sustained water contact it absorbs water, its physical/mechanical properties degrade, it softens, and tile can eventually detach from it; must always be tile-covered and never left exposed to direct sunlight (UV degrades it, evidenced by product samples aged 5-7 years still flexible when kept out of sun vs. degraded/cracked ones left exposed).
  2. **Rigid cement-based waterproofing** — narrow global application: potable-water reservoirs, water-supply/drainage channels. Explicitly **not suited to any application with dynamic loading or substrate movement** — a fully rigid, densely-packed sand/cement matrix ("матрешка" packing of coarse/medium/fine sand fractions around cement, minimizing pore space for low permeability) cracks under repeated back-and-forth hydrostatic pressure shifts, e.g. a pool being filled/drained repeatedly.
  3. **Elastic cement-based (two-component, cement + polymer)** — the recommended type for pools specifically, since it tolerates minor substrate/wall flex under hydrostatic pressure without cracking. Also usable for potable-water contact (a slight polymer smell/taste may persist temporarily if in direct large-area water contact, resolves under tile).
  4. **Two-component (liquid + powder) elastic cement-polymer mastic** — a fast-cure product line (named: superflex D2 in this source) mixed 1:1, tileable after only 4 hours — offered as the fast-turnaround option when a bathroom/pool needs to be finished quickly.
- **Rigid cement-based product composition mechanism, stated explicitly**: sand graded into multiple particle-size fractions (coarse/medium/fine) packed around cement to minimize pore space — denser packing directly produces lower permeability/better waterproofing, but this requires applying it in multiple thin passes (≤1mm per pass, 2-4 passes) rather than one thick coat.
- **Polymer-modified cement product mechanism**: roughly a quarter of the dry-mix bag by weight is polymer powder/binder — different polymer sub-types tolerate water exposure differently (fully water-resistant vs. partially-resoluble vs. fully water-soluble); the water-resistant polymer type is what gives the finished coating its flexible, slightly plastic-film-like membrane behavior layered on top of the rigid cement matrix's own structural hardness.

## Bathroom / WC — Threshold Height and Cross-Room Floor-Level Coordination

- **⚠️ Real personal case: lowering the bathroom floor ~1-2cm below the adjoining corridor floor as a deliberate alternative to a raised threshold** — the speaker's own bathroom has its tile finished roughly level with (very slightly below) the corridor tile, with no raised threshold at all; describes never once tripping on the resulting shallow dip in years of daily use, including with a young child jumping off a washing machine into the bathtub and splashing several liters of water onto the floor with no overflow into the corridor. This is a direct, concrete worked example of the exact floor-level-coordination decision this gap targets — a genuine alternative to both "full raised threshold" and "fully flush, zero-step" framings already on the page. `single-account`, but a real lived-in case with a specific mechanism (slight negative slope/dip functions as a shallow containment basin without a visible step).
- **⚠️ Historical Soviet-era threshold height rationale, worked backward from a real washbasin/bathtub volume**: the speaker calculates that a standard Soviet bathroom's ~5cm threshold height was sized so that a 60-liter galvanized washtub (used historically for laundry, including children's clothing, in communal apartments) could fully tip over/spill without the resulting water overflowing the threshold into the corridor — a real applied volume-vs-threshold-height design rationale, not an arbitrary figure. Separately, a full ~230-liter bathtub overflow scenario is cited as the other real-world event a threshold is meant to contain.
- **Current Russian code status (SP 73, cited by number) on threshold height, per this source**: the code requires that a threshold exist in any wet room with water-supply risers passing through it, and requires waterproofing to run up the walls with a fold-up ("подворот"), but **does not currently specify a numeric threshold height or fold-up height** — left to the installer's judgment. The speaker's own practical recommendation, independent of any code minimum: **1-1.5-2cm is enough to avoid a trip hazard while still containing an accidental spill**, distinct from the older ~5cm Soviet convention. This adds a second, independently-sourced code citation (SP 73) to this page's existing Kruglov/Ontario account of Russia's shifting flush-floor regulatory history, though this source does not address the flush-floor legality question directly — only the threshold-height number itself.
- **A real historical rough-stage defect mechanism, corroborating this page's existing rolled-membrane content from a different angle**: describes crews in older Soviet-era construction laying rolled roofing-felt/bitumen ("рубероид") waterproofing on bathroom floors and walls, then finding that plaster applied directly over the bitumen-coated wall section won't hold (falls off within minutes) because cement doesn't bond to bitumen — the field fix used was to leave a 20cm-high strip of galvanized wire mesh ("сетка рабица") hanging free like an apron at the top of the waterproofing's wall fold-up, giving the plaster something mechanical to key into above the bitumen zone rather than relying on direct bond. Presented as a real, once-common field workaround, not a recommended modern technique — modern mastic-based waterproofing doesn't have this bonding problem in the same way.

## Bathroom / WC — Application/QC Technique (Brand-Specific Product, Lower Confidence for This Project)

- **Deformation-joint tape only needed where relative wall movement exists** — a monolithic-concrete building with monolithic walls has no such movement at a wall-to-floor or wall-to-wall junction, so the speaker skips tape there and relies on mastic thickness alone; tape becomes necessary specifically in panel/block construction or anywhere two surfaces can move independently, since it lets the mastic layer absorb up to ~12mm of joint movement without tearing (tape itself doesn't stretch across its width, but the surrounding uncured mastic does).
- **Mastic elongation is a function of applied thickness, not a fixed percentage independent of thickness** — a quoted "270% elongation" spec means a given thickness of cured material can stretch to ~2.7x its own length before tearing, so a thin coat has much less absolute stretch capacity in millimeters than a thick coat even though the percentage figure is the same; a corner or joint area needs adequate thickness specifically because it experiences concentrated stress. Corroborates and adds mechanism detail to this page's existing thin-layer-thickness-control content (Sidorik's 0.8mm thin-layer QC note) — from the opposite direction (why enough thickness matters at stress points, vs. why not-too-much thickness matters for tile-leveling).
- **Two-tone product color is presented here as purely cosmetic/marketing, not a designed QC feature** — contrasts directly with the two other sources already on this page (Sidorik 2017, Weber Tech 822; Сергей ByPas, Ceresit CL 51) that frame a pink-then-gray two-coat color change as a deliberate self-verifying coverage check. This source's speaker states the color choice was originally just packaging/branding differentiation with no functional coverage-QC intent, though acknowledges a coverage gap is still visually obvious either way. Recorded as a **direct source disagreement on intent** (not mechanism) — worth flagging on the page as differing manufacturer/practitioner framing of the same visual signal.
- **Coverage/thickness numeric targets**: recommended finished (dried) thickness 0.7-1mm across two coats (~0.6-0.7mm wet each, drying to roughly 70% of applied thickness); rated coverage ~1200-1500g/m² (an 8kg bucket covers roughly 6-6.5m²). A roller-nap visual thickness check is offered as a low-tech field QC method: rolling out the wet coat should leave visible roller-nap texture lines with a ~4-5mm gap between ridges at correct thickness — a thinner, glassier-looking coat with ~1mm gaps between texture lines indicates under-application.
- **Product shelf-life/degradation field test**: fold or squeeze a display sample that's been sitting for a couple of months — visible cracking or a white bloom/rim forming indicates the product's flexibility is degrading with age even unused; the speaker's own multi-year-old cured samples remain flexible (tie into a loop, no cracking) as a durability claim for their specific product line.
