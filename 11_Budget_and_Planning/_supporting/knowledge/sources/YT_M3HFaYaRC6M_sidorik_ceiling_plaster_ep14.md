## Walls / Ceilings — Thick-Layer Ceiling Plaster Without Full Metal Mesh

- **Why plaster the ceiling instead of drywall/stretch ceiling, stated
  reasoning**: a plastered ceiling doesn't consume height — directly
  relevant in Belarus, where monolithic-frame buildings with only 255cm
  ceiling height are common; dropping the ceiling even a minimum ~7cm for
  drywall/stretch plus floor screed/flooring build-up leaves ≤245cm.
  Plastered ceilings are also cited as flatter than drywall or stretch
  ceiling, and cheaper than a drywall ceiling.
- **Code/manufacturer baseline and the problem it creates**: both
  regulatory guidance and most plaster manufacturers cap gypsum plaster
  ceiling application at 15mm without extra reinforcement; beyond that,
  code calls for shot/screwed galvanized metal mesh across the whole
  ceiling before plastering — a labor-intensive full-mesh install this
  practitioner wanted to avoid for a real 3cm-layer job (real observed
  ceiling deviation in this apartment: 0-20mm, growing to a max 27mm at
  points needing extra buildup for embedded lighting-circuit conduit).
- **Underlying mechanism reasoning, stated directly**: gypsum plaster
  doesn't form a chemical/crystalline bond with bare concrete (unlike some
  other binder systems) — it holds on *mechanical* surface roughness
  alone. A thick unsupported layer therefore needs artificially created
  roughness/anchor points, not just a stronger primer.
- **Devised alternative to full mesh — three-part system**: (1) strips cut
  from galvanized suspension-hanger stock, shot to the ceiling with a
  powder-actuated (gas) fastening tool (~10 strips/m²), then bent outward
  into "butterfly" anchors the plaster physically keys onto; (2) a latex
  bonding additive (named: Ceresit CT81) mixed with cement, sand, and
  water per the product's own ratio table into a thick ("like heavy sour
  cream") slurry, applied with a masonry brush (tested against a texture
  sprayer and a trowel/spatula first — both rejected as producing too
  little surface texture) to build a rough "fur coat" bonding layer —
  cured overnight into a bond described as noticeably stronger under
  scraping than ordinary tile adhesive; (3) polypropylene fiber mixed
  directly into the gypsum plaster itself (30g fiber per 30kg plaster
  bag) to bind the plaster mass together.
- **Named fastener buying tip**: powder-actuated nails from DLT.by —
  bullet-shaped tip, notched shank, better steel than standard nails,
  same price as ordinary nails, available in multiple sizes including
  16mm — recommended for anyone using a similar gas-fastening tool.
  Shooting fasteners is cited as cheaper and less labor than screwing
  dowel-nails for the same anchor count (qualitative, no figure given).
- **Ceiling-specific beacon layout technique**: chalk/snap line used to
  mark beacon lines on the ceiling; beacons spaced ~1m apart; a laser
  level mounted on an extendable pole projects the reference plane near
  the ceiling, with high/low points found by holding a tape measure to
  the ceiling and reading the gap to the beam. **Beacon-setting direction
  is inverted vs. a floor/wall beacon**: a partially-driven, height-
  adjustable dowel-nail anchor establishes a reference point via a
  carpenter's square held against the leveled laser mark, then each
  ceiling beacon is pushed *up* to that mark (a floor/wall beacon is
  instead pressed *down* to its mark).
- **Perimeter damper-tape experiment, a genuine first-time trial**: a
  drywall-profile foam sealing tape applied around the ceiling perimeter
  as an expansion/isolation buffer, borrowing the screed-perimeter-damper
  concept — speaker states he'd never tried isolating plaster from walls
  this way before and had mixed opinions about it from others, decided to
  test it himself. **Real downside found**: the tape's own adhesive
  leaves residue on the wall that needs extra sanding during corner trim.
  **Planned fix for future projects**: switch to the thinner (3mm),
  purpose-made screed-perimeter damper tape, stapled rather than glued.
  **Net verdict**: preferred the result with tape over without — formed a
  cleaner corner, any overlap/ridge easily knocked down with a spatula.
- **Application technique differences from wall plastering**: a
  contact/bonding coat (thin, spatula-spread) is essential before
  throwing plaster overhead — same principle as this practitioner's wall
  adhesion-layer trick (Round 3 ep.13), but framed as non-optional here.
  The physically hardest part of ceiling work is the upward throwing
  motion (real shoulder/arm fatigue for someone unaccustomed to it) — but
  a genuine advantage over wall plastering is that ceiling plaster doesn't
  sag/slide the way wall plaster does, so trim/cut work afterward is
  minimal.
- **Real productivity data point**: plastering one ~14m² room's ceiling
  took a full workday for two people (this practitioner + one helper).
- **Mid-project product substitution**: ran out of Волма (Volma) brand
  gypsum plaster partway through and switched to Rotband for the
  remainder, applied identically — a real substitution data point, not
  framed as a quality complaint either way.
- **Stilts purchase, real Belarus price**: industrial plastering stilts
  (named: Stiles.by brand) — **320 BYN (≈$120, trailing-6-month average to
  2021-03-06)** — good reviews cited, manual-reading strongly recommended
  before use due to setup nuances. Speaker started this job on trestles
  instead out of inexperience/fear of tripping over floor-laid pipes,
  planning to train on stilts before a future job.
- **Post-job validation**: after the plaster cured, the speaker tapped
  the entire ceiling and found no delamination anywhere — presented as
  validation of the no-full-mesh anchor system above.
- Corroborates (not new): speaker again states he skips the traditional
  float/polish finish step on the ceiling too, consistent with his stated
  wall-plastering practice/opinion from Round 3 ep.13.
