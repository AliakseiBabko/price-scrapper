## Flooring — Five-Material Pros/Cons Comparison

1. **Laminate**: fast, clean click-together installation (no dust/
   mess); available at every budget tier; more comfortable and less
   cold/injury-prone underfoot than tile. **Cons**: badly water-
   intolerant — spilled, unwiped water swells it within 2-3 days,
   unrepairable except by replacing individual planks, making it
   impractical for kitchen/bathroom/hallway use; needs a reasonably
   flat substrate or it can crack; **poor thermal conductivity — not
   suitable for heated floors**; temperature swings can dry it out and
   open gaps between planks, a very commonly seen defect; cheap budget
   laminate can delaminate over time — buy from a reputable
   manufacturer instead.
2. **Quartz-vinyl / SPC** ("laminate's main competitor," described as a
   laminate-linoleum hybrid that only borrows flexibility from
   linoleum): water-resistant (usable in hallway, kitchen, even
   bathroom, doesn't absorb moisture or swell); can be laid as one
   continuous run with no thresholds (no thermal-expansion "tenting");
   wide range of modern designs/textures; **⚠️ most manufacturers claim
   heated-floor compatibility, but the source cautions to always check
   the specific manufacturer's own technical spec sheet for the exact
   rated maximum temperature** rather than trust the general claim at
   face value; simple to install correctly if the installer knows the
   technique; available at every budget tier. **Cons**: thin material —
   transitioning to tile requires either a self-leveling pour or a
   stepped screed to absorb the height difference; more finicky
   substrate-flatness requirements than laminate — an insufficiently
   flat subfloor causes click-lock failures.
3. **Linoleum**: many associate the word with old Khrushchev-era flats,
   but a modern "comfort segment" product looks good; doesn't fear
   water and costs meaningfully less than quartz-vinyl; still commonly
   used in entryways/kitchens; unlike laminate/quartz-vinyl it's soft
   and comfortable barefoot, and stays genuinely warm-feeling at room
   temperature even unheated; can be laid over a less-than-perfect
   subfloor. **Cons**: laying over an uneven subfloor "photocopies"
   every bump/dip visibly; harder to install solo — the heavy, bulky
   roll genuinely needs a second person to move/unroll/cut; also
   shrinks/dries with temperature swings and can pull out from under
   baseboards in spots; **cannot be laid as one continuous threshold-
   free run** — always needs transition thresholds, unlike quartz-
   vinyl.
4. **Tile**: durable, cited **minimum 20-year service life**; completely
   water-resistant, the right default for bathroom/shower; also common
   in entryways (a high-moisture zone from wet/dirty shoes); **best
   thermal conductivity of all coverings compared when paired with a
   heated floor** — if genuine underfoot warmth is wanted, heat the
   tile. **Cons**: cold and uncomfortable barefoot in winter without a
   heated floor; a real injury-risk material (hard, slippery) needing
   careful walking; tiling labor is one of the most expensive line
   items in a whole renovation, and the process itself is dusty,
   messy, and slow — a full WC floor can take real time.
5. **Solid/engineered wood board or parquet**, framed explicitly as an
   emotional/tactile choice over a practical one: beautiful,
   eco-friendly, high-status; no imitation material fully replicates
   real wood's tactile/visual character. **Cons, described as where the
   pros essentially end**: expensive material and expensive
   installation (assumes an already well-prepared substrate);
   temperamental — easily scratched, water-sensitive, needs real
   ongoing care; temperature/humidity swings can cause localized
   warping.

## ⚠️ Real Named Cost-Driver — Uncoordinated Screed Level vs. Finish-Flooring Buildup

6. **⚠️ A specific, named mistake the speaker states can cost a client
   150,000-300,000 RUB unnecessarily**: pouring the whole screed to one
   flat level without accounting for each zone's different finish-
   flooring buildup thickness (worked example: porcelain tile ~1cm +
   tile adhesive ~3-5mm ≈ 1.3-1.5cm total in a hallway, vs. quartz-vinyl
   at only 3-5mm in an adjoining room) leaves a real height mismatch
   (~1cm+ in the example) once both finishes are laid. **Two after-the-
   fact fixes, both worse than avoiding the problem**: (a) a transition
   threshold strip — functionally annoying, people trip on it and
   remember it as a sign of poor renovation quality; (b) raise the
   thinner-flooring zone's level with self-leveling compound across its
   whole area — **real cited price: from 1,000 RUB/bag (≈$10)**, cost
   scaling directly with area — worked example easily reaches
   **150,000-200,000 RUB (≈$1,900-$2,500) in material alone, before
   labor**, for a single zone. **The actual fix, framed as the site
   foreman's or self-managing client's own responsibility**: at the
   screed-pouring stage, explicitly tell the screed crew each zone's
   exact planned finish-flooring buildup so some rooms are deliberately
   poured lower than others from the start — solves the whole problem
   for free at the screed stage instead of costing 150,000-300,000 RUB
   (≈$1,900-$3,700) later. Named causes for this being missed:
   carelessness, inexperience, or rushing the pour.

## Family Requirements / Preferences — Company Expert's Own Solid-Wood Preference

7. **18-19-year practitioner's personal home-flooring choice and its
   stated reasoning, distinct from the general market comparison
   above**: despite professionally working mostly with laminate/
   quartz-vinyl in the last 5-7 years, personally chose solid oak board
   for his own home, now ~12-13 years in use. Three named personal
   reasons: (a) **glued installation creates a genuinely monolithic
   feel** — glued to plywood or directly to screed with a quality
   two-component adhesive, unlike even the best "floating" laminate/
   quartz-vinyl on an underlayment, which always retains some perceptible
   micro-movement ("floating") even on a perfectly prepped substrate;
   (b) personal preference for how solid wood feels barefoot; (c) **most
   significant to him: refinishing ("циклёвка") capability** — after
   roughly 5-10 years (his own floor still hasn't needed it at 12-13
   years), the top ~2-3mm worn layer can be sanded off and refinished/
   fully recolored, producing a "whole new renovation" feeling without
   demolition; a chamfered edge is harder (but not impossible) to
   reproduce after refinishing.
8. **Engineered board pricing driver, tied directly to refinish
   capability**: an engineered board's price scales with the thickness
   of its real-wood top veneer layer — the same layer that determines
   how many refinishing cycles it can tolerate; a thicker top layer
   costs more and buys more future refinish cycles.
