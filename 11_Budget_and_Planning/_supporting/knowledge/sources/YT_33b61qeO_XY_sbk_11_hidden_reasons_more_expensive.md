## Planning Rules / Design Process & Budget — 11 Points

1. **Not every "designer" has architecture training or field experience** — a real, checkable risk: visualizations (especially AI-rendered) can specify a chandelier under a load-bearing concrete beam, or an outlet position that ends up behind a wall/wardrobe, because the designer never validated the drawing against real construction constraints. **Practical filter: hire a designer with a portfolio of *realized*, not just rendered, projects**, and prefer one who stays involved through construction over one who sells the drawing set and disappears.
2. **⚠️ Explicit "авторский надзор" ("author's supervision") vs. "технический надзор" ("technical supervision") distinction, with a common misconception named directly**: many clients wrongly believe author's supervision lets the designer control/police the builders. It does not — only technical supervision (a licensed, qualified independent inspector) can do that. Author's supervision (better called "author's *accompaniment*") is the designer periodically checking built work against the drawings (partition positions, outlet placement) and adjusting the design with the client when reality diverges — e.g. substituting a material no longer available in Russia with a close match. Independently corroborates this store's existing two-supervision-types distinction (ARCHWOOD, `YT_LOw05Uk3rUk`) from a second, unrelated company.
3. **⚠️ Technical supervision is framed as a real, quantified insurance policy, not a redundant cost**: stated example — paying ≈5,000 RUB for one inspector site visit can catch an error that would otherwise cost 2–3 million RUB to redo. Framed explicitly as independent of the builder (can produce pre-litigation documentation, testify in court if needed) — in the client's interest specifically because it's not paid by or beholden to the construction crew.
4. **⚠️ Budget buffer rule, with concrete named causes**: on top of a quoted budget (example given: 10 million RUB), keep **≈15–20% in reserve** (≈1.5–2 million on that example) — not because the quote is dishonest, but because of recurring, predictable causes: something forgotten in the estimate, a miscalculation, the client's own scope creep ("appetite comes with eating" — deciding mid-project to add ventilation or a home theater), or a real discovered defect (e.g. a bad developer-poured screed that must be fully redone once demolition starts). **Named concrete example**: garbage removal alone on a ~60 m² old-fund apartment can run **≈300,000 RUB** — a line item the source states is frequently omitted from initial estimates for this apartment type.
5. **⚠️ Lowball-then-inflate contractor pattern, explicit warning against price-only selection in the business/premium segment** — illustrated via a non-renovation analogy (a construction-equipment sale where a cheaper subcontractor was substituted last-minute, then took 1.5× the promised time, delivered defective work, and still tried to bill 30% over the original quote). The point for renovation buyers: choosing purely by low price in this segment is "the greatest foolishness you can commit."
6. **⚠️ Visualization ≠ built reality, with a concrete named mechanism**: a flush-to-floor shower pan is very often *drawn* that way but built protruding ~20cm above the floor in practice, because concealed plumbing routing can't be recessed further for technical reasons — not every designer (lacking construction background) catches this at drawing stage, and some builders push clients toward a simpler build regardless of what was drawn, framing the original design as unrealistic. **Practical mitigation stated**: get a full material specification with real stated costs attached to the design project, not just a rendered image, so a late "we ran out of budget" substitution is visible and traceable rather than silently absorbed into "well, we improvised."
7. **Business/premium-segment crews are not a paid-more version of economy-segment crews — a different skillset, not just a price tier**: the "pay double, get double the quality" pitch from an economy-oriented crew is explicitly called false — stated analogy: a crew that can't assemble a Rolls-Royce will not produce one no matter how much they're paid, the way adding parts to a Lada doesn't turn it into a different car. Concrete example given: large-format porcelain slab (stated ≈100,000 RUB/m² material cost) cut by an unqualified installer with a cheap angle grinder is a real, checkable risk of losing that material investment to cracking.
8. **⚠️ "Rough work is at least 50% of success" — a real named failure case**: engineered/parquet flooring installed over a poor-quality developer-poured screed can start creaking within 2 weeks and visibly lifting within a month, requiring **both the flooring and the screed underneath it to be redone** — not a flooring defect, a rough-stage defect that only surfaces after finish work is complete and furnished. General rule stated: anything decorative (paint, wallpaper) is cheap/easy to fix later; anything concealed (rough electrical, plumbing, screed, plastering) is very expensive or impossible to fix later — never economize on rough-stage/engineering work specifically.
9. **⚠️ Imported/custom-ordered materials carry real, open-ended lead-time risk, not a fixed schedule delta**: a fully custom-imported-material project's timeline is explicitly *not* comparable to a same-size project sourced from a local big-box retailer — realistic worst case cited as 2-3 years given current logistics conditions, a risk outside the buyer's control. **Practical mitigation**: order long-lead materials early and arrange separate (non-apartment) heated storage in advance, since not every supplier offers extended on-site storage.
10. **Custom-made furniture is its own project with its own lead time (2-3 months, up to ~6 months for imported components) and must be measured/ordered *during* construction, not after** — waiting until construction is "done" to start furniture design adds the furniture's own lead time sequentially on top of the renovation timeline, and furniture installers routinely damage nearby finish work (paint, decorative surfaces) during their own install, requiring a further touch-up/repaint pass. **A specific coordination failure mode named**: an outlet position finalized during rough electrical can end up hidden behind furniture that wasn't yet specified, requiring rework of the electrical point itself.
11. **⚠️ Contractors act in their own interest by default — a written contract, defined payment/inspection stages, and a daily photo/video report channel are the stated countermeasures**, not "trust" — the source's own stated practice: one shared chat, daily photo/video updates regardless of the client's physical location, and a defined inspection-stage structure the client can act on quickly if something looks wrong.
