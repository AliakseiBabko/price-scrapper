## Planning Rules — Three-Factor Flooring Selection Framework

- **⚠️ Room-purpose mismatch, real illustrative case**: a budget hotel owner, advised by a price-selected (not experience-selected) designer, used porcelain tile in guest corridors because it's "durable, lasts 100 years" — the real-world consequence was that every footstep (especially heels) echoed through the building at night, a guest-facing defect the designer never modeled. **Rule extracted**: hospitality/high-traffic-with-quiet-hours spaces default to carpet specifically for footstep-noise damping, not because tile isn't durable — durability and fitness-for-purpose are separate axes.
- **Commercial-grade vs. residential-grade carpet is a real, distinct product tier, not a marketing label**: residential carpet in a genuinely high-traffic commercial space (e.g. that same hotel corridor) shows bald/worn patches within 1-2 months and must be repeatedly replaced — a real, recurring cost if the wrong grade is chosen.
- **Operating-conditions check, worked example**: a house with hydronic (water-based) underfloor heating on the ground floor can safely use porcelain tile or quartz-vinyl there, but **laminate becomes "a house of cards"** under the same heating — deforms/warps, a hard incompatibility, not a durability nuance.
- **Budget-segment mismatch is presented as an aesthetic/coherence check, not just a cost one**: linoleum in a 150-million-RUB premium renovation, or imported high-end engineered wood in a bare rental unit, are each called out as internally incoherent choices regardless of the buyer's ability to pay — match the flooring tier to the project's overall segment.

## Materials — Flooring Type-by-Type: Real Mechanisms Behind Each Pro/Con

- **Linoleum — genuinely seamless (one continuous sheet), so it tolerates a bucket of spilled water with zero risk to neighbors below**, and is standard in hospitals/kindergartens for exactly this reason (frequent mopping, no seams to trap water). **⚠️ Hidden-cost trap named directly**: linoleum's famous subfloor tolerance is a double-edged sword — laid over a genuinely uneven base, every bump and dip telegraphs straight through and can never be hidden afterward; buying cheap linoleum while skipping proper subfloor prep isn't real savings, since the subfloor work itself costs real money regardless of the flooring choice made on top of it.
- **⚠️ Linoleum installation mechanics, a real labor-cost mechanism**: it arrives rolled and must be allowed to "relax"/flatten before being fixed at the baseboards — skipping this and fixing it immediately locks permanent waves into the material that never resolve. Because it's one continuous heavy sheet, one installer working alone in a room of any real size struggles physically and risks scraping/damaging the walls — **it's genuinely a two-person job**, so while the nominal per-unit labor rate looks cheap, real installed cost is roughly double a naive one-person estimate.
- **⚠️ Linoleum shrinks over time — a named long-term failure mode**: sheet linoleum trimmed tight to the walls at install can shrink and visibly pull out from under the baseboards after 1-2 years; this cannot be restored/re-stretched once it happens.
- **Laminate — historically a premium wood-imitation material, now considered obsolete by the speaker** given cheaper materials (linoleum, quartz-vinyl) have since gained comparable wood/tile-imitation textures. Cheap laminate fears water, expands/contracts with humidity, and can visibly tent/warp ("stand up like a house") — not durable. **Named single practical advantage over linoleum**: a dropped lit cigarette leaves a barely-visible mark on laminate vs. an unrepairable hole in linoleum — relevant specifically for a rental unit. **Practical price-point observation**: at genuinely expensive laminate price tiers, the speaker's own recommendation is to buy real solid wood instead, since the price gap has narrowed.
- **⚠️ Quartz-vinyl / porcelain-tile transition cost mechanism, a concrete named example**: laminate-on-underlay sits at roughly the same finished height as porcelain tile, so that specific transition usually needs no self-leveling correction — **but quartz-vinyl is thin enough that matching its height to adjacent porcelain tile (e.g. to avoid a threshold at a bathroom doorway) can force a full-apartment self-leveling pour**, a real, non-obvious added cost driven purely by the height mismatch between two otherwise-compatible materials, not by either material's quality.
- **⚠️ Quartz-vinyl's "scratches don't show" reputation is only partially true**: dark/monochrome quartz-vinyl shows longitudinal scratches clearly and they cannot be hidden or blended — the product must be professionally restored, not just touched up. Genuinely new nuance (this store's earlier trial-round quartz-vinyl note, `YT_KXmidtaUNxI`, covered construction types and underfloor-heating compatibility but not this dark-color-scratch-visibility caveat).
- **⚠️ Quartz-vinyl substrate-flatness requirement, consistent with — and independently corroborating — this round's own video 2 screed-inspection case**: cheap quartz-vinyl over an imperfect substrate develops loose/creaking locks over time, difficult to remediate; the source states plainly that an ideally flat substrate "costs real money," tying this material's real cost back to substrate prep the same way linoleum's tolerance/hidden-cost pairing does above.
- **Engineered/parquet board — business/premium segment only, rarely economy**, due to both price and required careful handling. **⚠️ Real observed defect the speaker personally dislikes**: returning to a just-finished job a month or two later, engineered board already shows small scratches even before occupancy — framed as the speaker's own aesthetic objection (others may consider this "normal for real wood"), not a universal defect claim. The "wood breathes / comfortable underfoot" claim is explicitly flagged by the speaker as subjective, not a verified mechanism.
- **Carpet (ковролин) sound-damping and softness make it suitable for young children playing on the floor**; installation differs by room size — small rooms can use a loose-laid carpet, but larger areas must be glued down to prevent wrinkling/tripping hazards. **Maintenance downside named directly**: carpet and rugs require regular vacuuming/cleaning and can harbor dust mites and small allergens — a specific caution for households with allergy suffers in private housing.
- **⚠️ Porcelain tile / ceramic — a genuine regulatory prohibition named directly, not just a design preference**: **wall-to-wall porcelain tile across an entire apartment is prohibited by soundproofing (impact-noise) building code**, except where the unit is on the ground floor with no neighbors below, or in a private (detached) house. This is a hard compliance fact, not a stylistic caution — genuinely new to this store.
- **Porcelain tile secondary cons**: cold underfoot without underfloor heating (comfortable with it); glossy-finish tile is slippery, a real caution for anyone who walks around in wool socks; **installation labor for porcelain tile is described as the single most expensive labor category among all materials covered in this video**, due to the number of technical nuances and physical difficulty; and a wrong color/texture choice in tile is called capable of "ruining a renovation" more severely than a mistake with any other flooring material, because of its visual dominance and difficulty of correction.
- **Poured/self-leveling industrial floors and cork are given only brief treatment**: poured (наливные) floors originated for heavy industrial use (forklift traffic in warehouses) and are now mainly seen in institutional corridors/garages requiring frequent cleaning to a high sanitation standard — the speaker states apartment use is simply not justified for this material. Cork is not discussed beyond being named in the material list (no content extracted).
- **Solid wood flooring — framed as a two-extreme material**: either genuinely premium (expensive, no real cons discussed) or inherited Soviet-era "sборный щитовой паркет" (assembled panel parquet), which the speaker calls squeaky, outdated, and generally overdue for replacement. Behaves as real wood (expansion/contraction) either way.
- **⚠️ Microcement — the speaker is openly skeptical of its rising popularity, names a specific structural limitation**: microcement **cannot reinforce or bridge micro-cracks in the substrate beneath it** — any hairline cracking in a self-leveling layer or a ground/polished screed will telegraph directly through the finished microcement surface and, once applied, is very difficult to remediate; living with the resulting crack is presented as the realistic outcome. **This makes microcement's substrate-prep requirement the most demanding (and therefore most expensive) of all materials covered**, an ironic result given its visually simple, minimal appearance.
- **⚠️ Developer screed reliability claim, restated and quantified — direct corroboration of this round's video 2 case study**: the speaker states developer screed is defective in **≈95% of cases** and fails to meet any applicable code norm, referencing "more than one" prior video on the subject (consistent with video 2 of this round, `l0aR7nQGh4M`). Separately notes some Moscow developers reportedly skip pouring any screed at all, leaving the buyer/contractor to pour their own — the same practical recommendation given independently in video 2.
