## Planning Rules / Design — Ten Named Interior "Cheapening" Mistakes and Their Fixes

- **⚠️ Open/visible clutter storage, named mechanism**: drying racks, mops, boxes, bags, vacuum cleaners, and household chemicals left in visible storage is what the speaker calls "visual noise" — the fix is not fewer possessions but a **dedicated place for every object** (closed cabinets, niches, furniture with built-in storage, concealed systems); stated directly as "an expensive interior isn't one with few things, it's one where every thing has its own place."
- **⚠️ Furniture pushed flush against every wall, named mechanism**: pushing all furniture to the walls to "free up" the center is described as counter-productive — it reads as a "waiting room" (sofa on one wall, TV on another, armchair on a third), because the composition breaks down with everything at the edges and nothing in the middle. **Fix**: furniture should form zones, oriented around a focal point (fireplace, stove, TV zone, art, a large mirror); even a 10-15cm gap from the wall creates a sense of airiness, and traffic paths/proportions should be respected.
- **⚠️ Excess small decor, named mechanism**: buying candles, figurines, and knick-knacks post-renovation until the space reads as a museum rather than a home — causes the eye to have "nothing to latch onto," i.e. visual noise from too many competing small focal points. **Fix**: one or two accent objects only (a large mirror, one art object, or two large figurines) — "minimalism isn't emptiness, it's the skill of leaving only what matters."
- **⚠️ Single-source or scattered lighting, five named sub-mistakes, corroborates existing resale-liquidity lighting fact independently**: (1) one fixture for an entire room, or the opposite — too many mismatched sources (spots + chandeliers all mixed); (2) overly cold color temperature; (3) over-lit zones; (4) no distinct lighting "scenarios." **Fix, four elements**: multi-level lighting (ceiling fixtures + wall sconces + accent lighting + task lamps), warm color temperature specifically (stated as the driver of a "cozy/rich" feel, not just any warm-vs-cold preference), dimming capability (especially valuable done per-zone), and smart-lighting systems as an accessible modern option. This independently corroborates this store's existing Round 3 layered-lighting-as-resale-value-lever fact from the same channel's `uqv1-7DCKYI` interview — now a second, independent statement of the same underlying principle from a different video/format.
- **⚠️ Visible/tangled cables, named mechanism and fix**: extension cords across the room, cables hanging under a TV, tangled cable clusters at a computer desk. **Fix, three tiers**: cable organizers (compact routing accessories) as the cheap/retrofit fix; at the design stage, routing cable runs into concealed channels/chases (in-wall routing) as the durable fix; wireless devices as a further option (named trade-off: periodic battery replacement).
- **⚠️ Uncoordinated materials/colors/textures chosen independently per element, named mechanism**: tile, wallpaper, baseboard, lighting, and outlet/switch plates each individually chosen in isolation can each look good alone but clash once combined — no unifying logic, materials "don't interact with each other" (named texture clash examples: concave vs. convex surfaces mixed, cold grey vs. wood, gloss vs. matte). **Fix**: build the interior on a deliberately limited palette of materials/textures/colors/shades that are pre-verified to work together — described as simple-looking but why designers spend real time on material selection specifically.
- **⚠️ Books/magazines stored as a visible pile rather than sorted, named mechanism**: books stacked randomly across the apartment (on the bed, on the fridge) reads as disorder rather than design, even when nominally "on a shelf" if sizes/colors are mismatched and chaotic. **Fix**: sort by size, color, or format, use closed shelving or dedicated small tables/compositions — with an explicit caution against overcorrecting (millimeter-precise color-sorting mixing, e.g., Russian literature next to biology texts, reads as "like you're about to move out," not designed).
- **⚠️ Cheap decorative molding/cornices, three distinct named failure modes**: (1) **material-quality ceiling**: molding cast in cheap polyurethane/foam (пенопласт) will always visually read as cheap regardless of installation quality — "however you glue it, cut it, or paint it, it stays a foam cornice," contrasted with better materials (gypsum, дюрополимер/duropolymer, higher-grade polyurethane) that don't have this visual tell; framed as acceptable only for commercial spaces, not a "premium interior" goal; (2) **overuse**: piling on so much molding that it's the only thing visible entering the room is self-defeating, not "the more the pricier"; (3) **poor installation**: crooked runs, visible seams/gaps, and bad paint finish turn even a good material into an instant visual defect — described as something that, once seen, a client won't want repeated. **Combined fix**: coordinate molding with other trim elements (cornices, baseboards), don't overload the room, install precisely, and choose a material grade appropriate to the intended finish level. A related install-conflict callout: switch/outlet plates and molding runs competing for the same wall position is a design-coordination failure (a dimensioning/planning error), not a materials issue.
- **⚠️ Visible plumbing/utility inspection hatches and exposed pipes, two named fixes**: a cheap white plastic inspection hatch mounted directly over an otherwise nice bathroom tile job is called out as a common budget-driven eyesore; the stated fix is a **magnetic tile-faced access hatch** ("плитка на магнитах") instead of the plain plastic panel — acknowledged as a real cost trade-off, not free, but "not that much more expensive" than the plain hatch. A decorative-picture-over-the-hatch workaround is mentioned but flagged as inconsistent — sometimes clashes badly with the rest of the interior, sometimes reads as intentional and works. Separately, exposed visible pipe runs are called a "plague of budget renovations" — acceptable if genuinely commercial/utility space needing access, but in a residential apartment (especially one with an actual design project) should be boxed in or concealed via furniture/purpose-built panel systems, with an explicit "just leaving it visible" ruled out.
- **⚠️ Disorganized clothing/shoe storage, named mechanism and fix**: one shoe in the entryway, the other hanging off a light fixture; a single chair pressed into service as year-round clothing storage (summer through winter items piled on it) is called out directly as visibly wrong. **Fix**: built-in wardrobes, pull-out drawers, and dedicated storage systems (including for shoes specifically) that conceal the items rather than pile them in the open.
