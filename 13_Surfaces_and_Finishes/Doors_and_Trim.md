Covers interior and entrance door anatomy, mount-type selection (hinged/sliding-pocket/concealed), rough-opening and casing sizing, entrance-door security and manufacturer tiers, swing-direction safety rules, material/finish tiers, and pricing by mount type. Format follows [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/_supporting/wiki_page_format|the shared page template]] — narrative sections first, a Quick Reference table at the end.

## 1. Key Concepts — Door Anatomy & Vocabulary

- **A door unit (дверной блок) has five components**: **leaf** (полотно, the moving panel), **frame** (коробка, the fixed box the leaf hinges/slides against), **jamb extensions** (доборы, panels that extend the frame to match a wall's actual thickness), **casing/trim** (наличники, the decorative trim covering the wall-frame junction), and **hardware** (фурнитура — hinges, handle, lock). Standard leaf height is 2.0 m; standard leaf widths are 60/70/80/90 cm. `confirmed`, `single-account` (Zemstandart batch). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **Telescopic jamb extensions are generally preferable to flat-board extensions** — they slide into a channel in the frame and self-adjust to an uneven wall or a slightly twisted frame, producing a cleaner junction than a flat board that can't absorb wall irregularity. `confirmed` as one practitioner's stated preference, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **Three mount types dominate the interior-door market**: classic hinged, sliding-pocket (into a wall cavity or behind a false wall), and concealed/hidden-mount (flush with the wall, no visible frame or casing). See §2 for a full comparison — the three differ materially on soundproofing, mechanism reliability, installation cost, and wall-prep requirements, not just appearance.

## 2. Mount Types Compared

A two-host structured comparison across 6 criteria (soundproofing, reliability, cost, wall-prep, installation logistics, use-case) is the most systematic single source on this topic in the store — summarized below, supplemented by the Zemstandart batch's earlier single-account findings and the second batch's independent corroboration.

### 2.1 Classic Hinged (default recommendation)

- Seals on 3 of 4 sides via the frame's rebate — this is the structural reason hinged doors outperform both alternatives on soundproofing.
- Full turnkey installation ≈5,000 RUB/door (2026, RUB, one company's stated rate) — the cheapest and simplest of the three to install.
- No moving mechanism beyond the hinges/handle themselves — lowest long-term failure risk of the three types.
- **This page's default recommendation** absent a specific reason to choose otherwise (space constraint, a narrow soundproofing-tolerant use case).

### 2.2 Sliding-Pocket

- **Materially worse soundproofing than hinged doors, even when hidden behind a false wall** — a basic sliding door has essentially no seal, versus a hinged door's 3-sided rebate seal. First stated `single-account` by one Zemstandart source (~80% of that speaker's own sliding-door clients complained about sound; complaints stopped once switched to hinged), and **now independently corroborated by a second, unrelated company** (Ontario), which separately ranks sliding-pocket doors as the clear soundproofing outsider among the three mount types for the same underlying mechanism (leaf anchored only at the top rail, minimal seal elsewhere) and recommends caution specifically in bathrooms/WCs and bedrooms. `confirmed`, genuinely two-source-corroborated. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b026_slidingdoors_1b571ea5.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`]
- **Mechanism reliability is a second, independently corroborated weakness.** The Zemstandart account: rollers, stoppers, and soft-close dampers commonly begin failing en masse ~3-4 years after installation, and repair requires disassembling substantial finished work (furniture, ceiling, flooring, part of the wall) to reach the hidden mechanism — `single-account`, but the speaker's stated primary reason for abandoning sliding doors in his own practice after using them heavily from ~2009. Ontario's account names a **different specific fragile component** — the opening-limiter/stopper inside the pocket cavity, awkward to reach/repair once damaged (some designs need a wall access hatch planned in for service) — a complementary, not duplicate, mechanism. Together these are treated as `confirmed`, two-source-corroborated on the general reliability weakness, though the specific failure points differ. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b026_slidingdoors_1b571ea5.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`]
- **Height/width penalty**: sliding-door hardware adds ≈5 cm to overall installed height versus a hinged door on the same 2 m leaf; an 80 cm rough opening yields ≈79 cm clear width with a hinged door but only ≈71 cm (≈8 cm less) with a sliding door — in tight corridors this can shrink an opening to as low as 60 cm. `confirmed` as a directly-spoken worked example, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b026_slidingdoors_1b571ea5.txt`]
- **Appropriate as a narrow exception, not a default**: a private ensuite bathroom/shower serving only 1-2 people (e.g. attached to a bedroom for a couple), where a hinged door's swing arc has no viable path. The downsides are judged tolerable specifically because a small, intimate user group is affected and usage frequency is low. Outside this case, both the Zemstandart and Ontario sources' standing recommendation is against sliding interior doors. `confirmed`, real project example plus explicit decision logic, `single-account` on the exception case specifically. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b027_slidingwhen_d5b14f10.txt`]
- **Pricing (material/hardware only, RUB, 2026, one company's stated ranges)**: from ~20,000 RUB. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`]

### 2.3 Concealed / Hidden-Mount

The most expensive, most demanding, and most contested mount type — see §3 for the full breakdown of its wall-prep, tolerance, schedule, and logistics implications, and §7 for the unresolved framing tension around "concealed door regret" content.

- **Costs roughly 2–3× an equivalent classic door once frame/hardware/prep/installation are fully counted**, independently stated by two unrelated companies (Ontario's itemized ≈30,000 RUB total install, roughly matching the door's own ≈30,000 RUB material/kit floor; Avalremont's independent 2–3× multiplier framing, compounding to "hundreds of thousands of RUB" across 5–6 doors in one apartment, with design renders typically not disclosing this delta up front). `confirmed`, two-source-corroborated on the multiplier itself. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v2_87348fdc.txt`]
- **Itemized concealed-door full installation** (one company, RUB, 2026): commonly ≈30,000 RUB/door — 7,000–10,000 structural (frame, concealed hinges, lock) + 5,000–6,000 two-sided painting + 5,000–7,000 flush-side wall-seam finishing + ≈7,000 exposed-reveal finishing. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`]
- **Concealed-door material/hardware-only kit**: from ~30,000 RUB (one company's stated floor). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v1_5b400bf1.txt`]

## 3. Rough-Opening and Casing Sizing Rules

- **Rough opening = leaf width + 10 cm** (5 cm clearance per side) — e.g. a 60 cm leaf needs a 70 cm opening, 70→80, 80→90. `confirmed`, corroborated independently by two unrelated sources (Zemstandart's door-specific source and the Дома Минска Ep2 designer interview, which states the same rule in a general layout-planning context). [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`; also stated in `11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_QHl1YEHMfgE_doma_minska_severny_bereg_ep2_layout.md`]
- **Casing standoff from an adjacent wall/corner** should be roughly 3-4 cm (this specific sub-figure has a likely transcription gap in the source and is recorded `uncertain`, not fully confirmed); total minimum wall run for an 80 cm door including opening allowance and casing standoff on both sides ≈110 cm ("метр десять"). The leaf-width+10cm rule and the 110 cm total-run figure are `confirmed`; the 3-4 cm standoff sub-figure is `uncertain`. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **The casing-standoff rule is a default, not an absolute** — a real documented case kept casing nearly flush to a wall because the opposite directions were blocked by a neighboring apartment's wall and a fixed electrical conduit box. The explicit prioritization rule given: **functional door width over cosmetic junction detail** when the two conflict — never sacrifice a door's usable clear width purely to satisfy the standoff convention. `confirmed`, `single-account`, real constrained-site example. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b075_entrancedoors_691c33b5.txt`]
- **The 60cm-leaf-vs-60cm-appliance rebate gotcha**: a 60 cm-wide leaf will *not* pass a 60 cm-wide appliance (e.g. a washing machine) through a 60 cm door, despite matching nominal widths. The frame's rebate/stop (прихлоп, ~1 cm) plus the ~2-3 mm perimeter installation gap reduce the true clear opening below the nominal leaf width. **Practical implication: never plan a doorway's width against an appliance's own nominal width without subtracting for the frame rebate and installation gap.** `confirmed`, concrete non-obvious mechanism, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **A doorless, casing-trimmed opening between two rooms still needs a small wall stub (~10 cm, "при стенок") preserved at each outer corner** so casing can terminate cleanly on both faces of the wall — removing it (without checking with the design technologist first) leaves one face impossible to trim cleanly. `confirmed`, real jobsite mistake-and-fix example, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b029_doorway_7f7feafa.txt`]
- **A second Zemskov/Zemstandart video (added 2026-08-10) corroborates the value of a proper door opening for baseboard termination from a different angle**: in a real 88.5m² project, framing a previously-open L-shaped corner with a proper door opening (and casing/jamb-extension trim) gave the baseboard a clean termination point at the casing, instead of a bare cut edge with a cap that — per Zemskov — reliably pops off from routine contact with a mop/broom/vacuum. Same underlying principle as the wall-stub rule above (a doorway opening gives casing/baseboard something correct to terminate against), independently arrived at in a different project context by the same practitioner two years apart. `single-account` (same channel, not independent corroboration). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_entry_hallway_dividing_wall_case_8963951b.txt`]
- **Adding a bottom threshold/rebate (порог с прихлопом) purely for extra soundproofing is not recommended** — modern interior door soundproofing is already adequate on a well-installed hinged door (the audible gain from a threshold is described as imperceptible), while a ~3 cm threshold becomes a lasting trip hazard. `confirmed` as one speaker's recommendation, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **Default interior door leaf widths**: 80 cm for living spaces (70 cm only if 80 doesn't physically fit; avoid 90 cm), 70 cm for wet rooms/utility rooms (60 cm only if space is very tight). Never let the finished casing sit flush against an adjacent wall/corner (see standoff rule above). `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]
- **Opening height: 2.07 m (207 cm), measured from the future finished screed surface — not from the rough waterproofing layer beneath it.** A speaker-stated standard from the same channel as the rough-opening rule above, added 2026-08-04. `single-account`, `confirmed` as the speaker's own stated standard. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pre_screed_waterproofing_acceptance_5b57e2e4.txt`]
- **Lintel material comparison for openings (added 2026-08-04)**: three types cited — reinforced aerated-concrete (AAC) lintel blocks, steel angle iron, or rebar embedded in mortar. AAC lintels are technically specified in this speaker's own company drawings (to match code/GOST references) but not actually recommended in practice — sold by only one supplier at roughly 20× normal cost with long lead times. Of the remaining two, rebar is acceptable for narrow openings (70–90 cm) but **will sag/deflect on wider spans** (e.g. a large kitchen-living room portal); angle iron (specifically cited: 40×4 mm, painted in two coats to prevent rust) is the recommended default for openings wider than ~90 cm and works fine for narrow openings too. `unverified`/`single-account` company preference, but the underlying deflection-risk mechanism (rebar sagging on wide spans) is a portable structural heuristic. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pre_screed_waterproofing_acceptance_5b57e2e4.txt`]
- **A second, slightly different opening-height figure exists in this batch, not silently reconciled with the 2.07 m figure above (added 2026-08-04)**: a separate video from the same channel gives a pre-plastering QC check of **minimum opening height 2.06 m**, verified with a tape measure resting on the floor (not held aloft by hand, which is flagged as an unreliable measuring habit to watch for in a crew). Both figures are `single-account` from the same practitioner, so this is not a corroborated contradiction — more likely rounding or a per-project variance than two genuinely different standards — but the two aren't blended into one number here. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **A taller 2.30 m interior door height is recommended for premium-segment projects with ceilings above 2.80 m (added 2026-08-04)** — a distinct, deliberately upsized alternative to the 2.00 m/2.07 m-opening default above, not a replacement for it. Costs roughly 2–4× a standard 2.00 m door; framed as "strongly recommended, and mandatory for wealthy clients" once ceiling height clears 2.80 m. `single-account`, segment-specific. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]

## 3a. Style Selection, Hardware, and Security — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

> [!NOTE]
> This entire subsection is a single Zemskov/Zemstandart (Alexey Zemskov, Moscow) video, `single-account`, framed by the practitioner as what he personally tells clients during design handoff — his own taste/experience, not a market survey. Adds to this page's existing "first batch, one channel" corroboration caveat at the top. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_interior_entrance_door_buying_guide_c6cf2fc5.txt`]

- **Zemskov's three interior door style categories**: Modern (contemporary but not sharp/futuristic — **his own stated default**), Hi-Tech (sharp lines, heavy glass, futuristic — only reads correctly in genuinely minimalist spaces, per Zemskov), Classic (reserved, in his view, for ceilings above ~3m — "imperial"-scale classic doors look out of place in a typical apartment under standard ceiling height). His rule: doors and furniture should be "from the same era" stylistically.
- **Zemskov's panel/finish preferences**: avoid raised door-panel moldings (his view: outdated, adds thickness for no benefit); prefer rounded, not sharp, panel edges (his reasoning: sharp corners visibly collect dust); avoid cheap laminated-paper-wrap surfaces (his observation: they peel/delaminate); on light/white doors, prefer a mottled/textured finish over flat gloss (his reasoning: flat gloss shows every fingerprint).
- **Zemskov's casing (наличник) convention**: for a Modern-style door, use straight/butt-joint casing corners, not classic 45°-mitered ones — and make the top casing piece slightly thinner than the sides so it sits recessed, hiding the top piece's cut end grain (his stated highest-quality finish detail).
- **Zemskov's handle-selection method**: grip and pull the handle firmly ~10 times in a row — reject it if uncomfortable at any point. He also advises against gold/shiny-plated handles (his observation: plating wears through at contact points, leaving bare-metal patches) in favor of silver-toned finish, and against oversized/ornate handles.
- **Zemskov's hinge-type-by-style rule**: Hi-Tech doors, concealed hinges only; Classic doors, visible/traditional hinges only; Modern can use either. His caveat: not every concealed-hinge model opens a full 180° — a door needing a wider-than-90° swing (his example: a bathroom door) must have that specified to the supplier in advance.
- **Zemskov's bathroom-vs-office lock distinction**: a bathroom door needs a lock securable from inside but openable from outside in an emergency (e.g. with a coin); an office/study door needs a proper keyed lock instead, since its purpose is securing the room when the occupant leaves.
- **Zemskov recommends magnetic (not spring-tongue) door latches** — no protruding mechanism to wear out, engages only on closing.
- **Zemskov's project-sequencing rule, easy to get backwards**: choose interior doors first → then matching baseboard/casing profile → only then the entrance door's interior-facing trim panel — even though the entrance door is bought first chronologically (start of project) while interior doors install last, its interior trim and the surrounding wall-reveal finish (откосы) must match the interior doors' eventual style.
- **Zemskov's entrance-door hardware recommendations**: vandal-resistant exterior finish, not wood-look (his reasoning: wood-look shows permanent, unrepairable damage); 2-3 hinges (3 preferred for a heavier door); two locks — one behind an armored strike-plate cover, the other a **re-pinnable type** so a lost key (~2,000 RUB) can be replaced without swapping the lock body; **passive deadbolt pins** (always-engaged, hinge-side) to resist a hinge-side grinder attack; and he explicitly recommends **against active deadbolt pins** (lock-turn-activated corner pins) — his stated reasoning: frame corners aren't the real weak point they're marketed to protect, and he's seen the connecting linkage rod wear through and snap, jamming the door permanently shut.
- **Zemskov's recommended-tier interior door price point (2019, historical — do not extrapolate to current pricing)**: 12,000-15,000 RUB/unit, factory-fitted hardware, which he describes as visually comparable to expensive Italian doors at a fraction of the price.

## 4. Concealed-Door-Specific Considerations

Concealed doors read as part of the wall rather than a framed object — that's the appeal, and also the source of every drawback below. Two independent renovation companies (Avalremont, Forcemontage) and one retailer/comparison channel (Ontario) converge on a consistent picture; see §7 for why "avoid concealed doors" content itself carries a caveat worth reading before treating this section as a flat recommendation against them.

- **Wall-prep precondition**: unlike a classic door (whose casing hides minor wall unevenness), a concealed door reveals every deviation in the adjacent wall surface. Installing one in an otherwise economy-tier renovation is described as a mismatch ("sport wheels on a car with a broken suspension") — the added wall prep required is itself an extra, often unbudgeted, cost. `confirmed`, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v2_87348fdc.txt`]
- **Fabrication-tolerance risk**: concealed doors have essentially no tolerance for fabrication/registration errors — a leaf/frame strike-plate size mismatch is invisible until final installation. One documented real example required ~1.5 months of rework to correct. `confirmed`, real jobsite example shown on camera, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v6_3c09e22a.txt`]
- **Schedule impact**: concealed door + shadow-gap ("теневой") baseboard combination adds **1.5–2 months** to a renovation's schedule overall — largely because the matching shadow-gap baseboard alone requires up to **14 distinct installation operations**, versus a simple nailed classic baseboard. A separate documented fabrication-mismatch defect (see above) added a further ~1.5 months of rework on top. `confirmed`, first concrete schedule-impact figures for concealed doors in this knowledge base. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v6_3c09e22a.txt`]
- **Shadow-gap baseboard dependency**: a concealed door is dimensioned assuming its matching shadow-gap baseboard specifically — substituting a plain/classic baseboard causes the leaf to strike or bind against it on opening. This is a **structural dependency, not a style-matching preference** — a designer who draws a concealed door is implicitly also committing the project to the shadow-gap baseboard's own cost and installation burden. `confirmed`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v6_3c09e22a.txt`]
- **Installation logistics/sequencing**: flooring must be scribed/fitted around the pre-installed door assembly — the reverse of the normal classic-door sequence, where flooring goes down first and the door is trimmed to it. Frame boxes need active protection for the renovation's full remaining duration (often ~1 year) since they're installed at rough-in stage. Leaves sometimes arrive from the manufacturer unfinished (needing on-site painting/filing). A full apartment's leaves delivered all at once creates a real on-site storage problem — best practice given is to have the manufacturer hold leaves in their own warehouse until final hang-stage. `confirmed`, five genuinely distinct logistics facts, real jobsite examples shown on camera. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v6_3c09e22a.txt`]
- **Settling-crack risk**: a concealed door can develop a visible crack at the wall-junction seam months or years after installation, from ordinary building settling (worse in new-construction) — cosmetic/structural-motion, not a manufacturing defect, but alarming to a client expecting a permanently flawless flush surface. `confirmed`, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v2_87348fdc.txt`]
- **Daily-wear visibility**: because a concealed door reads as part of the wall rather than a separate framed object, fingerprints, scuffs, and impacts are more visible than on a classic door (whose casing visually "contains" such marks) — worse with dark paint, kids, or pets. **Independently demonstrated on camera by two sources**: both dark and light painted concealed-door surfaces show a lasting fingerprint mark from a single bare touch. `confirmed`, two independent sources. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v2_87348fdc.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v6_3c09e22a.txt`]

## 5. Entrance Doors

- **Minimum functional clear/light opening width: ~74 cm (740 mm)** — stated as the practical floor below which passing through with furniture (e.g. a non-collapsible armchair) becomes impractical. This floor should take priority over cosmetic rules (e.g. casing standoff, §3) when a hard site constraint (a neighboring wall, embedded electrical conduit) forces a choice. `confirmed`, concrete numeric floor from a real constrained case, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b075_entrancedoors_691c33b5.txt`]
- **Relocating a developer-installed entrance door opening in a light partition wall is generally low-complexity**: cut/rebuild the wall section, weld a steel angle-iron frame (~8 mm stock) around the new opening for reinforcement, backfill with cement-sand mortar, weld the door frame to the angle-iron, plaster to conceal it, and finish reveals with standard interior-door jamb-extension panels to match. One documented real project shifted an opening ~30 cm this way; the only cited downside was needing anti-vandal stairwell-plaster repair, and no cost figures were given for the work itself. `single-account`, real project in a light aerated-concrete partition — **may not generalize to load-bearing walls or larger shifts.** [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b076_metaldoor_ff4787d7.txt`]
- **Manufacturer tier ladder (one manufacturer's own 6 tiers, RUB, 2026)** — a single entrance-door manufacturer's own product-catalog walkthrough, the highest-promotional-density source in this batch: Optima 65,000–90,000 RUB (45 dB soundproofing); Master 77,000–120,000 RUB (55 dB); Comfort Pro 116,000–200,000 RUB (58 dB); Professional 148,000–280,000 RUB (65 dB, Italian locks); Smart (electronic lock, any tier) 85,000–380,000 RUB; Termo (thermal-break) from 94,000 RUB. `confirmed` as this manufacturer's own stated prices; **`unverified`/brand-specific — not a market-wide benchmark.** [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v3_bc76b9ee.txt`]
- **Security-spec tradeoff — a genuine, unresolved contradiction between two competing self-interested retailers, presented here without forcing a resolution**: one Moscow-area retailer's own budget product line frames thin steel (1.2 mm) and standard locks as fully adequate, citing a secondhand press statistic ("Moscow burglaries down 35× in 13 years"; "199 city-wide in 11 months," most reportedly involving unlocked doors, not forced entry) to argue that thicker-metal/high-security doors are oversold marketing — this retailer's own "sufficient" tier prices a basic 1.2mm-steel MDF-panel door at ≈65,000 RUB and a glass-trim + electronic-lock version at ≈95,000 RUB, explicitly marketed as an adequate alternative to 150,000–200,000+ RUB competitor doors. A competing manufacturer in the same batch (the tier ladder above) sells a full ladder up to 2 mm steel / Italian locks / 65 dB soundproofing at up to 380,000 RUB. **Neither framing is adopted as this page's own security guidance** — both are self-interested claims (one favors selling cheaper stock, the other favors selling expensive tiers). `unverified` on both sides; the burglary statistic specifically is a secondhand press citation, not independently verified. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v5_4995ff31.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v3_bc76b9ee.txt`]

## 6. Door Swing-Direction Rule

A coherent, safety-grounded rule set from a single source (`single-account`), but internally consistent across 5 distinct reasons:

- **Living-space doors (bedroom, living room, study, kids' room, kitchen, library) should open INWARD**, for: safety (fewer people pass through the room itself versus the shared corridor), light-switch ergonomics (handle and switch on the same inside wall — push-to-enter keeps orientation consistent in the dark), open-position convenience (an inward door parks against an interior wall rather than blocking an often-only-~1m-wide corridor), floor-stopper placement (inside the room rather than a corridor trip hazard), and closing convenience (reachable from the corridor).
- **WC/bathroom doors (and small utility rooms/closets) should open OUTWARD**, for space (bathrooms typically have less interior floor area than the corridor) and — the **safety-critical reason** — so that an incapacitated occupant collapsed against the door from inside can still be reached, rather than the door being blocked shut by their own body.
- **Quick heuristic**: a room whose light switch is inside the room gets an inward-opening door; a room whose switch is outside the room gets an outward-opening door.
- The source states fewer than 1% of his own projects use an inward-opening WC door, for unexplained edge-case reasons — treated as a rare exception, not evidence against the rule.

`confirmed` as the speaker's consistently-applied rule; `single-account` — not yet independently corroborated by another source in this knowledge base. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b207_design_b8b72802.txt`]

- **A related switch-hand nuance (added 2026-08-04, same channel)**: an entry door should swing toward the side where the switch is reachable, so a person entering can close the door with one hand while the other hand naturally reaches the switch — a door swinging away from the switch side was called out as a specific, avoidable design mistake on a real site. `unverified`, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_room_tour_switches_doors_waterproofing_1b1017a6.txt`]

### Installation-plane offset (the frame-level version of the same safety logic)

- **The door's installation-plane offset must match its swing direction**: the frame should sit flush with the wall on the side the door swings *into* (a clean foam joint under a flat casing on that side), with the full reveal treatment on the opposite side. Getting this backwards causes the open door to strike or split the opening's corner, limits the swing to roughly 90° instead of ~95°, and is linked to more serious injuries if someone is caught in the doorway. `confirmed`, safety-relevant mechanism, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b025_interiordoors_286a8d88.txt`]

## 7. Material & Finish Tiers

Two distinct axes — **frame/core material** and **surface finish** — both cross-cut by the same underlying theme: **repairability, not just up-front price, is the real quality differentiator.**

### 7.1 Frame/Core Material (retail, one Moscow/SPb retailer, by mount type — separate from concealed vs. classic)

Four tiers, ascending:
1. **Cheapest MDF-film/cardboard-core doors** — not repairable if damaged; a scratch or dent means replacing the whole leaf.
2. **"Eco-veneer" (экошпон) doors** — a better pine frame with MDF panels wrapped in polypropylene film. Worth knowing: **the "eco"/veneer naming has no actual connection to real wood veneer or ecological materials** — it's a marketing-naming convention, not a material claim.
3. **Solid-wood ("массив") doors** — repairable; a scratch or damage costs roughly ~5,000 RUB for professional restoration rather than replacement.
4. **Concealed doors with aluminum edge trim** — itself two sub-tiers: cheap Chinese aluminum trim on a cheap pine/cardboard core (explicitly warned against by the source) versus full-aluminum-frame construction, which won't warp and is field-repairable by touch-up paint/polish.

`confirmed`, `single-account` (one retailer's own stock and stated prices — `unverified` as market-wide data). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v4_ba769014.txt`]

### 7.2 Surface Finish: Enamel vs. Film

A separate axis from frame material or mount type:
- **Enamel** can be locally sanded, filled, and repainted if scratched or chipped (inexpensive, effectively invisible after repair) and can be fully repainted a different color later. One drawback: a cheap 1-2-coat application can show minor "orange peel" texture at an angle — a manufacturing-quality issue, not inherent to enamel as a material.
- **Film (PVC/polypropylene/"eco-veneer")** cannot be locally repaired at all — a scratch requires replacing the whole panel or leaf — and cannot be painted over. A manufacturing debris fleck trapped under film stays permanently visible as a raised defect, whereas the same defect under wet enamel can be sanded flush and repainted before the enamel cures. Film-wrapped doors reportedly build up static charge and attract more airborne dust than enamel, especially visible at seams/corners (worse near a busy road with frequently open windows).
- Day-to-day cleaning performance is **not** reported as a differentiator between the two.

`confirmed`, `single-account` (same retailer as §7.1). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v7_39b5ebae.txt`]

### 7.3 Hardware

- **Door hardware (hinges + handle + lock) should not be bought cheap even on an otherwise budget door** — a quality hardware set costs ~5,000–7,000 RUB and is stated to last ~10 years; low-priced hardware is described as unreliable by default. `confirmed`, `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_doors_v4_ba769014.txt`]
- **Hidden vs. exposed hinges — the cost delta is small relative to overall project cost (added 2026-08-04)**: a plain exposed hinge costs ~700 RUB vs. ~3,000 RUB for a normal (non-fully-concealed) hidden hinge — a ~2,300 RUB difference framed as ~0.004% of a full renovation budget, argued as never worth economizing on given how visually poor cheap exposed hinges look. `single-account`, `unverified` figure. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Thin (~6 mm) door casings require matching ultra-thin baseboards (added 2026-08-04)** — a thick baseboard meeting a thin casing leaves a visibly cut/exposed baseboard edge at the joint. Always acceptable to pair a thick casing with a thin baseboard; never the reverse. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

## 8. A Note on the "Avoid Concealed Doors" Framing

Worth flagging lightly rather than overclaiming: the concealed-door regret/hidden-cost content in §4 is real and comes from businesses with real jobsite experience — but those same businesses also sell the alternative (classic doors), so the "avoid concealed doors" framing itself carries a mild structural interest, the same way a competing retailer's security-spec claims do in §5. This doesn't discredit the specific mechanisms described (wall-prep prerequisite, fabrication tolerance, schedule impact are all concrete and checkable, not vague warnings) — but it's a reason to read the framing as "concealed doors have real, documented costs that are often undersold at the design stage," not as "concealed doors are a mistake," which is a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

## 9. Common Mistakes

- **Planning a doorway's width against an appliance's nominal width without accounting for the frame rebate and installation gap** — a 60 cm door will not pass a 60 cm appliance (§3).
- **Getting the installation-plane offset backwards relative to swing direction** — limits swing angle and creates a striking/injury hazard (§6).
- **Removing a doorless opening's corner wall stub without checking the design/technologist plan** — leaves one face impossible to trim cleanly (§3).
- **Committing to a concealed door without budgeting the wall-prep precondition or the shadow-gap baseboard it structurally depends on** — both are frequently undersold at the design/render stage (§4).
- **Glass-insert panels in bathroom/WC interior doors (added 2026-08-04)** — cited reasons to avoid: visible fingerprints, and significantly worse sound isolation into/out of the room compared to a solid door. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Substituting a classic baseboard for the shadow-gap baseboard a concealed door was dimensioned against** — causes the leaf to strike or bind on opening (§4).
- **Sacrificing a door's functional clear width purely to satisfy the cosmetic casing-standoff convention** — the explicit priority order given by the source is functional width first (§3, §5).
- **Choosing sliding-pocket doors as a default rather than a narrow exception** — soundproofing and mechanism-reliability weaknesses are now independently corroborated by two unrelated companies (§2.2).

### Practical guidance
- **Concealed doors compound cost across a whole apartment** — a 2-3× per-door multiplier across 5-6 doors adds up to "hundreds of thousands of RUB" that a design render typically doesn't disclose up front (§2.3, §4). Ask for the full itemized concealed-door line (wall prep + frame/hardware + install + shadow-gap baseboard) before committing to it at the design stage, not after.
- **Don't cut cost on door hardware even on an otherwise budget door** — a ~5,000-7,000 RUB hardware set lasting ~10 years is a small fraction of total door cost and a common false-economy point (§7.3).
- **Weigh the entrance-door security-spec decision independently of either retailer's framing** (§5) — the two sources in this store directly disagree, and neither is neutral.
