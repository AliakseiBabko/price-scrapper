# Doors & Trim — Rough-Opening and Casing Sizing Rules

Part of [[13_Surfaces_and_Finishes/Doors_and_Trim|Doors & Trim]].

- **Rough opening = leaf width + 10 cm** (5 cm clearance per side) — e.g. a 60 cm leaf needs a 70 cm opening, 70→80, 80→90. Corroborated independently by two unrelated sources (a door-specific source and a designer-interview source stating the same rule in a general layout-planning context).
- **Casing standoff from an adjacent wall/corner** should be roughly 3-4 cm (this specific sub-figure has a likely transcription gap in the source and is recorded `uncertain`); total minimum wall run for an 80 cm door including opening allowance and casing standoff on both sides ≈110 cm ("метр десять"). The leaf-width+10cm rule and the 110 cm total-run figure are `confirmed`; the 3-4 cm standoff sub-figure is `uncertain`.
- **The casing-standoff rule is a default, not an absolute** — a real documented case kept casing nearly flush to a wall because the opposite directions were blocked by a neighboring apartment's wall and a fixed electrical conduit box. **Explicit prioritization rule: functional door width over cosmetic junction detail** when the two conflict — never sacrifice a door's usable clear width purely to satisfy the standoff convention.
- **The 60cm-leaf-vs-60cm-appliance rebate gotcha**: a 60 cm-wide leaf will *not* pass a 60 cm-wide appliance (e.g. a washing machine) through a 60 cm door, despite matching nominal widths. The frame's rebate/stop (прихлоп, ~1 cm) plus the ~2-3 mm perimeter installation gap reduce the true clear opening below the nominal leaf width. **Never plan a doorway's width against an appliance's own nominal width without subtracting for the frame rebate and installation gap.**
- **A doorless, casing-trimmed opening between two rooms still needs a small wall stub (~10 cm, "при стенок") preserved at each outer corner** so casing can terminate cleanly on both faces of the wall — removing it (without checking with the design technologist first) leaves one face impossible to trim cleanly. A second, independently-arrived-at case (same practitioner, different project, two years apart) reinforces the same underlying principle from a different angle: framing a previously-open L-shaped corner with a proper door opening gave the baseboard a clean termination point at the casing, instead of a bare cut edge with a cap that reliably pops off from routine mop/broom/vacuum contact.
- **Adding a bottom threshold/rebate (порог с прихлопом) purely for extra soundproofing is not recommended** — modern interior door soundproofing is already adequate on a well-installed hinged door (the audible gain from a threshold is described as imperceptible), while a ~3 cm threshold becomes a lasting trip hazard.
- **Default interior door leaf widths**: 80 cm for living spaces (70 cm only if 80 doesn't physically fit; avoid 90 cm), 70 cm for wet rooms/utility rooms (60 cm only if space is very tight). Never let the finished casing sit flush against an adjacent wall/corner.
- **Opening height: 2.07 m (207 cm), measured from the future finished screed surface — not from the rough waterproofing layer beneath it.** A second, slightly different figure exists in the same batch, not silently reconciled with this one: a pre-plastering QC check elsewhere in the same source gives a minimum opening height of 2.06 m, verified with a tape measure resting on the floor (not held aloft by hand, an unreliable measuring habit). Both figures come from the same practitioner, so this isn't a corroborated contradiction — more likely rounding or per-project variance — but the two aren't blended into one number here.
- **A taller 2.30 m interior door height is recommended for premium-segment projects with ceilings above 2.80 m** — a deliberately upsized alternative to the 2.00 m/2.07 m default, not a replacement for it. Costs roughly 2–4× a standard 2.00 m door; framed as "strongly recommended, and mandatory for wealthy clients" once ceiling height clears 2.80 m. Segment-specific, not a general recommendation.
- **Lintel material comparison for openings**: three types cited — reinforced aerated-concrete (AAC) lintel blocks, steel angle iron, or rebar embedded in mortar. AAC lintels are technically specified in one company's own drawings (to match code/GOST references) but not actually recommended in practice — sold by only one supplier at roughly 20× normal cost with long lead times. Of the remaining two, **rebar is acceptable for narrow openings (70–90 cm) but will sag/deflect on wider spans** (e.g. a large kitchen-living room portal); angle iron (specifically cited: 40×4 mm, painted in two coats to prevent rust) is the recommended default for openings wider than ~90 cm and works fine for narrow openings too — the deflection-risk mechanism itself (rebar sagging on wide spans) is a portable structural heuristic worth knowing regardless of the specific supplier preference around it.

## Installation-Plane Offset (the frame-level version of the swing-direction safety logic)

**The door's installation-plane offset must match its swing direction**: the frame should sit flush with the wall on the side the door swings *into* (a clean foam joint under a flat casing on that side), with the full reveal treatment on the opposite side. Getting this backwards causes the open door to strike or split the opening's corner, limits the swing to roughly 90° instead of ~95°, and is linked to more serious injuries if someone is caught in the doorway — see [[13_Surfaces_and_Finishes/analysis/Door_Swing_Direction|Door Swing-Direction Rule]] for the full safety reasoning this connects to.
