## Underfloor Heating: Type Selection (Water vs. Electric vs. Film)

**Water (hydronic) heating ranks best on nearly every technical parameter, but is illegal in a standard apartment and should almost never be planned for one.** It cannot be connected to a central-heating system, and cannot be installed above a neighboring unit's living space — both restrictions apply regardless of one-pipe or two-pipe central-heating scheme, and together rule it out for well over 99% of apartments (the only real exception: an individually-metered, individually-heated ground-floor unit). Where it *is* legal (a private house with individual heating), it wins on durability (50-year rated life vs. 15 years for the other two types), reliability (~100% vs. ~40-70%), and running cost, at the price of the slowest heat-up/cool-down — best suited to running continuously rather than cycling on demand.

**Film (infrared) is the right default wherever water isn't an option and the covering allows it** — fastest heat-up (full heat in ~5 minutes under a thin covering), good repairability (it sits under a removable floating floor, so a failed section can be replaced without breaking out anything embedded), and the second-cheapest to run. Its restrictions are real, though: **never under ceramic tile** (the film layer blocks tile adhesive from bonding to the base — even perforated variants don't perforate enough to fix this), **never under premium laminate with metal-foil backing** (functionally the same as putting metal in a microwave), and **never in a wet room** (its electrical connections sit exposed behind only bitumen patches — any water ingress is a real shock risk).

**Electric (direct-heating cable/mat, embedded in screed or self-leveling compound) fills the remaining gaps** — under tile, in wet rooms — everywhere film can't go and water isn't legal. Trade-off: the most expensive to run, and essentially zero repairability once embedded (repair means breaking out the base layer entirely).

## Where Underfloor Heating Is Actually Needed

**Three rules a design project should apply by default, not case-by-case debate**: (1) replace a standard radiator with underfloor heating of the same "type" wherever legally possible — in practice this mostly means a private house with individual heating, since a standard apartment can't legally substitute water underfloor heating for central-heating radiators; (2) **heat every tile-covered floor area**, for two independent reasons — tile's high thermal conductivity makes a room-temperature floor feel genuinely cold underfoot (not just a preference — extended contact is a real chill/illness risk), and tile is chosen in wet-prone rooms specifically *because* it tolerates standing water, which still needs drying afterward to prevent mold; (3) **any insulated (joined) loggia needs underfloor heating, no exception** — the floor is the coldest surface in any room, and no other heat source (a relocated radiator, a space heater) warms it, because a neighbor's own floor insulation blocks heat transfer up through your loggia's slab regardless of their own floor-heating setup.

## Floor-Standing Radiators & In-Floor Convectors: Why to Avoid Both

**Both of these "skip the false wall" heating-fixture options carry real, fixture-specific failure modes that a standard wall-mounted radiator behind a false wall avoids entirely.** A floor-standing radiator's supply-pipe stub-outs sit in a tile cutout that cracks from thermal cycling (grout has no elasticity) and can violate laminate's required expansion gap badly enough to shift/crack the radiator assembly or void the laminate's own warranty — even the elongated bracket-foot holes some models use to address this only account for expansion in one direction, not the perpendicular direction laminate also expands in. It's also physically fragile (a hard mop strike can start a leak at the joints) and cleaning around the base/pipes is awkward. **Where a floor-standing unit can't be avoided** (floor-to-ceiling glazing, no windowsill depth), route the pipes through a windowsill recess or a 10-12cm podium instead of through the finished floor, so they never contact the flooring at all. For a wall-mounted radiator, false-wall depth also varies — ~50mm against an ordinary partition, but a full ~150mm against a load-bearing wall (which can't itself be recessed into).

**In-floor convectors carry every one of those risks plus several unique ones, and the single most serious documented safety incident recorded anywhere in this vault**: they're hydraulically incompatible with central heating (their longer, more convoluted plumbing path routes hot water to neighbors' simpler radiators instead), legally equivalent to illegal water underfloor heating, need a genuinely deep (120mm+) fan-assisted unit to perform adequately in a central-Russia climate (fan units cost 10-40x a comparable bimetal radiator and have short-lived, hard-to-replace fans), add real screed weight/height cost, and hide a leak until it's already flooded the neighbor below — versus a standard radiator's immediately visible leak. **Most importantly**: a documented real incident describes a young child's fingers becoming permanently trapped in a convector grille that laminate expansion had jammed solid, resulting in a lasting injury after failed rescue attempts. Avoid in-floor convectors wherever a wall-mounted radiator is feasible instead.
