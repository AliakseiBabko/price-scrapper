# Electrical & Lighting

Covers electrical planning and rough-in, panel/circuit decisions, switches and controls, and lighting design (technical and decorative). Format follows [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/_supporting/wiki_page_format|the shared engineering-systems page template]] — narrative sections first, a Quick Reference table at the end.

## 1. Key Concepts

- **Rough electrical vs. finish electrical** — rough-in (cable chasing/routing, panel assembly, temporary switches/sockets so the site can be worked with power) happens early, concealed under plaster/putty; finish electrical (outlets, switches, fixtures, panel trim) happens near the end, after painting/flooring. Pricing and mistakes cluster differently at each stage — see §3 and §4.
- **Technical light vs. decorative light** — technical light is the functional recessed/track lighting doing the actual room illumination; decorative light is the visible fixture (pendant, chandelier, sconce) chosen for its appearance. They're often sourced and budgeted separately, and the two scale very differently by quality tier (see §3.4).
- **A decorative light fixture may be an original or a "replica"** (an unlicensed reproduction of a designer fixture) — this is a real, commonly-encountered distinction in the market, not a technicality. Budget/mid-tier decorative lighting is commonly replica-sourced; know which you're buying, since price and provenance diverge sharply between the two.

## 2. Planning & Layout

- **Plan furniture layout before finalizing socket placement** — a socket blocked by a bed headboard or a cabinet is a common, avoidable mistake; work from the furniture plan, not a generic room template.
- **Use dedicated circuits and individual breakers for high-power consumers** (kitchen appliances, laundry equipment, and other major loads) — prevents circuit overload and is a basic safety/reliability measure, not a luxury.
- **Leave generous extra cable length (1.5–2m) on hardwired appliance connections** (e.g. a cooktop) — protects against a stretched diagonal run or a hazardous splice if the appliance's final position shifts slightly during installation.
- **On soundproofed/acoustically-insulated walls, project electrical back-boxes outward by the planned insulation thickness** — otherwise sockets end up recessed behind the finished wall surface instead of flush with it.
- **Route hidden in-wall channels for TV/AV wiring and behind-TV sockets** where a wall-mounted TV is planned — eliminates visible hanging cables between the TV and any console/soundbar.

## 3. Rough Electrical: Sequencing & Common Pitfalls

- **Plaster masonry (brick/block) walls before chasing electrical cable slots** — chasing into unplastered walls risks unanchored, loosely-hanging cable runs and typically forces the electrical work to be split into two messy, re-visited phases instead of one clean pass.
- **Do not cut recessed breaker-panel niches into load-bearing monolithic concrete walls** — this violates structural building codes; use a surface-mounted panel (in a cabinet or utility space) instead where the wall is load-bearing.
- **Do not puncture subfloor soundproofing membranes with mechanical anchors to clip electrical conduit** — this ruptures the acoustic insulation's integrity; use adhesive clips bonded to the soundproofing layer instead.
- **Price rough electrical work by floor area, not by cable length** — pricing by linear meters of cable run is a known vector for "turns out we needed more cable, please pay more" upselling; a floor-area-based quote is harder to inflate after the fact this way. *(See the smeta-literacy section of [[11_Budget_and_Planning/Budgeting_Guide|Budgeting Guide]] §4 for the general version of this rule, which applies the same logic to plumbing rough-in.)*

## 4. Switches & Controls

- **Install a master cut-off switch near the main entrance** — a single button that shuts down all (or most) apartment lighting on the way out, rather than checking/switching off room by room.
- **Use two-way ("проходной") switches for a room's main light where it makes sense** — most valuably bedside, so a light can be switched off without getting back up. Cheap to install, disproportionately high perceived comfort value.
- **Install dimmers as a low-cost upgrade** on primary living/bedroom lighting circuits — a simple way to adjust ambiance without needing multiple fixture types.
- **Minimize switch/gang count per location** — e.g. two gangs for a living room's main controls is commonly enough; six is usually over-specification that adds cost and visual clutter without functional benefit.

## 5. Lighting Design

- **Recessed point fixtures with a good diffusing bulb are commonly sufficient** for even room illumination — a cited example: roughly 4 well-placed fixtures adequately lighting a ~30 m² room, versus installs with far more fixtures than the space actually needs (over-specification is a common, avoidable cost driver here too).
- **For bedrooms, perimeter spot lighting is generally preferable to a single central pendant/chandelier directly over the bed** — a central fixture overhead creates glare when lying down and looking up; perimeter spots avoid this. Layer in a secondary/ambient lighting option (a lamp, a dimmed accent) alongside the main circuit for flexibility.
- **Curtain niches and window sills/reveals are worth wiring** — a recessed curtain-track niche with a built-in LED channel hides the track hardware and doubles as sleek indirect lighting; power outlets built into a window sill or reveal give convenient access for laptops or seasonal decorative lighting without a visible extension cord.
- **Technical vs. decorative light budgets scale very differently by quality tier** — technical (functional) lighting differentiates mainly on optics quality (lens design, light distribution/scene control) at higher tiers, while decorative lighting differentiates mainly on **original vs. replica** sourcing — budget/mid tiers commonly use replica decorative fixtures, while higher tiers move to licensed originals. *(Secondary reference, single-source, brand-free summary — see [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/case_studies/buro_segment_pricing_2026_case|BURO segment-tier case study]] for the full tier breakdown, which does name specific brands the source itself uses.)*

## 6. Buying / Practical Guidance

- **Sourcing lighting fixtures directly from overseas factory suppliers** is reported to cut hardware cost roughly 2–3× (some sources cite up to 50–70%) versus buying through local resellers — a meaningful lever specifically for lighting, which tends to carry high retail markup.
- **Don't pay a labor premium for purely decorative sub-screed cable routing patterns** — cables are fully encased in screed either way; code-compliant routing without unnecessary crossovers is sufficient, and paying extra for a "neater" hidden pattern buys nothing visible or functional.
