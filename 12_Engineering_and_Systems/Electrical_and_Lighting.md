## 1. Key Concepts

- **Rough electrical vs. finish electrical** — rough-in (cable chasing/routing, panel assembly, temporary switches/sockets so the site can be worked with power) happens early, concealed under plaster/putty; finish electrical (outlets, switches, fixtures, panel trim) happens near the end, after painting/flooring. Pricing and mistakes cluster differently at each stage — see §5 and §6.
- **Technical light vs. decorative light** — technical light is the functional recessed/track lighting doing the actual room illumination; decorative light is the visible fixture (pendant, chandelier, sconce) chosen for its appearance. They're often sourced and budgeted separately, and the two scale very differently by quality tier (see §8.4).
- **A decorative light fixture may be an original or a "replica"** (an unlicensed reproduction of a designer fixture) — this is a real, commonly-encountered distinction in the market, not a technicality. Budget/mid-tier decorative lighting is commonly replica-sourced; know which you're buying, since price and provenance diverge sharply between the two.
- **Four functional lighting categories** — main/general light, task light, decorative light, and night light. Each has a distinct role and its own recommended color temperature: ~4000K for main/task light (neutral-white, good for visual tasks), ~2700–3000K for decorative/night light (warm, comfortable at low brightness). A main/task-lit space should target roughly ≥500 lux for comfortable use. Night light belongs in hallways and WCs but should **never** be installed in a bedroom (disrupts sleep) — and decorative lighting is not a substitute for a dedicated night-light circuit where one is needed. `single-account`. *(See §8.1 for how this maps onto specific fixture types.)* [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_lighting_types_taxonomy_7b7745fa.txt`]

## 2. Planning & Layout

- **Plan furniture layout before finalizing socket placement** — a socket blocked by a bed headboard or a cabinet is a common, avoidable mistake; work from the furniture plan, not a generic room template.
- **Use dedicated circuits and individual breakers for high-power consumers** (kitchen appliances, laundry equipment, and other major loads) — prevents circuit overload and is a basic safety/reliability measure, not a luxury. *(See §3 for the full circuit-count formula and cable-gauge table this generalizes into.)*
- **Leave generous extra cable length (1.5–2m) on hardwired appliance connections** (e.g. a cooktop) — protects against a stretched diagonal run or a hazardous splice if the appliance's final position shifts slightly during installation.
- **On soundproofed/acoustically-insulated walls, project electrical back-boxes outward by the planned insulation thickness** — otherwise sockets end up recessed behind the finished wall surface instead of flush with it. On rough-in day, this means recessing the box *deeper* than a standard wall would need, anticipating the later-added insulation layer. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_panel_and_roughin_walkthrough_44e6f59d.txt`]
- **Route hidden in-wall channels for TV/AV wiring and behind-TV sockets** where a wall-mounted TV is planned — eliminates visible hanging cables between the TV and any console/soundbar. **Size the channel at least 40 mm** (round or square) even if the cables planned at rough-in time are thinner — an HDMI connector body is bulky and won't pass through an undersized channel later. Use a 4-module outlet plate behind the TV with one module deliberately left empty, plus a reserved vertical chase, so a future AV cable can still be pulled through after the TV/console is finalized. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_hiding_tv_cables_5c8f1d11.txt`]
- **Plan electrical layout against a 5–15 year family-change horizon**, not just the move-in-day household — children aging and changing needs, handedness, and general life-stage change are cited drivers. A low-cost future-proofing move: leave one or two extra outlets wired but hidden behind the finished wallpaper/wall surface, reachable later by cutting the wallpaper rather than opening the wall. A smart-lock-triggered lighting "scene" is offered as a reconfigurable alternative to a single physical master entrance kill-switch (compare §7's simpler physical-switch version). `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_alternative_layout_planning_and_smart_home_081f275f.txt`]
- **Electric-point layout via centerline referencing (added 2026-08-04, reinforced 2026-08-10)**: instead of dimensioning each point from a wall corner, define a virtual centerline (e.g. centered on a future bed) at a set offset from the corner, then dimension every point symmetrically from that centerline. Produces provably symmetric placement (e.g. matching sconces/outlets either side of a bed) regardless of minor furniture-placement shifts. **A second same-channel source states this as an explicit exception to the corner/opening default-reference rule** (§ below): bedroom points around a bed should introduce an intermediate "center of bed" reference point and never default to a corner/doorway — reasoning given is that bed-relative asymmetry (e.g. 5cm off-center one side, 11cm the other) is far more visually noticeable than corner-relative precision, even when the corner-relative numbers look cleaner (e.g. round 70/80cm). `single-account`, same-channel restatement (not independent corroboration — treat as one company's stable practice, not two confirmations). [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_electrical_point_notation_bed_center_exception_8259a335.txt`]
- **Video door-intercom placement (added 2026-08-04)**: mount it in the "clean zone" (living area), not the "dirty zone" near the entry/shoe area — answering it shouldn't require crossing a dirty floor in socks/bare feet. `single-account`, stated as "critically important" but frequently overlooked. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Under-sink cabinet electrical provisioning (added 2026-08-04)**: provision 3 outlets at the kitchen-sink cabinet at project stage — garbage disposal, active water-filtration unit, and one spare — so a later retrofit doesn't require new wiring. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]

## 3. Cable Sizing, Circuits & Panel Design

- **Cable-gauge-by-load table (copper)**: 1.5 mm² for lighting circuits, 2.5 mm² for socket/outlet circuits, 4 mm² for ovens, 6 mm² for cooktops and other high-draw appliances. A 4–6 mm² high-load circuit should never be split or branched partway along its run. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_cable_sizing_breaker_selection_99e7c33b.txt`]
- **Breaker curve selection**: curve B for socket circuits (tolerates appliance inrush current without nuisance-tripping), curve C for lighting and AC circuits. Curve D is not recommended for residential use. Breaker amperage pairs to cable gauge: roughly 10A for 1.5 mm², 16A for 2.5 mm², 20A for 4 mm², 32A for 6 mm² (this practitioner's convention — verify against your local code before treating as a fixed rule). `single-account`, internally consistent across two independent videos from the same channel. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_cable_sizing_breaker_selection_99e7c33b.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_panel_circuit_formula_31314f45.txt`]
- **Panel circuit-count formula**: at least 2 circuits per room (one lighting, one sockets), plus 1 dedicated circuit for AC where present; oven and cooktop each get their own dedicated circuit; wet rooms (bathroom, WC, kitchen) require an RCD/differential breaker. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_panel_circuit_formula_31314f45.txt`]
- **Keep at least 15 cm separation between power cabling and low-voltage/signal cabling (added 2026-08-04)** — reduces electromagnetic interference between the two. On the same channel: a 15kW three-phase panel is cited as insufficient to run two tankless water heaters plus an induction cooktop simultaneously — use a recirculation pump with a thermal sensor instead of a second full tankless heater if total connected load is tight. `single-account`. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pro_secrets_lifehacks_f0be401f.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_zemskov_premium_class_tips_71691249.txt`]
- **Three checkable "cornerstones" for accepting a finished panel (added 2026-08-04)**: (1) every eventual load is accounted for and split into separate circuits per room/function, so any single area (outlets, AC, loggia, etc.) can be isolated independently; (2) every breaker is labeled/marked; (3) **selectivity** — breakers sized/coordinated so a downstream fault trips only the smallest relevant breaker, never the main incomer or the building's own riser breaker. **Test every outlet and switch (with a test lamp or tester) before accepting rough electrical** — protects against a later trade (e.g. a ceiling installer) puncturing a cable and the customer wrongly blaming the original electrician. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_split_rough_finish_contracting_and_rough_stage_checklist_9a4efa5e.txt`]
- **Route electrical conduit above plumbing runs on the ceiling, not stacked on the floor** — avoids compounding conduit-plus-pipe thickness inside the screed depth near door thresholds (a common cause of an unwanted raised threshold lip). Conduit should clear adjacent walls by 15 cm minimum (20–25 cm preferred) for install access and later baguette/trim clearance. A voltage-control relay is recommended as a standard panel component; wireless leak sensors are preferred over wired ones since they can be relocated later without rewiring. `single-account`, guest electrician's stated practice on this channel. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_panel_and_roughin_walkthrough_44e6f59d.txt`]
- **Sample only the ground (yellow-green) conductor when checking an existing de-energized service-entry cable's gauge** — avoids disturbing the live/neutral conductors unnecessarily. Extending an undersized service cable: crimp ferrule sleeves convert stranded wire to a solid-equivalent end for screw-terminal connections, and a heat-shrink gel splice kit handles any in-line joint. `single-account`, procedural detail. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_extending_service_entry_cable_d5e17a29.txt`]
- **Document electrical point coordinates in a design plan consistently**: heights are measured from finished floor by default, *except* window-slope outlets/switches (measured from the slope/sill instead) and ceiling-referenced points like an AC-unit alignment mark (measured from the ceiling). Kitchen outlet heights are still measured from the floor at design time, not the countertop, since the exact final countertop height is harder to plan against reliably before it's installed. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_electrical_point_coordinates_in_design_projects_2dc422a9.txt`]
- **Point-content notation is a separate concern from coordinate notation (added 2026-08-10)**: a standard abbreviation legend keeps a point's description compact without losing precision — R = outlet, V = switch, ТВ = TV cable output, УТП = UTP/ethernet output, with a leading digit for quantity (e.g. "2R+ТВ+УТП" = a 4-module point: 2 outlets + TV + UTP). **Never split a point's content description from its location marker into a separate reference table** — a real cited failure (a table-row mismatch, row 7 confused with row 8) produced an as-built switch where two outlets were specified, discovered only after the client paid for rework out of pocket. `single-account`, real cited failure with financial consequence. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_electrical_point_notation_bed_center_exception_8259a335.txt`]
- **Corner-labeling convention, independently restated by the same channel ~2.5 years apart**: rooms shown top-down; corners labeled alphabetically clockwise from the top-left (А, Б, В, Г, extending further for rooms with more than 4 corners); a point's horizontal reference defaults to the nearest door/window opening or the nearest labeled corner, whichever is closer — stated explicitly as a project-wide fallback rule so individual points don't need to restate an obvious reference. **Worked verification payoff**: with this convention a client can independently verify any installed point in ~3-5 minutes using only a tape measure and the single sheet for that room. `single-account`, same-channel restatement over time, not independent corroboration. [sources: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_electrical_point_coordinates_in_design_projects_2dc422a9.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_electrical_point_notation_bed_center_exception_8259a335.txt`]
- **Why "finished floor" specifically means the finished screed surface, and why screed must be poured before rough electrical (added 2026-08-04)**: most electric-point heights are referenced to the "zero mark" — the finished screed surface, not the sub-floor or the waterproofing layer beneath it. Wiring electrical before screed is poured risks the crew missing the true zero mark, a documented cause of misaligned outlets/switches above a kitchen backsplash or asymmetric bedroom light fixtures. Point coordinates are also referenced to wall corners/opening edges, so a room's geometry should be re-verified before both screed *and* electrical rough-in — errors are still cheap to fix before screed, but expensive once electrical points are set against them. `single-account`, `confirmed`, with a concrete failure-mode example given. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pre_screed_waterproofing_acceptance_5b57e2e4.txt`]
- **On walls getting frame-mounted soundproofing against a neighbor's wall, add an extra electrical setback before applying the standard offsets (added 2026-08-04)**: the soundproofing furring-out setback should be 10 cm plus the soundproofing layer's own thickness; from *that* face, the standard offsets apply (electric points 15 cm from the corner, switches 90 cm from floor — see §4). Electricians must be told about this setback before starting, or their conduit/box work ends up buried inside the eventual soundproofing/furring assembly. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_pre_screed_waterproofing_acceptance_5b57e2e4.txt`]
- **Cable-splicing capsules for extending an incoming high-load cable (added 2026-08-04)**: when running a new full-length cable from the building's electrical room isn't possible, extend the existing cable with a rated splicing capsule rather than any ad-hoc splice — fire-safety-rated and reliable; an incorrect splice on a high-load cable risks overheating and, in the worst case, fire. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Aggregate all data/AV cabling behind one low-voltage panel (added 2026-08-04)**: TVs, workstations, a redundant internet feed from the building's own low-voltage room, Wi-Fi router, and network switch behind one cover — for reliable Wi-Fi/wired internet to every outlet without visible clutter. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Outlet/junction boxes must themselves be sound-rated wherever the surrounding wall has soundproofing (added 2026-08-04)** — a standard (non-rated) box lets essentially all sound through the wall at that point, defeating an otherwise well-executed soundproofed wall. A common, easy-to-miss defect. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Dedicated non-switchable circuit for a router + security camera (added 2026-08-04)**: one always-on outlet, wired ahead of the main panel's breakers (with a splitter for router/camera/accessories) so it stays powered even when every breaker in the panel is off — lets remote camera footage be checked without a truck roll, and prevents anyone on-site from disabling surveillance by killing power at the panel. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **Size a specialty appliance's power feed by that appliance's own installer, not the general electrician's assumption (added 2026-08-04)** — a general rule generalized from a sauna-heater example: a fire-resistant cable rated for the heater's declared load must be sized by the sauna contractor's own spec, not guessed at by the general crew; an under-specified cable sized without checking would have needed an expensive replacement. Applies to any specialty equipment with its own manufacturer-specific load/wiring requirements, not just saunas. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_business_class_five_attributes_19385e7a.txt`]

- **Rough-electrical service life is distinct from finish-electrical wear, per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)**: cable/connection lifespan doesn't depend on user behavior the way a socket/switch's does (a socket wears based on how carefully a plug is pulled). Zemskov's stated design target for rough electrical: survive at minimum 5 renovation cycles, at his estimated 5-7 years between renovations — implying a ~30-year minimum lifespan target. His explanation for why breaker protection matters here: a 2.5mm² cable can handle 20-25A continuously but is protected by a 16A breaker (this store's already-recorded pairing), so the breaker trips well before the cable itself would begin to heat up under a fault, given correct installation — reinforces/explains the mechanism behind the cable-gauge/breaker table above rather than adding a new number. Zemskov's own anecdotal, `unverified` observations: aluminum wiring surviving 70+ years in some properties he's seen, and an estimate that modern GOST-standard cable with spring-clamp terminals could plausibly last on the order of 100 years — his own opinion/estimate, not a manufacturer or code figure. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_rough_electrical_lifespan_testing_e85f5373.txt`]

## 4. Mounting Heights & Positioning Standards

A repeated on-camera demonstration (four people, heights 150–185 cm) found that the resting hand-drop height from standing is essentially constant across adults — the basis for treating switch height as a fixed constant rather than something to scale per-occupant. `single-account`, but internally consistent across multiple videos from the same channel, including the most recent (2025) source in this batch.

- **Switches: 90 cm from finished floor** (95 cm as a variant for unusually tall households), **switch center 15 cm from the rough door-opening edge** — moving it to 10 cm was shown causing the switch to conflict with door casing once installed. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_switch_outlet_mounting_heights_44f28608.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_switch_height_offset_rules_d3142f90.txt`]
- **Bedside points scale off furniture, not a fixed number**: bedside lamp switch ~120 cm if headboard-mounted, or ~70 cm if nightstand-mounted; bedside outlets ~70 cm; an over-bed pendant/lamp cable drop at ~100 cm. Horizontal position is set by a formula combining false-wall offset + curtain-zone clearance + nightstand width + half the bed's width. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_switch_outlet_mounting_heights_44f28608.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_bedside_outlet_placement_e4adedc3.txt`]
- **Countertop outlets: 110 cm from finished floor by default**, shifting by the same delta as the countertop height when a non-standard countertop is planned (e.g. +10 cm countertop → +10 cm outlets). Window-slope and ceiling-referenced points are the explicit exceptions (see §3's documentation-convention entry).
- **A small set of point types should track occupant eye level instead of a fixed height**: video-intercom monitors and underfloor-heating thermostats, both read visually while standing/using them — unlike switches, which are operated by feel, not sight. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_switch_outlet_mounting_heights_44f28608.txt`]

## 5. Rough Electrical: Sequencing & Common Pitfalls

- **Plaster masonry (brick/block) walls before chasing electrical cable slots** — chasing into unplastered walls risks unanchored, loosely-hanging cable runs and typically forces the electrical work to be split into two messy, re-visited phases instead of one clean pass.
- **Do not cut recessed breaker-panel niches into load-bearing monolithic concrete walls** — this violates structural building codes; use a surface-mounted panel (in a cabinet or utility space) instead where the wall is load-bearing.
- **Do not puncture subfloor soundproofing membranes with mechanical anchors to clip electrical conduit** — this ruptures the acoustic insulation's integrity; use adhesive clips bonded to the soundproofing layer instead.
- **Price rough electrical work by floor area, not by cable length** — pricing by linear meters of cable run is a known vector for "turns out we needed more cable, please pay more" upselling; a floor-area-based quote is harder to inflate after the fact this way. *(See the smeta-literacy section of [[11_Budget_and_Planning/Budgeting_Guide|Budgeting Guide]] §4 for the general version of this rule, which applies the same logic to plumbing rough-in.)*
- **A window-slope-mounted outlet/switch panel must be glued (foam adhesive) to the slope structure, not merely clipped in** — a loose/wobbly slope outlet when unplugging a cord is a simple, homeowner-testable sign of a bad glue job. The commonly repeated "condensation can short a slope outlet" worry is treated by this source as a myth: the slope cavity sits at the same temperature as the room, so there's no dew-point crossing inside it to condense water in the first place. `single-account`, and the physics reasoning is not independently verified here. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_outlets_in_window_slopes_d351e012.txt`]
- **Load-test the panel and verify every point's coordinates right after rough wiring is finished — not deferred to a later "pre-finish" inspection (added 2026-08-04)**. Extends §3's panel-acceptance checklist with a timing rule: catching a defective/miswired cable or a misplaced point *before* plastering/tiling closes the wall is fast; catching it at the pre-finish stage instead is described as slow, painful, and expensive. A supervisor who only checks point coordinates at pre-finish is flagged as a sign of inadequate on-site QC. `single-account`, `confirmed`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt`]

## 6. Temporary / Construction-Stage Electrical

Site power during the renovation itself uses a deliberately simpler, distinct spec from the permanent finish wiring — worth planning for separately, not treating as an afterthought.

- **Temporary wiring spec**: 2×2.5 mm² ungrounded, vs. permanent 3×1.5 mm² (lighting) / 3×2.5 mm² (sockets), both grounded — grounding is treated as unnecessary at the temporary stage. Install temp wiring after the design/electrical plan exists but before interior walls go up, and after ceiling priming (not before). Keep temp cable runs continuous with no in-line splices, and maintain at least 1 m between a temporary bulb socket and any outlet block (heat and cable-tugging risk). Reserve a dedicated ~10A circuit specifically for a job-site charging station. `single-account`, corroborated across two videos from the same channel. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_temporary_wiring_during_renovation_1979b3f1.txt`, `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_temporary_construction_electrical_c533e500.txt`]
- **Off-the-shelf retail extension cords are commonly undersized for sustained job-site power draw** (this source cites conductors as thin as ~0.75 mm²) — build a job-site extension from proper 2.5 mm² cable instead of relying on a retail cord for tools or heaters. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_temporary_wiring_during_renovation_1979b3f1.txt`]
- **Leave 1–2 working temporary outlets plus one working light per room during rough-in**, even on a multi-floor build before any finish work starts — improves site usability and safety while work is ongoing. `single-account`, thin technical detail from a customer-testimonial-style source. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_cottage_electrical_testimonial_35fcb62a.txt`]
- **A single-outlet variant of temporary wiring (added 2026-08-04)**: run a flat two-conductor cable (2×0.75 mm², rated for most power tools except heavy-draw ones) from the panel to one outlet, mounted via adhesive-tape cable clips, instead of stringing extension cords + work lights room to room — reduces trip hazards and avoids re-routing dangling cords during screed pours. **Note the tension with the fact above**: a different video on this same channel calls ~0.75 mm² conductors undersized for sustained job-site draw and recommends building a job-site extension from proper 2.5 mm² cable instead — both are `single-account` from the same practitioner; the likely reconciliation is scale (one dedicated, short, single-outlet run vs. a general-purpose multi-tool extension cord), but this isn't stated explicitly by either source, so the tension is recorded rather than silently resolved. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]

## 7. Switches & Controls

- **Install a master cut-off switch near the main entrance** — a single button that shuts down all (or most) apartment lighting on the way out, rather than checking/switching off room by room. **In a fuller version of this switch (added 2026-08-04), it also controls the duty/night-light circuit below**, giving one action that covers every light in the apartment, not just the main circuits. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Use two-way ("проходной") switches for a room's main light where it makes sense** — most valuably bedside, so a light can be switched off without getting back up. Cheap to install, disproportionately high perceived comfort value. **Extend the same logic across a whole chain of adjoining passage rooms (added 2026-08-04)** — e.g. entry → corridor → living room — so every room in the chain shares one switch group controlling the same circuit, switchable from any point in the chain, and nobody has to backtrack to turn off a light in a room they've already left. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Dedicated "duty"/night-light circuit (added 2026-08-04)**: a separate, always-on low-level light — distinct from the main light — in hallways, entries, and bathrooms, letting someone navigate at night without triggering bright main lighting; controlled from at least the entry door and typically from multiple points. Consistent with, and adds a controls-level detail to, this page's existing four-category lighting taxonomy (§8) — a night-light circuit belongs in hallways/WCs, never bedrooms. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **Install dimmers as a low-cost upgrade** on primary living/bedroom lighting circuits — a simple way to adjust ambiance without needing multiple fixture types. **Finalize the actual bulbs being used before buying a dimmer**: dimmer compatibility depends on matching leading-edge vs. trailing-edge dimming to the bulb/driver type, and buying the wrong pairing is a common, avoidable mistake. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_choosing_finish_electrical_10bb55ba.txt`]
- **Minimize switch/gang count per location** — e.g. two gangs for a living room's main controls is commonly enough; six is usually over-specification that adds cost and visual clutter without functional benefit. Prefer several single-gang switches over one crowded multi-gang plate where space allows, and keep every standard toggle switch in the apartment on the **same ON/OFF orientation** (e.g. always down = on) — a mixed-orientation install is a common, avoidable annoyance. Never position a switch inside a future door's swing zone, checked against the door leaf's full opening arc, not just its closed position. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_switch_placement_general_rules_0b688415.txt`]
- **Two-way switches work in pairs; a run with three or more control points adds "intermediate" (crossover) switches between the two end switches**, not more two-way switches — a distinct component from a standard two-way switch. Wire joints must terminate in a proper junction box; twisted-and-taped joints left exposed outside a junction box are a common, unsafe jobsite shortcut. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_two_way_switch_wiring_d19dbb7b.txt`]
- **A bathroom/WC exhaust fan's switch belongs inside the room it serves, not in the adjoining hallway.** Automating a fan is fine for auto-OFF (a timer that shuts it off after a delay) but not for auto-ON (an occupancy/humidity sensor that starts it without warning) — the auto-ON case is described as startling for the occupant. `single-account`, opinion-based but mechanism-specific. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_bathroom_fan_switch_d8c0351d.txt`]
- **Prefer spring-clamp (screwless, self-clamping) terminals over screw terminals on finish-stage outlets/switches** — screw terminals are more prone to loosening from thermal cycling over time. Evaluate a device by country of manufacture and terminal type rather than by brand name alone. Avoid switches with an integrated LED backlight/indicator (a reported flicker/glow issue with some LED bulb types). `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_choosing_finish_electrical_10bb55ba.txt`]
- **Kitchen counter outlet rule (added 2026-08-04)**: every open/free counter zone gets its own outlet; the main cooking zone additionally needs its own task-lighting switch. **Avoid touch/capacitive-sensor controls for standard-tier under-cabinet lighting** — reported as unreliable (doesn't consistently respond to a hand-wave despite marketing claims) and less convenient with wet/dirty hands, which can still press a physical switch but may be reluctant to touch a sensor pad; may be more appropriate for premium-segment kitchens. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

## 8. Lighting Design

- **Recessed point fixtures with a good diffusing bulb are commonly sufficient** for even room illumination — a cited example: roughly 4 well-placed fixtures adequately lighting a ~30 m² room, versus installs with far more fixtures than the space actually needs (over-specification is a common, avoidable cost driver here too).
- **For bedrooms, perimeter spot lighting is generally preferable to a single central pendant/chandelier directly over the bed** — a central fixture overhead creates glare when lying down and looking up; perimeter spots avoid this. Layer in a secondary/ambient lighting option (a lamp, a dimmed accent) alongside the main circuit for flexibility.
- **Curtain niches and window sills/reveals are worth wiring** — a recessed curtain-track niche with a built-in LED channel hides the track hardware and doubles as sleek indirect lighting; power outlets built into a window sill or reveal give convenient access for laptops or seasonal decorative lighting without a visible extension cord.
- **Technical vs. decorative light budgets scale very differently by quality tier** — technical (functional) lighting differentiates mainly on optics quality (lens design, light distribution/scene control) at higher tiers, while decorative lighting differentiates mainly on **original vs. replica** sourcing — budget/mid tiers commonly use replica decorative fixtures, while higher tiers move to licensed originals. *(Secondary reference, single-source, brand-free summary — see [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/case_studies/buro_segment_pricing_2026_case|BURO segment-tier case study]] for the full tier breakdown, which does name specific brands the source itself uses.)*

### 8.1 Ceiling Light Source Types

Three distinct ceiling light-source types come up repeatedly once you move past a single generic "downlight" mental model — worth knowing before choosing a technical-lighting approach for a stretch or drywall ceiling. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_light_source_types_bbe33d4f.txt`]

- **Perimeter LED strip** (run inside a stretch-ceiling baguette channel) is **decorative/supplementary only** — never sufficient as a room's main light source, even when told otherwise. It reads well as ambiance but cannot replace true task/main lighting.
- **Flat light panels** are bright and energy-efficient, but difficult to retrofit onto an *existing* stretch ceiling since they need mounting holes the ceiling wasn't prepped for — plan for them before the ceiling goes in, not after.
- **Softboxes** (fixtures that emit light from their full surface/sides, not just the underside) give the most even room-fill light at comparatively low wattage — this source's own stated preference among the three types.

### 8.2 Five Arguments Against Recessed Spotlights — per Zemskov/Zemstandart (added 2026-08-10)

> [!NOTE]
> Demonstrated live by Zemskov in his own rented apartment (which has recessed spotlights he criticizes as "what not to do," not his own installed work) — `single-account`, but extensively demonstrated with real counts rather than asserted in the abstract. Reinforces and substantially extends this store's existing brief recessed-spotlight warnings (from `web_zemspro_about_development` and `yt_8odGOTVAuIk`, same channel). [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_recessed_spotlights_against_a91e17ca.txt`]

1. **Constant bulb burnout, per Zemskov's own live count**: in his rented apartment, bulbs fully replaced ~2 months prior already showed 5 of a group out, 2 of 9 out elsewhere, 3 of 12 out elsewhere, and one hallway group down to 1 lit out of several — verified live by testing each switch individually to rule out a wiring fault. His stated mechanism: a recessed-spotlight bulb fails on the same schedule as any bulb; with 40 installed, the *aggregate* failure rate is 40× a single fixture's. He acknowledges some viewers report longer-lived LED bulbs — treat the mechanism (many fixtures = proportionally more visible failures) as the durable point, not his specific ~3-month figure.
2. **Uneven "cone" light pattern, per Zemskov**: on textured/patterned wallpaper, narrow spotlight cones create visibly uneven illumination that reads as a bumpy wall even when the wall is flat — he says this is worse on plain wallpaper than heavily-patterned (which already masks unevenness).
3. **Harsh reclined glare, per Zemskov**: lying in a bathtub or reclined chair under spotlights (especially halogen) produces direct eye glare; he describes routinely getting up specifically to switch them off mid-relaxation.
4. **Poor actual room fill despite visual brightness, per Zemskov**: a narrow downward beam mostly hits a dark floor that reflects little light back; walls get only the cone's edge, so ambient fill stays weak even with many fixtures lit — his view is this drives occupants to keep adding more light sources without fixing the underlying problem.
5. **Higher total cost than one premium fixture, per Zemskov**: each spotlight needs its own wiring run, most are 12V and need a step-down transformer (an added cost and failure point), and ongoing maintenance accrues on both bulbs and supply. His own comparison: an 18-spotlight installation shown in the video cost several times more than a single high-end chandelier that would give brighter, more even, lower-maintenance light in the same space (no specific total figures given for either option).
- **Bonus injury note, per Zemskov**: spotlight glass diffuser lenses can heat up enough that their retaining mechanism fails and the lens falls and shatters — he cites one real incident in his own unit.

## 9. Country House / Cottage-Specific Electrical

Content specific to a standalone house/cottage rather than an apartment — a distinct planning context with its own long-cable-run and exterior-exposure concerns. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_cottage_intercom_prewiring_d9d61714.txt`]

- **A country-house intercom (video-entry) system needs a long, dedicated cable run pre-planned early** — from the gate/street call panel to the indoor unit — unlike an apartment intercom's short run. Retrofitting after the building shell/paving is finished requires demolition.
- **Any outdoor low-voltage cable end must be sealed against water ingress** — an unsealed, untaped cable end was documented on a real jobsite as an active corrosion risk.

## 10. Buying, Installation Quality & Hiring Red Flags

- **Sourcing lighting fixtures directly from overseas factory suppliers** is reported to cut hardware cost roughly 2–3× (some sources cite up to 50–70%) versus buying through local resellers — a meaningful lever specifically for lighting, which tends to carry high retail markup.
- **Don't pay a labor premium for purely decorative sub-screed cable routing patterns** — cables are fully encased in screed either way; code-compliant routing without unnecessary crossovers is sufficient, and paying extra for a "neater" hidden pattern buys nothing visible or functional.
- **Buy finish-stage switches/outlets/frames roughly one week before installation, not months in advance** — reduces storage damage/loss risk and avoids having already bought fixtures invalidated by a mid-project design change. Count the actual number of finish electrical points room-by-room after rough-in is complete (not from the original drawing) since e.g. kitchen point counts commonly shift once final cabinetry is confirmed. Keep a small spare stock — roughly 3–4 spare outlets and a few spare switches/frames of each type used, for a ~100 m² apartment. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_buying_finish_electrics_ea6d4af9.txt`]
- **A hired electrician's own tool kit is a usable, checkable vetting signal**: a dust-extraction vacuum attached to the drill during wall-chasing/socket-hole cutting is a mandatory red-flag check — its absence means the crew is spreading masonry dust through the unit's cavities, a later air-quality issue. Round, cleanly drilled socket-box holes (vs. hacked/oversized square holes) indicate a competent installer. Standard on-center spacing for a ganged multi-socket box run is 71 mm. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_electrician_tool_kit_1ec9e3da.txt`]
- **Strip cable insulation only with a dedicated wire-stripping tool, never a utility/box knife** — a utility knife risks nicking the conductor's copper strands, a latent failure point. Remove a device's factory-installed mounting screws before rough-finish work (plastering/puttying), not at final finish-electrical install — screw holes left in during finishing get clogged with paint/putty and have to be re-drilled later. `single-account`. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_socket_installation_technique_db371f80.txt`]
- **Lighting-circuit wiring is thinner-gauge than socket-circuit wiring and isn't rated for high-draw loads** — improvising a power tap from a light fixture/socket to run a higher-draw tool or appliance risks overheating the lighting circuit's conductors. `single-account`, safety caveat attached to an otherwise informal "hack" video. [source: `90_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_lightbulb_socket_power_hack_35bdf49c.txt`]
