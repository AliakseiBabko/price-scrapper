# Electrical — Lighting Design

Covers general fixture-count guidance, bedroom lighting, ceiling light-source types, and a detailed practitioner case against recessed spotlights. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Electrical_and_Lighting|Electrical & Lighting]].

## General Principles

**Recessed point fixtures with a good diffusing bulb are commonly sufficient** for even room illumination — a cited example: roughly 4 well-placed fixtures adequately lighting a ~30 m² room, versus installs with far more fixtures than the space actually needs (over-specification is a common, avoidable cost driver).

## Color Temperature Rules (added 2026-08-20)

- **⚠️ Never exceed 4000K anywhere in a residential space.** Comfortable range for living areas: **2700–4000K**. Warm light (lower K) reads as cozier and generally makes an interior look more expensive; **3000K is the single most universal default** — cozy enough without reading yellow, works in nearly any room. Reserve **neutral ~4000K light for pure task areas** — laundry rooms, walk-in closets, storage rooms, and specifically a kitchen worktop's own task lighting (see Kitchen note below) — not for general/ambient illumination.
- **⚠️ Every fixture running simultaneously in the same scene must share the same color temperature.** A ceiling fixture at 3000K next to a wall sconce at 4000K running together (e.g. evening TV-watching) reads as visibly mismatched and cheap, even though each fixture is individually fine.
- **⚠️ Evening/decorative lighting must be the same color temperature throughout the entire home, not just within one room.** A hallway's evening lighting at a different temperature than the bedroom/living room's evening lighting stands out as visually "off" the moment you move between rooms.
- **⚠️ Both over- and under-powering a fixture is a real mistake, not just under-powering.** Overly bright light in the evening measurably disrupts melatonin production and circadian rhythm, making it harder to wind down for sleep — this isn't just a comfort preference. Conversely, underpowered light in a zone that genuinely needs brightness (entryway, bathroom, walk-in closet) causes eye strain and faster visual fatigue from working in insufficient light. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## Scenario-Based Lighting: the "Minimum Three Scenarios" Rule (added 2026-08-20)

**⚠️ Golden rule: every frequently-used room needs a minimum of three distinct lighting scenarios (scenes), not one or two fixtures wired as a single on/off group.** The single most common lighting mistake, per this source, is a room with only one (or at most two) light sources total — insufficient for how differently a room is actually used across a day (cleaning/task use vs. relaxed evening use vs. a specific secondary need like mirror grooming or reading). Worked room-by-room scenario sets:

- **Bathroom (4 scenarios)**: main/task light (cleaning, grooming); decorative/evening dimmed light; dedicated mirror light (see the bathroom-specific note below); a night-light scenario for occasional nighttime use.
- **Kitchen-living (3 scenarios + task lighting)**: main light (overcast days, cleaning); evening/decorative light (dinner, movie ambiance); worktop task lighting (see Kitchen note below) plus separate dining-table task lighting (a pendant or a narrow-beam spot).
- **Bedroom (3-4 scenarios)**: main/task light; evening/decorative light (same temperature as the rest of the home); an individual reading light per side, ideally dimmable, aimed directly at the activity (book, phone, laptop) rather than general room-filling — the best implementation is a **directional** fixture, not just any bedside lamp; optionally, bias/ambient lighting for TV viewing if a bedroom TV is used (see Cinema-Mode Bias Lighting below).
- **Hallway/entrance (4 scenarios)**: main light; evening light; mirror light; a navigation/night-light scenario — see Night/Navigation Lighting below.

`single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## Cinema-Mode Bias Lighting (added 2026-08-20)

**⚠️ Light the wall/zone around the TV itself, not the whole room, to reduce eye strain during viewing — a real physiological mechanism, not just ambiance.** Watching a bright screen in an otherwise dark room forces the eye to repeatedly readjust to a large contrast swing every time your gaze leaves the screen (checking a phone, glancing around) — a small sconce, a lit niche near the TV, or a bias-lighting product that colors itself to match the on-screen content all reduce that contrast without needing to light the whole room. Applies equally to a living-room TV and a bedroom TV. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## Night/Navigation Lighting (added 2026-08-20)

**⚠️ A dedicated low-level navigation/night-light circuit, corroborating and extending this store's existing whole-apartment night-light-circuit guidance** (see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Lighting_and_Electrical|Bathroom — Lighting & Electrical]]): implementation options include an LED strip run under the ceiling, baseboard-integrated lighting, a small fixture built into a wall-outlet-style faceplate (matching the switch/outlet product line, if the manufacturer offers one), or a plain recessed wall fixture — purpose is safe navigation (avoiding bumped corners/doors) without triggering a full bright light at night, typically routing between the bedroom and the kitchen or bathroom.

**⚠️ Avoid triggering navigation/night lighting from a motion sensor in a bedroom specifically — a real, previously-unrecorded failure mode.** It sounds convenient (light auto-triggers when a foot touches the floor at night), but in practice a motion sensor near a bed fires on any incidental motion — a foot or arm hanging off the mattress edge, a blanket corner dropping to the floor — producing frequent, genuinely irritating false triggers throughout the night. **Wire bedroom night lighting to a manual switch instead.** This is a bedroom-specific caution distinct from a hallway/corridor motion sensor, which doesn't have the same false-trigger exposure. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## Avoid Directional/Spot Light Above Any Reclining Position (added 2026-08-20)

**⚠️ A recurring pattern across rooms, worth stating as one general rule: never place a spotlight, track fixture, or narrow-beam downlight directly above a spot where someone reclines and looks upward** — a bed, a sofa, a bathtub. This corroborates and generalizes the existing bathtub-reclining-glare point in the "Five Arguments Against Recessed Spotlights" section above and this page's own existing bedroom central-pendant caution. If a fixture must be positioned there anyway, use one with a narrow, carefully aimed optic (light directed away from the reclining eye-line) rather than a broad downward spread. `single-account`, `unverified`, though converging with the page's existing recessed-spotlight and central-pendant findings above. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

## Fixture Buying Criteria Beyond Color Temperature (added 2026-08-20)

**⚠️ A fixture's suitability depends on more than color temperature and looks — check before buying, not just from a catalog photo**: brightness/lumen output for the space, beam angle, CRI (color rendering index — how accurately the light renders true colors), smart-home protocol compatibility if relevant (e.g. DALI), and whether the fixture has a lens (affects beam control/glare). For a **business-plus or premium-tier project specifically, a proper lighting calculation from a lighting designer is recommended as close to mandatory** — for an economy/comfort-tier project, a full lighting-designer calculation is described as not always necessary; the more elaborate the project, the more this step matters. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_dJMsXYUyh7A_kruglov_lighting_guide|YT_dJMsXYUyh7A]]]

**For bedrooms, perimeter spot lighting is generally preferable to a single central pendant/chandelier directly over the bed** — a central fixture overhead creates glare when lying down and looking up; perimeter spots avoid this. Layer in a secondary/ambient lighting option (a lamp, a dimmed accent) alongside the main circuit for flexibility.

**Curtain niches and window sills/reveals are worth wiring**: a recessed curtain-track niche with a built-in LED channel hides the track hardware and doubles as sleek indirect lighting; power outlets built into a window sill or reveal give convenient access for laptops or seasonal decorative lighting without a visible extension cord.

## Ceiling Light Source Types

Three distinct ceiling light-source types come up repeatedly once you move past a single generic "downlight" mental model:

- **Perimeter LED strip** (run inside a stretch-ceiling baguette channel) is **decorative/supplementary only** — never sufficient as a room's main light source, even when told otherwise. It reads well as ambiance but cannot replace true task/main lighting.
- **Flat light panels** are bright and energy-efficient, but difficult to retrofit onto an *existing* stretch ceiling since they need mounting holes the ceiling wasn't prepped for — plan for them before the ceiling goes in, not after.
- **Softboxes** (fixtures that emit light from their full surface/sides, not just the underside) give the most even room-fill light at comparatively low wattage — this source's own stated preference among the three.

## Five Arguments Against Recessed Spotlights — per Zemskov/Zemstandart

> [!NOTE]
> Demonstrated live by Zemskov in his own rented apartment (which has recessed spotlights he criticizes as "what not to do," not his own installed work) — `single-account`, but extensively demonstrated with real counts rather than asserted in the abstract. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_recessed_spotlights_against_a91e17ca.txt`]

1. **Constant bulb burnout, per Zemskov's own live count**: in his rented apartment, bulbs fully replaced ~2 months prior already showed 5 of a group out, 2 of 9 out elsewhere, 3 of 12 out elsewhere, and one hallway group down to 1 lit out of several — verified live by testing each switch individually to rule out a wiring fault. His stated mechanism: a recessed-spotlight bulb fails on the same schedule as any bulb; with 40 installed, the *aggregate* failure rate is 40× a single fixture's. Treat the mechanism (many fixtures = proportionally more visible failures) as the durable point, not his specific ~3-month figure.
2. **Uneven "cone" light pattern**: on textured/patterned wallpaper, narrow spotlight cones create visibly uneven illumination that reads as a bumpy wall even when the wall is flat — worse on plain wallpaper than heavily-patterned (which already masks unevenness).
3. **Harsh reclined glare**: lying in a bathtub or reclined chair under spotlights (especially halogen) produces direct eye glare — routinely enough to get up specifically to switch them off mid-relaxation.
4. **Poor actual room fill despite visual brightness**: a narrow downward beam mostly hits a dark floor that reflects little light back; walls get only the cone's edge, so ambient fill stays weak even with many fixtures lit — driving occupants to keep adding more light sources without fixing the underlying problem.
5. **Higher total cost than one premium fixture**: each spotlight needs its own wiring run, most are 12V and need a step-down transformer (an added cost and failure point), and ongoing maintenance accrues on both bulbs and supply. An 18-spotlight installation shown cost several times more than a single high-end chandelier that would give brighter, more even, lower-maintenance light in the same space.

**Bonus injury note**: spotlight glass diffuser lenses can heat up enough that their retaining mechanism fails and the lens falls and shatters — one real incident cited in the speaker's own unit.
