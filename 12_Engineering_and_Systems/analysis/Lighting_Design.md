# Electrical — Lighting Design

Covers general fixture-count guidance, bedroom lighting, ceiling light-source types, and a detailed practitioner case against recessed spotlights. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Electrical_and_Lighting|Electrical & Lighting]].

## General Principles

**Recessed point fixtures with a good diffusing bulb are commonly sufficient** for even room illumination — a cited example: roughly 4 well-placed fixtures adequately lighting a ~30 m² room, versus installs with far more fixtures than the space actually needs (over-specification is a common, avoidable cost driver).

**For bedrooms, perimeter spot lighting is generally preferable to a single central pendant/chandelier directly over the bed** — a central fixture overhead creates glare when lying down and looking up; perimeter spots avoid this. Layer in a secondary/ambient lighting option (a lamp, a dimmed accent) alongside the main circuit for flexibility.

**Curtain niches and window sills/reveals are worth wiring**: a recessed curtain-track niche with a built-in LED channel hides the track hardware and doubles as sleek indirect lighting; power outlets built into a window sill or reveal give convenient access for laptops or seasonal decorative lighting without a visible extension cord.

## Ceiling Light Source Types

Three distinct ceiling light-source types come up repeatedly once you move past a single generic "downlight" mental model:

- **Perimeter LED strip** (run inside a stretch-ceiling baguette channel) is **decorative/supplementary only** — never sufficient as a room's main light source, even when told otherwise. It reads well as ambiance but cannot replace true task/main lighting.
- **Flat light panels** are bright and energy-efficient, but difficult to retrofit onto an *existing* stretch ceiling since they need mounting holes the ceiling wasn't prepped for — plan for them before the ceiling goes in, not after.
- **Softboxes** (fixtures that emit light from their full surface/sides, not just the underside) give the most even room-fill light at comparatively low wattage — this source's own stated preference among the three.

## Five Arguments Against Recessed Spotlights — per Zemskov/Zemstandart

> [!NOTE]
> Demonstrated live by Zemskov in his own rented apartment (which has recessed spotlights he criticizes as "what not to do," not his own installed work) — `single-account`, but extensively demonstrated with real counts rather than asserted in the abstract. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260810_recessed_spotlights_against_a91e17ca.txt`]

1. **Constant bulb burnout, per Zemskov's own live count**: in his rented apartment, bulbs fully replaced ~2 months prior already showed 5 of a group out, 2 of 9 out elsewhere, 3 of 12 out elsewhere, and one hallway group down to 1 lit out of several — verified live by testing each switch individually to rule out a wiring fault. His stated mechanism: a recessed-spotlight bulb fails on the same schedule as any bulb; with 40 installed, the *aggregate* failure rate is 40× a single fixture's. Treat the mechanism (many fixtures = proportionally more visible failures) as the durable point, not his specific ~3-month figure.
2. **Uneven "cone" light pattern**: on textured/patterned wallpaper, narrow spotlight cones create visibly uneven illumination that reads as a bumpy wall even when the wall is flat — worse on plain wallpaper than heavily-patterned (which already masks unevenness).
3. **Harsh reclined glare**: lying in a bathtub or reclined chair under spotlights (especially halogen) produces direct eye glare — routinely enough to get up specifically to switch them off mid-relaxation.
4. **Poor actual room fill despite visual brightness**: a narrow downward beam mostly hits a dark floor that reflects little light back; walls get only the cone's edge, so ambient fill stays weak even with many fixtures lit — driving occupants to keep adding more light sources without fixing the underlying problem.
5. **Higher total cost than one premium fixture**: each spotlight needs its own wiring run, most are 12V and need a step-down transformer (an added cost and failure point), and ongoing maintenance accrues on both bulbs and supply. An 18-spotlight installation shown cost several times more than a single high-end chandelier that would give brighter, more even, lower-maintenance light in the same space.

**Bonus injury note**: spotlight glass diffuser lenses can heat up enough that their retaining mechanism fails and the lens falls and shatters — one real incident cited in the speaker's own unit.
