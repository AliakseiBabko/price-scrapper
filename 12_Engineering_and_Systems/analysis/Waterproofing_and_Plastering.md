# Plumbing — Waterproofing & Plastering

Covers code-level waterproofing requirements, the gypsum-vs-cement plaster tradeoff, and detailed floor-membrane installation/QC technique. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]].

## Waterproofing & Plastering Standards

- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited regulatory sources are jurisdiction-specific and do not isolate one channel: **A flush (level-with-the-rest-of-the-apartment) bathroom floor's legal status in Russia has reportedly changed over time** — permitted, then prohibited, then permitted again as of recent sources, **conditional specifically on waterproofing the entire apartment's floor, not just the bathroom**. If this condition isn't met and it's discovered, code enforcement can require retrofitting a raised threshold, or in the worst case a full teardown and redo. One source frames it plainly: it's cheaper and easier to just waterproof the whole apartment upfront than to risk this outcome later. **This regulatory history is Russia-specific and its current status in Belarus is unconfirmed** — treat a flush floor as requiring explicit local verification before committing to it.
- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited regulatory source is not channel-isolated: **Roll/membrane waterproofing under the screed, with an upturn onto the walls, is mandatory by code in Russia (per one source) with no exceptions** — it always separates the screed from direct wall/floor-finish contact. After the screed, brush/paint-on ("обмазочная") waterproofing with taped joints at the floor-wall junction is applied, plus the same taped-joint treatment at wall corners.
- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited code/practice source is not channel-isolated: **Brush-on wall waterproofing is not legally mandatory when using cement-based plaster, but is mandatory when using gypsum-based plaster**, since gypsum plaster must be protected from moisture exposure. One source's own company uses gypsum plaster throughout its bathroom work and therefore brush-waterproofs floor-to-ceiling on every bathroom wall regardless of the cement-plaster exemption.
- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited comparison is not channel-isolated: **Gypsum vs. cement plaster tradeoff**: gypsum is faster to apply (more workable, easier to level), lighter (less structural load on the slab — relevant since tile adds further weight on top), and dries meaningfully faster (cited: cement plaster 21–30 days vs. gypsum plaster 7–14 days). One source cites Knauf as now endorsing gypsum plaster for wet rooms specifically.
- **Add an anti-fungal admixture to ceiling paint applied above a stretch ceiling, apartment-wide, not just in wet rooms** — rough-stage work (screed curing, plastering, even plain painting) generates significant condensate, and a ceiling surface can accumulate enough moisture to grow mold that's invisible once the stretch-ceiling membrane is installed over it. A preventable, easily-skipped step since the mold stays hidden until the ceiling is eventually opened. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **Always wrap the sewage stack in sound-insulating material during any bathroom renovation in a multi-unit building** — corroborated by two sources; one gives concrete product/cost detail (a bitumen-adhesive wrap kit, ~3,000–5,000 RUB, a few hours of DIY-feasible installation). Without it, plastic-stack drain noise from an upstairs flush is described as sounding like "a waterfall behind the wall" — a comfort issue, not a structural one, but a low-cost fix worth including as standard scope.
- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited source is archive-only and has no `channel:` field: **Brush-on/paint-applied waterproofing should always be bright blue, never gray** — gray waterproofing is visually indistinguishable from the surrounding gray plaster, making coverage gaps invisible to a non-expert inspecting the work. Bright blue makes any missed patch obvious even without training. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_split_rough_finish_contracting_and_rough_stage_checklist_9a4efa5e.txt`]

## Floor Waterproofing Membrane Installation & QC

Four videos from a "general renovation tips" playlist (same channel, `single-account`) cover rolled bitumen/felt-backed membrane installation and acceptance-inspection in unusual technical depth:

- **Membrane sheet orientation**: rolled sheets have one bare edge (felt backing stripped) meant to overlap the next sheet — this bare edge must face **toward the wall**, not run down the middle of a floor span. Placed backwards, two bare (non-felted) edges end up overlapped against each other mid-floor, creating a long unsealed seam that defeats sound isolation across the whole run. **Checkable defect**: a visible "dip" along the seam — the unsealed section sits thinner and lower than the felted areas around it.
- **Seam/overlap integrity check**: walk every seam and overlap pocket and gently try to lift the edge with a tool (not a fingernail/putty knife). A pocket that doesn't tear/lift further than a small existing flap is properly bonded; one that tears open (revealing an unglued dry patch) is a real defect. A good single-contour install should, per one source, let several buckets of water poured in one room theoretically spread to every room without reaching the unit below.
- **Patch technique for a torn seam**: heat the substrate spot and the patch piece *separately* first, then press together once both are hot, then heat-seal the patch's perimeter with a torch. A common shortcut to reject: crews heating only the perimeter and smearing it for looks without properly melting the base contact. Keep patches small — a large patch can't be reheated evenly before it cools, causing poor adhesion.
- **Never waterproof over the area immediately around a sewage/drain stack, outside wet rooms** — a deliberate gap must be left there so that if an upstairs neighbor floods, water drains straight down through the gap to the unit below rather than pooling and eventually breaching sideways into your own unit. Corroborated across all four sources as a commonly-forgotten, "actively harmful to the owner" mistake when crews seal it out of over-caution.
- **Waterproofing-height-vs-screed rule, with concrete examples**: membrane must extend above the planned final screed level — cited fold-up heights from one real apartment: 10cm, 11.5cm, 10cm, 12cm across different rooms — trimmed down after the screed is poured and sealed with acoustic sealant at the finished-floor line. One source sets the target screed level with a laser level referenced from the building's own entry-door threshold, so the interior finished floor matches the corridor floor with no step at the front door.
- attribution: unconfirmed — the cited source is single-account and has no decisive channel field: **"Single contour" vs. "single bowl" waterproofing**: single-contour (continuous across all rooms, applied *after* interior walls exist, no seams/thresholds between rooms) is preferred over a single-bowl approach (one continuous sheet applied to the whole apartment *before* interior walls go up). The single-bowl-first method is easier to execute and looks impressive on camera, but has two real downsides: a pinhole anywhere in it leaks straight through to the unit below, and membrane installed before walls exist gets reliably punctured multiple times during wall construction. `single-account`, speaker's stated reasoning against a technique he says he used on his own earlier project.
- **Perimeter damper strip**: a foam damper strip must run the full perimeter where waterproofing meets a wall at the pour line, to absorb screed expansion/contraction and prevent cracking — reportedly forgotten by installers "guaranteed." Waterproofing correctly folded up the wall at that seam can itself serve this damper function.
- **Wet-room walls need full heat-bonding**; elsewhere on the same floor, wall-bonding is more optional — in a bathroom the membrane should be melted enough to become "part of the wall," fully sealed around the entire perimeter including at load-bearing structure.
- **Two-pass site cleaning before priming**: knock down construction dust with a garden pump sprayer and a long-handled broom, bag it — then repeat *after* grinding down protrusions/bumps with a rotary hammer, especially at wall perimeters and around ventilation shafts. Leftover bumps under the membrane cause the first course of interior-wall blocks to sit at an angle, needing roughly 10x more correction time later. Priming itself is mandatory before floor leveling and before wall waterproofing — unprimed substrate won't bond either layer.
- **Torch-welding safety**: bring at least 2 spare piezo igniters and 2 spare gas canisters to a job (both have high enough failure rates to stall work for hours without backup); **a fire extinguisher must be present on-site any time open-flame torch work is happening** — described as a rule frequently forgotten by both crews and customers. Top seams are torch-welded *in addition to* standard adhesive bonding as a deliberate redundancy measure ("200% instead of the standard 100%").
- **Save membrane offcuts** — used to reinforce/tape penetrations at load-bearing columns and ventilation shafts, and kept even after installation to patch later punctures caused by maintenance crews replacing central heating risers.
- **Screed reinforcement rule**: screed thinner than 6–7cm should be mesh-reinforced to prevent cracking; ≥6–7cm can go without. If the sub-floor is so uneven a thin (~3cm) screed would result, raise the whole apartment's screed level instead (minimum ~4–4.5cm at the thinnest point) if the entry threshold allows it; if it doesn't, use specialized leveling materials in that zone instead of standard screed — even a triple-reinforced 3cm screed will crack.
- **Underfloor heating pipe QC**: the manifold must be permanently fixed at its final designed location *before* pipe runs are laid — repositioning it later disturbs every connected run. Pipes should run parallel to main walls, bend gently (roughly 0.5m radius, no kinks), be wrapped in a continuous protective flex sleeve, and be documented (photographed) precisely enough to be located later without a thermal camera. For anchoring pipe to the sub-floor, one source prefers older bitumen adhesive strips (heat-gun bonded) over newer peel-and-stick strips, describing the newer strips as harder to apply correctly for roughly 5x the cost with no real benefit.
- **Threshold-free flooring is close to a free byproduct of single-contour waterproofing, not a separate cost decision**: since a combined sound+waterproofing membrane applied continuously under the whole floor is already recommended for most apartments, and most customers want floor soundproofing anyway, going fully level (no door-sill steps) across the apartment becomes a natural extension of a decision already made rather than extra scope. `single-account`. ## Rough-Stage Acceptance Checklist Items

- **A dry-trap siphon at the water-inlet node is worth including even on the most basic build** — lets filter-flush water drain automatically instead of needing a bucket; without one, infrequent manual flushing plus standing water in a regular siphon causes sewer odor. **Dishwashers get the same treatment**: a dry-trap siphon under a dishwasher prevents sewer-gas odor the same way — framed as standard practice most homeowners don't know to ask for. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_what_is_this_60m2_contractor_control_f7ab173e.txt`]
- **Deliberately unclamped rough-stage plumbing fittings are not a defect**: crews should leave supply fittings (e.g. at a mixer connection) resting loosely with slack and crimp-sleeve fittings pre-installed but not yet crimped, until tile is laid and the exact grout-line position is known — the fitting is then centered on a tile joint for a clean look. A rough-stage handover showing stubs with sleeves on but fittings not yet compressed is expected, not a red flag.
- **A freestanding-tub installation footprint should be sized only a few millimeters larger than the tub itself**, not tiled out to a wide margin before sliding the tub in — the tile edge should overlap the tub rim so water drains back into the tub rather than behind it.

## Screed Expansion Joints and Two-Layer Floor Soundproofing

`single-account`. See [[11_Budget_and_Planning/Renovation_Sequence|Renovation Sequence]]'s Flooring-Transition Planning section for the wider theme this connects to.

- **Expansion/control joints are required at every wall-to-floor junction and every corner, but only for walls built *after* the screed was poured** — a wall that predates the screed pour doesn't need one at its base. Their absence lets the screed crack over time; verify their presence during screed work, since they can't be added after the fact without cutting into cured screed. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **Two-layer floor soundproofing**: a first branded underlayment layer plus an additional thermal/sound-insulation felt layer on top, applied continuously across the whole floor — including areas later reopened for radiator-niche work (the section is removed and precisely reinstalled afterward, to avoid gaps in coverage). The material's overlapped/lapped edges, later trimmed off, double as an expansion buffer absorbing the screed's thermal expansion/contraction — flagged as a detail that shouldn't be economized away. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
