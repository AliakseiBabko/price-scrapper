# Plumbing — Waterproofing & Plastering

Covers code-level waterproofing requirements, the gypsum-vs-cement plaster tradeoff, and detailed floor-membrane installation/QC technique. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]].

## Waterproofing & Plastering Standards

- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario reports: **A flush (level-with-the-rest-of-the-apartment) bathroom floor's legal status in Russia has reportedly changed over time** — permitted, then prohibited, then permitted again as of recent sources, **conditional specifically on waterproofing the entire apartment's floor, not just the bathroom**. If this condition isn't met and it's discovered, code enforcement can require retrofitting a raised threshold, or in the worst case a full teardown and redo. One source frames it plainly: it's cheaper and easier to just waterproof the whole apartment upfront than to risk this outcome later. **This regulatory history is Russia-specific and its current status in Belarus is unconfirmed** — treat a flush floor as requiring explicit local verification before committing to it.
- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario states: **Roll/membrane waterproofing under the screed, with an upturn onto the walls, is mandatory by code in Russia (per one source) with no exceptions** — it always separates the screed from direct wall/floor-finish contact. After the screed, brush/paint-on ("обмазочная") waterproofing with taped joints at the floor-wall junction is applied, plus the same taped-joint treatment at wall corners.
- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario explains: **Brush-on wall waterproofing is not legally mandatory when using cement-based plaster, but is mandatory when using gypsum-based plaster**, since gypsum plaster must be protected from moisture exposure. One source's own company uses gypsum plaster throughout its bathroom work and therefore brush-waterproofs floor-to-ceiling on every bathroom wall regardless of the cement-plaster exemption.
- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario compares: **Gypsum vs. cement plaster tradeoff**: gypsum is faster to apply (more workable, easier to level), lighter (less structural load on the slab — relevant since tile adds further weight on top), and dries meaningfully faster (cited: cement plaster 21–30 days vs. gypsum plaster 7–14 days). One source cites Knauf as now endorsing gypsum plaster for wet rooms specifically.
- **Add an anti-fungal admixture to ceiling paint applied above a stretch ceiling, apartment-wide, not just in wet rooms** — rough-stage work (screed curing, plastering, even plain painting) generates significant condensate, and a ceiling surface can accumulate enough moisture to grow mold that's invisible once the stretch-ceiling membrane is installed over it. A preventable, easily-skipped step since the mold stays hidden until the ceiling is eventually opened. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **Always wrap the sewage stack in sound-insulating material during any bathroom renovation in a multi-unit building** — corroborated by two sources; one gives concrete product/cost detail (a bitumen-adhesive wrap kit, ~3,000–5,000 RUB, a few hours of DIY-feasible installation). Without it, plastic-stack drain noise from an upstairs flush is described as sounding like "a waterfall behind the wall" — a comfort issue, not a structural one, but a low-cost fix worth including as standard scope. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- Zemstandart/Alexey Zemskov recommends: **Brush-on/paint-applied waterproofing should always be bright blue, never gray** — gray waterproofing is visually indistinguishable from the surrounding gray plaster, making coverage gaps invisible to a non-expert inspecting the work. Bright blue makes any missed patch obvious even without training. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_split_rough_finish_contracting_and_rough_stage_checklist_9a4efa5e.txt`]

## Floor Waterproofing Membrane Installation & QC

Four videos from a "general renovation tips" playlist (same channel, `single-account`) cover rolled bitumen/felt-backed membrane installation and acceptance-inspection in unusual technical depth:

- **Membrane sheet orientation**: rolled sheets have one bare edge (felt backing stripped) meant to overlap the next sheet — this bare edge must face **toward the wall**, not run down the middle of a floor span. Placed backwards, two bare (non-felted) edges end up overlapped against each other mid-floor, creating a long unsealed seam that defeats sound isolation across the whole run. **Checkable defect**: a visible "dip" along the seam — the unsealed section sits thinner and lower than the felted areas around it. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Seam/overlap integrity check**: walk every seam and overlap pocket and gently try to lift the edge with a tool (not a fingernail/putty knife). A pocket that doesn't tear/lift further than a small existing flap is properly bonded; one that tears open (revealing an unglued dry patch) is a real defect. A good single-contour install should, per one source, let several buckets of water poured in one room theoretically spread to every room without reaching the unit below. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Patch technique for a torn seam**: heat the substrate spot and the patch piece *separately* first, then press together once both are hot, then heat-seal the patch's perimeter with a torch. A common shortcut to reject: crews heating only the perimeter and smearing it for looks without properly melting the base contact. Keep patches small — a large patch can't be reheated evenly before it cools, causing poor adhesion. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Never waterproof over the area immediately around a sewage/drain stack, outside wet rooms** — a deliberate gap must be left there so that if an upstairs neighbor floods, water drains straight down through the gap to the unit below rather than pooling and eventually breaching sideways into your own unit. Corroborated across all four sources as a commonly-forgotten, "actively harmful to the owner" mistake when crews seal it out of over-caution. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Waterproofing-height-vs-screed rule, with concrete examples**: membrane must extend above the planned final screed level — cited fold-up heights from one real apartment: 10cm, 11.5cm, 10cm, 12cm across different rooms — trimmed down after the screed is poured and sealed with acoustic sealant at the finished-floor line. One source sets the target screed level with a laser level referenced from the building's own entry-door threshold, so the interior finished floor matches the corridor floor with no step at the front door. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- Zemstandart/Alexey Zemskov explains: **"Single contour" vs. "single bowl" waterproofing**: single-contour (continuous across all rooms, applied *after* interior walls exist, no seams/thresholds between rooms) is preferred over a single-bowl approach (one continuous sheet applied to the whole apartment *before* interior walls go up). The single-bowl-first method is easier to execute and looks impressive on camera, but has two real downsides: a pinhole anywhere in it leaks straight through to the unit below, and membrane installed before walls exist gets reliably punctured multiple times during wall construction. `single-account`, speaker's stated reasoning against a technique he says he used on his own earlier project.
- **Perimeter damper strip**: a foam damper strip must run the full perimeter where waterproofing meets a wall at the pour line, to absorb screed expansion/contraction and prevent cracking — reportedly forgotten by installers "guaranteed." Waterproofing correctly folded up the wall at that seam can itself serve this damper function. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Wet-room walls need full heat-bonding**; elsewhere on the same floor, wall-bonding is more optional — in a bathroom the membrane should be melted enough to become "part of the wall," fully sealed around the entire perimeter including at load-bearing structure. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Two-pass site cleaning before priming**: knock down construction dust with a garden pump sprayer and a long-handled broom, bag it — then repeat *after* grinding down protrusions/bumps with a rotary hammer, especially at wall perimeters and around ventilation shafts. Leftover bumps under the membrane cause the first course of interior-wall blocks to sit at an angle, needing roughly 10x more correction time later. Priming itself is mandatory before floor leveling and before wall waterproofing — unprimed substrate won't bond either layer. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Torch-welding safety**: bring at least 2 spare piezo igniters and 2 spare gas canisters to a job (both have high enough failure rates to stall work for hours without backup); **a fire extinguisher must be present on-site any time open-flame torch work is happening** — described as a rule frequently forgotten by both crews and customers. Top seams are torch-welded *in addition to* standard adhesive bonding as a deliberate redundancy measure ("200% instead of the standard 100%"). [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Save membrane offcuts** — used to reinforce/tape penetrations at load-bearing columns and ventilation shafts, and kept even after installation to patch later punctures caused by maintenance crews replacing central heating risers. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Screed reinforcement rule**: screed thinner than 6–7cm should be mesh-reinforced to prevent cracking; ≥6–7cm can go without. If the sub-floor is so uneven a thin (~3cm) screed would result, raise the whole apartment's screed level instead (minimum ~4–4.5cm at the thinnest point) if the entry threshold allows it; if it doesn't, use specialized leveling materials in that zone instead of standard screed — even a triple-reinforced 3cm screed will crack. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Underfloor heating pipe QC**: the manifold must be permanently fixed at its final designed location *before* pipe runs are laid — repositioning it later disturbs every connected run. Pipes should run parallel to main walls, bend gently (roughly 0.5m radius, no kinks), be wrapped in a continuous protective flex sleeve, and be documented (photographed) precisely enough to be located later without a thermal camera. For anchoring pipe to the sub-floor, one source prefers older bitumen adhesive strips (heat-gun bonded) over newer peel-and-stick strips, describing the newer strips as harder to apply correctly for roughly 5x the cost with no real benefit. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt|20260804_never_take_this]]]
- **Threshold-free flooring is close to a free byproduct of single-contour waterproofing, not a separate cost decision**: since a combined sound+waterproofing membrane applied continuously under the whole floor is already recommended for most apartments, and most customers want floor soundproofing anyway, going fully level (no door-sill steps) across the apartment becomes a natural extension of a decision already made rather than extra scope. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

## Brush-On/Cementitious Waterproofing: Product Selection & Application Technique

Pavel Sidorik (individual finisher, `single-account`) demonstrates a
full brush-on bathroom waterproofing job with two named products,
filling a gap in this page's otherwise roll/membrane-heavy coverage:

- **Product comparison**: Ceresit CR 65 (dry cement-based) is
  non-deformable-substrate-only (concrete, cement plaster/screed),
  **not usable on gypsum substrates**, cheaper, and requires the
  substrate be pre-wetted before application, with tile-lay possible
  only after **7 days**. Ceresit CL 51 (ready-mixed elastic mastic) works
  on **any substrate**, tolerates minor substrate cracking without
  losing its seal, costs more, is interior-only (not rated for pools/
  constant water contact), and allows tiling after only **16 hours** —
  the speaker's own preferred product for that speed advantage.
- **Two named application mistakes with cement-based waterproofing**:
  applying it to an unwetted/dry substrate (correct prep: lightly spray
  the wall with a garden pump sprayer and let it absorb moisture first),
  and applying it to a gypsum-based substrate at all (not rated for it).
- **Waterproof the floor last, not first** — waterproofing the floor
  before the walls forces the installer to walk on the fresh floor
  membrane to reach the remaining walls, damaging it.
- **A real client-driven scope-cutting compromise, named explicitly**:
  correct technique tapes every wall corner (including vertical
  corners) since cracks form there; this source's client asked to skip
  vertical-corner taping to save cost. In a **panel building
  specifically, the vertical joints between wall panels are the single
  most crack-vulnerable point** — flagged as a real compromise, not a
  neutral simplification, even though it was the client's own choice.
- **Corner-taping technique**: brush mastic into the corner, coat the
  tape itself with mastic before pressing it in (an extra-adhesion step
  beyond the product's own minimum instructions), trim excess, then coat
  the entire tape (not just its edges) with mastic afterward.
- **Two-coat, cross-hatched mastic application**: first coat in one
  direction, wait ~1.5 hours, second coat perpendicular to the first —
  the same cross-hatch logic commonly used for tile adhesive, applied
  here to brush-on waterproofing coats.
- **Scope tradeoff**: full floor-to-ceiling waterproofing on every wall
  is the default, but a cost-saving partial-scope alternative exists —
  waterproof only the floor and the walls actually within a fixture's
  splash zone.
- Cement-based waterproofing product includes a red pigment specifically
  so the applied coverage is visually distinguishable from the grey
  substrate, making gaps easy to spot.

## Plaster Substrate Prep, Crack Prevention, and Mesh Reinforcement (added 2026-08-24, Round 3, ep.11)

Pavel Sidorik (individual finisher, `single-account`), 1st of 5 consecutive
plastering episodes this round — region unresolved this episode, no
pricing stated:

- **5 substrate types in one real apartment needing different prep**: AAC
  block partitions (most crack-prone), silicate-brick block walls (visually
  similar to but distinct from AAC), monolithic load-bearing concrete
  (including ventilation-duct surrounds — often problematic, smooth,
  plaster-repellent), partially-retained flat developer plaster, and
  expanded-clay-concrete block with only a thin spatter-bond layer left.
  **Material joints between any two types, and corners generally, are the
  highest crack-risk zones.**
- **Developer plaster needs mechanical resurfacing even when visually
  flat**: crews often skip proper floating/smoothing or over-wet
  already-drying plaster to force a smooth finish ("wet-on-wet"), leaving
  a weak surface skin (gypsum laitance) that later coats bond to
  unreliably. Fix: sand the whole surface with a long-reach ("жираф")
  drywall sander, 60-80 grit vacuum-hole paper, connected to a shop vacuum
  (also stabilizes the sander against the wall). Real time: ~15-25 min/wall.
  **Governing rule: a leveling/skim coat can only go over a *stronger*
  substrate, never the reverse.**
- **Double-priming rule for highly absorbent substrates (AAC specifically)**:
  first primer pass diluted 1:1 with water, second pass (after full dry,
  ~2hr) undiluted — reduces over-absorbency, since plaster losing moisture
  too fast to an over-thirsty substrate is a direct crack-formation
  mechanism.
- **Chase-filling myth, explicitly corrected**: "plaster will just cover
  the cable chase" is wrong — plaster doesn't reliably achieve full depth
  coverage everywhere, and an unfilled chase risks a visible surface
  groove later as thicker plaster there settles differently. Chases must
  always be pre-filled before plastering.
- **Mesh reinforcement — mandatory for AAC, strictly-beneficial-but-optional
  for silicate/expanded-clay-concrete/brick**: alkali-resistant fiberglass
  facade mesh (160g/m², 5×5mm cell). Not a guarantee against cracking (a
  serious settlement event can still tear it) but the only technique that
  meaningfully resists ordinary crack formation. **Two application
  methods**: (1) classic — adhesive (named: Ceresit CT 85, micro-fiber
  reinforced) dabbed and leveled, mesh pressed in, second coat over it,
  10cm overlaps/corner-returns; (2) embedded directly in the plaster body
  — mesh temporarily screwed to the wall before beacons go up, folded
  aside during plastering, folded back and pressed into the wet plaster
  coat, then covered with a second coat — ends up inside the plaster mass
  rather than under it.
- **Three-tier joint/crack-control technique at dissimilar-material seams**:
  (1) fill/foam the gap, then adhesive + mesh + second coat; (2) same, plus
  a drywall-profile foam sealing tape laid over the seam first — a crack
  that eventually forms stays trapped under the tape instead of surfacing;
  (3) same mechanism as (2) but with a stretchable composite tape (named:
  Straight Flex) instead of the profile tape.
- **Monolithic-concrete prep for gypsum plaster**: grind with an angle
  grinder + diamond cup wheel to remove cement laitance and open surface
  pores (laitance is the weak link a concrete-bonding primer can peel away
  with it if not removed first), prime, foam-fill duct-penetration gaps,
  trim, mesh-reinforce the seam.
- **Concrete-bonding primer ("бетон-контакт") technique, and the real
  bonding mechanism**: named product Ceresit CT 19 must be applied in a
  deliberately thick coat that dries with a nap/"fur" texture, not rolled
  flat — rolling it too thin leaves too little exposed quartz sand, and
  **quartz sand, not the acrylic binder, is what actually anchors the
  subsequent gypsum plaster** (the acrylic is only a binder for the primer
  layer itself, not the bonding mechanism to the next coat). Dry time ~3hr.
  [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_K-OopxgJ8wY_sidorik_crack_prevention_ep11|YT_K-OopxgJ8wY]]]

## Beacon (Lighthouse) Installation via Laser Level, and 90° Corner Technique (added 2026-08-24, Round 3, ep.12)

Pavel Sidorik, same project, 2nd of 5 consecutive plastering episodes —
Belarus level 1 (a Belarusian retailer, "Постройка.бай," named directly as
this project's own pricing source, though no figures were spoken aloud in
this episode to extract):

- **Laser-level beacon-setting sequence**: find the wall's true plane by
  moving the laser toward the wall until the beam just starts to
  disappear/clip (reveals the flattest achievable plane and every bump);
  target minimum plaster thickness ≈1.5cm including the beacon; mark 11cm
  out from the laser line at each end (10cm = straightedge thickness, +1cm
  = beacon+plaster allowance), then re-aim the laser onto these new marks
  before dabbing plaster and setting beacons (aligned half-beam-on-beacon,
  half-showing-through).
- **Install end beacons first, cross-check middle beacons against a
  straightedge spanning both ends, not against the laser individually** —
  the laser carries its own margin of error; a physical straightedge
  cross-check between two already-set beacons catches drift the laser
  alone would miss. No-laser fallback: string line stretched between end
  beacons, middle beacons aligned to the string.
- **Straightedge-tilt self-check via the laser beam itself**: perpendicular
  hold keeps the beam visible/centered on the straightedge; tilting makes
  the beam visibly disappear — a built-in check with no extra tool needed.
- **Beacon spacing**: ~1.2m max for a beginner; an experienced installer
  can go ~1.5x wider, at the risk of a trapezoidal screed straightedge
  bowing between beacons and over-scooping plaster.
- **90°-corner-alignment technique using the same laser**: mark a matching
  offset (e.g. 10cm) on both walls at a corner with a carpenter's square;
  position the laser so one plane extends off the *longer* wall while the
  other sits against the perpendicular wall — this projects the long
  wall's plane onto the corner and immediately reveals (via a measured
  plaster-thickness difference at each side) whether the second wall
  deviates from true 90°, which beacon placement then corrects. The longer
  wall is used as the default reference plane (needs less added material).
  Real locations needing verified 90° in this project: built-in-wardrobe
  walls, bathroom, kitchen-corridor corner.
- **Tie-in technique at a retained-developer-plaster boundary**: one
  beacon rests on a straightedge spanning from the existing flat plane so
  new plaster ties in flush rather than stepping.
- Re-patch beacons after final trim, ideally once the setting plaster has
  fully hardened, to prevent sagging under later straightedge pressure.
  [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_bF1I2olZSc0_sidorik_beacons_ep12|YT_bF1I2olZSc0]]]

## Ten Plastering-Workflow Tricks: Chases, Mixing, Trim Timing, Corners (added 2026-08-24, Round 3, ep.13)

Pavel Sidorik, same project, 3rd of 5 consecutive plastering episodes —
Belarus level 1 again (Постройка.бай named as pricing source, no figures
spoken this episode either — this pass's estimate explicitly excludes
beacon cost, priced separately in ep.12's estimate):

- **Chase-filling-in-finished-plaster, correct sequencing** (a different
  scenario from ep.11's pre-plaster chase rule): filling a chase flush
  *immediately* causes it to draw in/shrink into a groove invisible until
  raking light after painting; correct technique overfills with a slight
  bump, waits ~40 min until firm-but-still-cuttable, then trims flush —
  at that hardness it won't sink further.
- **Digital cable-location documentation**: photograph the wall, then use
  a photo-annotation measuring app (named: Image Metr) to place dimension
  callouts directly on the photo, optionally auto-populated via a
  Bluetooth laser distance meter — a real upgrade over hand-written notes.
- **Trim (подрезка) timing and its diagnostic role**: check with a
  fingertip ~40-50 min after application (ready when firm but still
  slightly yielding); scrape against the beacons with the straightedge
  repeatedly until the straightedge stops removing material — that's the
  actual signal the plane is flat, not a fixed time or visual guess.
  Beacons are single-use — pulled beacons bend on removal and can't be
  reliably reused.
- **Inside-corner sequencing**: plaster the two walls at an inside corner
  on separate days (parallel walls first, perpendicular the next day) so
  one side is already flat/hard when forming the corner; sharpen/trim
  with a plain flat spatula, not a specialized corner trowel (worse
  result, in this practitioner's own experience).
- **Outside-corner technique with a temporary reference rail**: find the
  wall's highest point (laser or bubble level) as the reference plane;
  verify true 90° there with a carpenter's square (a laser's own angle to
  the wall may not itself be exactly 90°); temporarily fasten a
  straightedge/rail to the adjacent wall at that verified reference
  (screwed with oversized holes for fine adjustment, or set with a
  fast-setting plaster mix); plaster the corner using the rail and the
  flat wall as the two references, screeding with a carpenter's square
  rather than a straightedge; trim once set and remove the rail.
- **Adhesion-layer trick**: trowel a thin bonding coat of the same
  plaster onto tricky substrates before throwing dabs — dabs thrown
  directly sometimes fail to bond or trap air pockets.
- **Half-strip technique for lower physical effort**: throw and level
  only half a plaster strip at a time, then throw the second half onto
  the now-leveled first half (easier to pull against an established edge)
  — roughly halves per-strip pulling effort, useful for a beginner or a
  physically lighter worker.
- **Solo-worker high-reach mixing trick**: carry the empty mixing bucket
  and a dry bag of plaster up to platform height separately rather than
  lifting a fully mixed >50kg bucket — mix directly on the platform
  instead. A two-paddle mixer attachment mixes faster/better than a
  single-paddle one (this practitioner's own comparison); rinse the
  paddle immediately after each batch or dried plaster later needs
  hammering off.
- **Speaker's own stated opinion (`single-account`): skips the traditional
  float/polish finish step entirely on his own projects**, reasoning that
  it takes as long as puttying anyway, is often rushed into a
  delamination-prone "wet-on-wet" condition, and still leaves raking-
  light-visible "orange peel" texture needing putty regardless — his
  stated philosophy is that plastering's job is to deliver a flat plane,
  smoothness is putty's job. He machine-sands set plaster instead
  (120-grit, ~10 min/wall) for what he subjectively judges a flatter
  result, while explicitly noting many practitioners float successfully
  and get good results the traditional way.
  [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_Q70kwfR0LGs_sidorik_plaster_tricks_ep13|YT_Q70kwfR0LGs]]]

## Thick-Layer Ceiling Plaster Without Full Metal Mesh (added 2026-08-24, Round 3, ep.14)

Pavel Sidorik, same project, 4th of 5 consecutive plastering episodes —
Belarus level 1 (direct statement that avoiding ceiling-height loss is
"very relevant for Belarus" given common 255cm monolithic-building
ceilings; stilts priced in BYN):

- **Why plaster a ceiling rather than drop it with drywall/stretch**:
  doesn't consume height — directly relevant given common 255cm ceilings
  in Belarusian monolithic buildings (dropping even a minimum ~7cm for
  drywall/stretch plus floor buildup leaves ≤245cm); also flatter than
  drywall/stretch and cheaper than a drywall ceiling.
- **Code baseline and the problem**: code and most plaster manufacturers
  cap unsupported gypsum plaster at 15mm on a ceiling; beyond that, code
  calls for a full galvanized-mesh screw/shoot-on installation before
  plastering. **Mechanism reason this matters**: gypsum plaster doesn't
  chemically bond to bare concrete — it holds on mechanical roughness
  alone, so a thick unsupported layer needs artificial anchor points.
- **Three-part alternative devised in place of full mesh**: (1) galvanized
  suspension-hanger strips shot to the ceiling with a powder-actuated
  tool (~10/m²) then bent into "butterfly" anchors; (2) a latex bonding
  additive (named: Ceresit CT81) mixed into a thick cement/sand/water
  slurry and brushed on (a masonry brush outperformed a texture sprayer
  or trowel in this practitioner's own testing) to build a rough bonding
  "fur coat," cured overnight into a bond noticeably stronger under
  scraping than tile adhesive; (3) polypropylene fiber mixed directly into
  the plaster (30g per 30kg bag) to bind the plaster mass together.
  **Post-cure validation**: the whole finished ceiling was tapped and
  showed no delamination anywhere.
- **Ceiling beacon-setting is direction-inverted vs. floor/wall beacons**:
  a laser on an extendable pole projects the reference plane near the
  ceiling; a height-adjustable dowel-nail anchor plus a carpenter's
  square establishes the target mark, and each beacon is pushed **up**
  to that mark (a floor/wall beacon is instead pressed *down*).
- **Perimeter damper-tape first-time trial**: drywall-profile foam sealing
  tape applied around the ceiling perimeter as an expansion buffer between
  plaster and wall — real downside found (adhesive residue needing extra
  sanding at trim time), with a planned fix (switch to thin 3mm dedicated
  screed-perimeter tape, stapled not glued) but a net-positive verdict —
  cleaner corner than without tape.
- **Ceiling vs. wall plastering technique differences**: a bonding coat is
  essential (not optional) before throwing plaster overhead; the upward
  throwing motion is the physically hardest part for someone unaccustomed
  to it, but ceiling plaster doesn't sag/slide the way wall plaster does,
  so trim work afterward is minimal. Real productivity data point: one
  ~14m² room's ceiling took a full workday for two people.
- Mid-project brand substitution (Волма/Volma → Rotband gypsum plaster,
  used identically) — a real substitution data point, not a quality
  complaint. Stilts purchase (named: Stiles.by) **320 BYN (≈$120,
  trailing-6-month average to 2021-03-06)**.

## Bathroom Plastering: Substrate-Prep Methods for Wet Rooms (added 2026-08-24, Round 3, ep.15)

Bathroom finish work (tile + labor) is typically the single most expensive finish stage in an apartment renovation, so a later plaster/tile delamination failure is correspondingly the costliest mistake to redo — bathroom walls get extra substrate-prep scrutiny beyond ordinary rooms, plus verified 90° corner geometry for built-in fixture fit.

- **90°-corner technique for a bathroom, extending the ep.12 general method, specifically solving for equal door-reveal length**: requires a laser level with two mutually perpendicular 360° beams. Mark equal offset distances (e.g. 7cm, arbitrary but must match) on both sides of the door-opening wall from the corner; place the laser at those marks so its beam matches both, capturing the exterior wall's already-plastered level plane. Switch to the laser's perpendicular beam, mark points along it, then move the laser inside and re-set it against the interior wall, offset from the highest point by the beacon's own thickness (6mm in this job) — keeps the interior reference plane strictly parallel to the exterior one, which is what guarantees the future door's reveal ("откосы") comes out equal length on both sides once installed.
- **Beacon-prep, corroborating and extending ep.12/13**: cut beacons (bought in 3m stock) with an angle grinder + metal-cutting disc, never metal shears (shears crimp/deform the profile); trim ~2cm off factory ends since they're often bent/damaged in shipping; length = ceiling height minus 1cm. Eye protection required when grinding.
- **Beacon-setting adhesive, a product-driven technique change**: sets beacons using the same cement-sand wall plaster itself (named: Holcim) rather than gypsum plaster — this specific product's stickiness is now close enough to gypsum that he's stopped reaching for gypsum plaster just for beacon-setting.
- **Core substrate-prep problem**: most cement-sand plasters (unlike gypsum) aren't inherently sticky and risk delaminating on smooth block substrates specifically — though some modern polymer-modified cement-sand plasters have enough plasticity/stickiness to behave closer to gypsum. Speaker still preps the substrate regardless of the specific product's stated quality, as insurance against the high cost of a later tile-plaster delamination failure. **Four substrate-prep methods, in order of complexity**:
  1. **Bonding coat of the same plaster** — notched-trowel a thin skim coat of the same cement-sand plaster onto the wall first, then throw dabs onto that pre-coated area; works especially well paired with an already-sticky (polymer-rich) plaster product.
  2. **Tile adhesive as bonding layer** — named product Ceresit CM16 (acknowledged as possibly overkill; any C2-class tile adhesive works) combed on horizontally with a notched trowel, left to cure a full 24 hours into a hard, textured substrate that grips smooth block far better than bare block. **Sequencing caution**: plaster only opposite/parallel walls in one working pass, not two adjacent walls — working a fresh corner between two just-plastered adjacent walls risks damaging the corner edge with the straightedge; do adjacent walls on separate days instead.
  3. **Dedicated bonding additive, spatter-coat texture** — named additive Ceresit CC81 mixed per its own package ratio table into a cement/sand/water slurry, brushed on as a rough spatter-coat texture onto a lightly dampened (not wet) substrate; cures ~24h before plaster goes on; produces a substrate stated as "already good for tiling" even before the plaster coat.
  4. **Same bonding additive, wet-on-wet application** — same CC81 slurry as method 3, but applied as a continuous coat and plastered *immediately* while still wet — a technique normally used for bonding thin-layer screeds/pours, applied here to wall plaster instead. Reports a strong, well-adhered result: "the additive literally glues the plaster to the substrate."
- **Cement-sand plaster consistency check**: draw a trowel through the mixed plaster — correct consistency holds the groove shape without flowing back/slumping.
- **Named hopper-gun product and real working notes**: "Рик Сталь," a Belarusian (Gomel-manufactured), stainless-steel hopper gun, described as ideal specifically for cement-sand plaster (also usable for gypsum). Needs only a modest air compressor (his: 50L tank sufficient). Lets a genuinely untrained helper apply plaster competently on a first attempt — demonstrated live with his assistant successfully using it the first time. Markedly faster than hand-throwing plaster.
- **Cement-sand plaster cure/trim timing, contrasted directly with gypsum plaster**: trim after ~5 hours of set time — far more forgiving than gypsum's ~40-50 minute window (ep.13); can even be left overnight and trimmed the next morning while still workable. Stated directly as genuinely easier to work with in this respect than gypsum plaster.
- **Deliberate wet-room chase-fill choice**: fills every beacon-channel chase flush in the bathroom specifically (rather than leaving it, an option a non-wet-room wall might tolerate) because the later waterproofing membrane needs a continuous flat substrate to bond to correctly.

No pricing figures spoken in this episode. ## Screed Expansion Joints and Two-Layer Floor Soundproofing

`single-account`. See [[11_Budget_and_Planning/Renovation_Sequence|Renovation Sequence]]'s Flooring-Transition Planning section for the wider theme this connects to.

- **Expansion/control joints are required at every wall-to-floor junction and every corner, but only for walls built *after* the screed was poured** — a wall that predates the screed pour doesn't need one at its base. Their absence lets the screed crack over time; verify their presence during screed work, since they can't be added after the fact without cutting into cured screed. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_never_take_this_from_masters_70m2_4b3c72b6.txt`]
- **Two-layer floor soundproofing**: a first branded underlayment layer plus an additional thermal/sound-insulation felt layer on top, applied continuously across the whole floor — including areas later reopened for radiator-niche work (the section is removed and precisely reinstalled afterward, to avoid gaps in coverage). The material's overlapped/lapped edges, later trimmed off, double as an expansion buffer absorbing the screed's thermal expansion/contraction — flagged as a detail that shouldn't be economized away. ## Primer (Грунтовка) Selection & Application — General Guide (added 2026-08-24, Round 6)

Pavel Sidorik, standalone general-technique FAQ, not part of the numbered
project series. Region: unresolved (Belarus/Russia mentioned only as
primer-manufacturing plant locations, a product-market fact, not this
speaker's own project location). `single-account`.

- **Core mechanism**: primer is the bonding layer between a substrate and
  whatever is applied next (plaster, tile adhesive, self-leveling
  compound) — it binds surface dust, reduces substrate absorbency, and
  promotes even distribution and longer service life of the material
  applied over it via adhesion. Demonstrated on camera: brick given 2
  coats of deep-penetration primer (Ceresit CT17) showed dramatically
  reduced water absorption vs. an untreated control brick.
- **Absorbency-tier framework**: substrates are highly-absorbent /
  medium-absorbent / low-absorbent / non-absorbent; manufacturers print a
  substrate-specific application table on the container (a porous
  substrate gets a diluted first coat, then a full-concentration coat).
  **Over-application risk on non-absorbent substrates** (e.g. bare
  monolithic concrete): repeated coats can build a continuous
  plastic-like film that can delaminate together with whatever is applied
  over it — "more is not better," follow the manufacturer's specified
  coat count exactly. This generalizes the double-priming rule already
  above (AAC-specific, highly-absorbent case) to the opposite,
  non-absorbent end of the same spectrum.
- **Three application methods, ranked by speed and tradeoffs**:
  (1) spray/airless ("безвоздушка") — fastest, most productive, suited to
  all work types, but requires masking anything that could be damaged by
  overspray; (2) powered sprayer/gun ("краскопульт") — second-fastest,
  same masking downside; (3) roller — the practical default for many
  jobs since nothing needs masking (long-nap for textured surfaces,
  short-nap velour for a smooth finish-coat substrate like putty before
  painting); **for floors specifically, roller is the recommended
  method** even though spray is faster on walls; (4) brush ("маклoвица")
  — slowest, small areas only, **must not be used on finish-coat putty
  before painting** (stiff bristles visibly scratch the putty surface).
  Universal control point: watch for drips/running streaks and roll them
  flat immediately if they occur.
- **Re-priming between coats rule**: only needed if dust-generating work
  (sanding, scraping) was done on the prior coat — skip the intermediate
  priming step if no dusty work occurred between coats.
- **"Will plaster/tile adhesive detach along with the primer?" — resolved
  as a non-issue**: primer's polymer concentration is engineered so its
  adhesion matches or exceeds the material applied over it; manufacturers
  of cement-based plasters/adhesives deliberately *limit* that material's
  own adhesion strength ("защита от дураков") since an overly strong bond
  can tear itself off a weak substrate (e.g. aerated concrete) as it
  shrinks while curing. Primer doesn't form one continuous film either —
  it follows the substrate's microscopic irregularities, binding dust and
  making the substrate itself more "tacky," rather than sealing it. With
  cement-based material on a cement-based substrate specifically, the
  cement crystals bond directly to the substrate, not to the primer
  layer.
- **PVA glue is not a valid primer substitute** — primer is an
  engineered, specifically-formulated polymer blend; PVA glue is
  ~90-95% polyvinyl acetate, different chemistry. A home-mixed substitute
  shifts all liability for a resulting failure onto the person making the
  substitution.
- **PPE by application method**: brush/roller application doesn't
  strictly require a respirator (though primer has a noticeable smell —
  the speaker wears one anyway as habit); **spray application does
  require a respirator**, since airborne concentration builds up and is
  inhaled, plus a coverall to protect clothing.
- **Why one manufacturer sells many near-identical SKUs**: different
  national manufacturing plants (Belarus, Russia, possibly
  Ukraine/elsewhere in Europe) with their own packaging design; different
  concentration formats (ready-to-use vs. concentrate — the Belarus
  market specifically offers 1:2 and 1:3 concentrate ratios); ordinary
  marketing/packaging differentiation. Underlying product quality is
  presented as essentially equivalent across variants.
- **Three pricing drivers**: (1) quantity/quality of active polymer
  ingredients, (2) testing/certification the manufacturer performs, (3)
  warranty/guarantee terms offered — the cheapest primer from a reputable
  manufacturer can perform equivalently for a given job.
- **No single truly universal primer exists**, but deep-penetration
  primer is the closest, covering the widest range of tasks.
