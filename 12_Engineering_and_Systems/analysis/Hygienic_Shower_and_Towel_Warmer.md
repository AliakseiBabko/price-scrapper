# Plumbing — Hygienic Shower & Towel Warmer

Covers a specific, preventable hygienic-shower failure mode, and the case for electric over hydronic towel warmers. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]].

**USD normalization for the dated towel-warmer comparison:** using the 2025-04-04 source date and trailing 6-month USD/RUB average of 95.97, 10,000–15,000 RUB hydronic installation is ≈$100–$160, ~2,500 RUB electric installation is ≈$30, and 30,000–50,000 RUB for a dedicated leak-protection branch is ≈$310–$520. The 10–15 RUB/month electricity figure is below $1/month at the same rate. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_video_s1cWUR4l90w_8a6aadd3.txt|20260731_video_s1cWUR4l9]]]

## Hygienic Shower / Bidet Spray — a Specific, Preventable Failure Mode

- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario warns: **The failure mode, stated precisely**: a user opens the hygienic-shower/bidet supply valve, uses the spray, releases the trigger (which stops water at the nozzle) — but forgets to close the *upstream* shutoff valve. The hose and mixer then sit under full, sustained line pressure indefinitely, well beyond what they're rated to hold continuously; left this way for an extended period (the example given is a vacation), the source states it will "100%" eventually fail and flood the unit and likely the neighbors below.
- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario states: **Correct usage sequence, stated explicitly by the source**: open the shutoff valve → spray → release the trigger → close the shutoff valve → also bleed/release the residual pressure trapped in the hose.
- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario recommends: **A "vent-type" (anti-forgetfulness) hygienic-shower valve design is recommended as the practical fix for apartments**: it's physically impossible to hang the spray nozzle back on its wall holder unless the water-supply valve has been closed first, forcing the correct shutoff sequence through the fixture's own mechanical design rather than relying on the user remembering.

## Towel-Warmer Sustained-Run Acceptance Test (Петришин-Строй, added 2026-08-24, Round 2)

Region level 2 (channel-level Moscow association only, no city named this
episode): **a specific false-negative test-timing failure mode at the
hydrostatic pressure-test/acceptance stage** — don't just confirm the
towel warmer heats at initial system startup; let it run a full 1-3 hours
before judging it working. A unit can heat normally right when the system
first pressurizes, but cool back down within 1-3 hours if there's an
underlying insufficient-pressure, poor-circulation, or placement problem
— a short initial check alone will miss this and pass a defective
installation that only reveals itself later. [source: [[_Sources/YT_OgIZhrxD4v4_petrishin_plumbing_quality_checklist|OgIZhrxD4v4]]]

## Towel Warmer: Water (Hydronic) vs. Electric

**The case for electric as the default isn't stylistic — it's a specific, safety-driven cost/risk comparison:** [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_3_e759e2c7.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]

- Konstantin Kruglov/Ontario and Zemstandart/Alexey Zemskov compare: **Availability**: electric works in any room and is largely independent of the building's own hot-water schedule — power outages are argued to be both rarer and shorter (roughly 1–2 hours/year outside genuine emergencies) than scheduled hot-water outages (commonly 10+ days/year, sometimes a full summer in some regions — see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Water_Heaters|Water Heaters]]).
- Zemstandart/Alexey Zemskov explains: **A hydronic towel warmer typically sits downstream of the leak-protection system's own shutoff valve, so a leak there isn't caught by that system at all** — covering it properly requires installing a second, fully separate leak-protection branch (its own valve, sensor, and wiring) specifically for that one fixture, adding a cited ~45,000 RUB or more in hardware alone, plus roughly 6 additional pressurized joints to the overall system.
- **Independent corroboration of this coverage gap, framed as a simpler fix (added 2026-08-24, Round 5)**: Pavel Sidorik makes the same observation — a common mistake is installing leak-protection valves on the main hot/cold risers but skipping the towel-warmer branch, calling an unprotected hydronic towel warmer "a delayed-action mine." His stated remediation is simpler than the second-branch approach above: just extend the existing leak-protection system's valve coverage to the towel-warmer branch itself, rather than building a fully separate second branch — treat these as two sources describing the same underlying coverage gap with different remediation framing, not a contradiction (a second full branch is more thorough; extending existing valve coverage is the minimum fix). A related mistake from the same source: soldering a bypass into the towel-warmer's jumper/loop in a way that lets water take the path of least resistance around (rather than through) the unit — defeats its purpose even though the loop still technically carries flow. [source: [[_Sources/YT_Cj2U_wVlG-I_sidorik_plumbing_intro_node_errors|Cj2U_wVlG-I_sidorik_plumbing_intro_node_errors]]]
- **A hydronic towel warmer is permanently pressurized and circulating**, tied into a dedicated riser or tapped into the hot-water stack — it can lose heat output if line pressure drops or if it sits too far from the riser, and bleeding trapped air (a Maevsky valve) doesn't reliably fix this. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_3_e759e2c7.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **A specific, notable failure mode: stray/galvanic current corrosion from another unit's wiring fault.** If a neighbor anywhere on the shared stack incorrectly grounds an appliance (the example given is a washing machine) to the towel-warmer riser/stack, stray electrical current can flow through the water inside your own pipes, causing electrochemical corrosion that thins the pipe wall from the inside — eventually producing a pinhole leak (cited as growing to ~3mm) that floods the downstairs neighbor, caused entirely by a fault in someone else's apartment and undetectable/unpreventable from your own unit. The source states their company has witnessed this happen directly, and frames not installing a hydronic towel warmer at all as the only real defense against this specific risk. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_3_e759e2c7.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **A real-world corroboration of this stance, from a second source**: the presenter reports their own building's management company is now going door-to-door offering to disconnect residents' hydronic towel warmers *for free* and helping arrange electric replacements — described as a direct response to recurring leaks/incidents across the building, framed as cheaper for the management company than continuing to handle failures. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_video_6lacLnqpJbM_4b68d812.txt|20260731_video_6lacLnqpJ]]]
- **Cost comparison, concrete numbers**: installing a hydronic towel warmer costs roughly **10,000–15,000 RUB**, since it requires the management company's own plumbers to weld shutoff valves into the riser (a scheduled, supervised job) — versus roughly **~2,500 RUB** to install an electric unit. Electric running cost is cited at up to ~100W while actively heating, cycling on and off to maintain temperature, working out to roughly **10–15 RUB/month** in electricity — trivial next to the potential cost of a hydronic leak (a dedicated second leak-protection branch alone runs 30,000–50,000 RUB) or a flooded neighbor's repair bill. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_video_s1cWUR4l90w_8a6aadd3.txt|20260731_video_s1cWUR4l9]]]
- **A distinct, non-leak failure mode worth knowing if a hydronic unit is used anyway**: a hydronic towel warmer typically has a bypass; if central supply pressure drops (e.g., during heavy simultaneous building-wide demand), the bypass may stop "pushing through" properly and the unit simply stops heating — nothing leaks, nothing looks wrong, it just goes cold. **If installing a hydronic towel warmer despite the above, place it as close to the riser as possible with as few pipe angles/turns as possible** — this reduces exposure to the pressure-drop failure mode, though it doesn't address the leak-protection-coverage or stray-current risks above. An electric towel warmer has no equivalent placement constraint — it can go anywhere, including the ceiling. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_video_6lacLnqpJbM_4b68d812.txt|20260731_video_6lacLnqpJ]]]
- **A rigorous electric-running-cost calculation from a different source, materially higher than the figure above — flagged as an unresolved disagreement, not reconciled (added 2026-08-24, Round 5)**: Pavel Sidorik computes a 1m × 50cm electric unit's average draw as 225W (300W max × 75% typical duty), giving 5.4 kWh/day, 1,971 kWh/year, and — at the Belarusian electricity tariff — **350 BYN/year ≈ $138/year ($1,380 over 10 years, the source's own directly-stated figure, not a converted approximation)**. This is roughly 9-14x higher than this page's existing ~10-15 RUB/month (≈$10-15/year) figure above, sourced from a different, apparently smaller/lower-duty unit cited at ~100W max with on/off cycling. **Neither figure is discarded** — record both, since possible reconciling factors (unit size/wattage, duty-cycle assumptions) aren't confirmed in either source, and per this project's price-comparability rule a gap this size shouldn't be silently averaged or resolved in favor of one source. [source: [[_Sources/YT_-JlpzE6aHiU_sidorik_towel_warmer_ep25|-JlpzE6aHiU_sidorik_towel_warmer_ep25]]]
- **A named hydronic product and sizing rule (same source)**: Sunerzha "Сирокко," a ladder-style bottom-connection unit in food-grade AISI 304L stainless steel with an internal "Polimer protect" anti-corrosion coating. Size to the room with margin; power-sizing rule: ~40-50 W per m² of room area.
- **A real cost comparison on this specific project, in USD already (no conversion needed)**: hydronic towel-warmer installation including chase-cutting cost **$70**; the speaker's own estimate for an electric equivalent's installation: **~$35**. From a reputable brand, hydronic and electric units of comparable quality otherwise cost about the same — cost varies far more by model/design quality than by type; a cheap "industrial minimalist" hydronic radiator can be found discarded for free near new-building construction sites, while a premium seamless-steel design radiator costs meaningfully more.
- **Lifespan and control comparison**: a hydronic unit lasts at least 10, often 20 years (units installed 10-15 years earlier by this practitioner are still working); electric lifespan is less certain, since the heating element will likely need eventual replacement. Electric is more convenient to operate day-to-day (adjustable power, shutoff timer); a classic hydronic unit has no built-in temperature control, though adding shutoff/control valves lets its temperature be regulated too.
- **A concrete mitigation for the stray-current corrosion risk documented above: grounding.** Mandatory grounding for a hydronic towel warmer directly addresses the electrochemical-corrosion risk from stray currents. Cable spec used: 4mm² multi-strand (code calls for 6mm², but it didn't physically fit under the decorative caps); the developer's original grounding wire was only 2.5mm². Connects the towel warmer and the hot/cold risers via КТР connectors.
- **Decision heuristic**: new building, sound new pipes, real grounding, thick walls that chase easily → hydronic recommended (more economical long-run). Old khrushchevka, thin walls, old rusty pipes, high leak risk, no grounding → electric recommended, since "it's not the time to economize."
- **The original case for a towel rail at all stands independently of type**: it genuinely helps post-shower humidity evacuate via ventilation, and skipping a bathroom heat source entirely does invite mold risk over time — this only argues for *which type*, not against having one. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_3_e759e2c7.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **Pre-order and dry-fit towel warmers before tiling, not after** — towel warmers have handedness (left/right water connection); without pre-ordering/dry-fitting the actual unit against the wall, installers cut the connection opening in the wrong spot, forcing a full tile tear-out and re-tile. The same applies to mixers, which have model-specific hole spacing. `single-account`, `confirmed`. [source: 20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt](../../_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt)
- **Vertical radiators are an increasingly common alternative to standard horizontal ones** for three stated reasons: aesthetics, higher heat output for a given wall footprint, and fitting spaces too narrow for a standard horizontal radiator. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]
- **A third alternative beyond the hydronic-vs-electric-rail comparison above: electric underfloor-heating cable mounted vertically on a wall, finished with tile, as a heated-wall towel-warmer substitute.** A real jobsite example: two bathroom wall sections get electric floor-heating cable mounted on the wall (not the floor) and finished with porcelain tile over it, functioning as a heated wall/towel-hanging surface — the room's actual floor separately gets its own, standard electric underfloor heating. `single-account`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_gKBzDEllg4M_kruglov_best_wiring_a_to_z|gKBzDEllg4M_kruglov_best_wiring_a_to_z]]]

**⚠️ Heated wall vs. electric towel warmer — a quantified tradeoff (Round 14 triage, added 2026-08-28), extending the heated-wall concept above with cost/time figures not previously recorded**: a heated wall costs roughly **3-4× more to install** than an electric towel warmer, and roughly **3-4× more to run**, because a towel warmer can simply be run on a timer for a few hours in the evening while a heated wall needs **2-3 hours just to reach target temperature** (heat must first warm the tile adhesive, then the wall substrate, then the porcelain finish tile itself). Achieving "warm robe on waking" with a heated wall means either leaving it on 24/7 or timing a start ~3 hours ahead — both meaningfully raise running cost versus a fast-heating towel warmer. Framed as a real cost-vs-aesthetic tradeoff, not an argument against the heated-wall concept itself. Konstantin Kruglov / Ontario, `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_86fmWWVXark_kruglov_stop_using_trends|YT_86fmWWVXark]]]
- **Further corroboration from a third-party practitioner (Петришин-Строй, added 2026-08-24, Round 11)**: states plainly that the company is increasingly avoiding recommending water-based (hydronic) towel warmers in favor of electric — matches this page's already-established electric-over-hydronic case, not a new mechanism. [source: [[_Sources/YT_QginFVl00Hw_petrishin_nonstandard_bathroom_panoramic_vidnoe|YT_QginFVl00Hw]]]

## Recessing an Exposed Towel-Warmer Riser and Transit Pipe (ДЕЛАТЬ НЕ ПЕРЕДЕЛАТЬ, added 2026-08-28, Round 7)

Vladimir Amelchenko addresses a common client complaint distinct from the hydronic-vs-electric choice above: a steel towel-warmer riser (змеевик) and transit hot-water line standing proud of the wall, blocking flush tile installation.

- **⚠️ Named recessing technique**: chase the wall to create a cavity, cut the existing riser stack, weld or press-fit (опрессовка) a sleeve/coupling at the cut point (access to this joint is required regardless of method), then route the towel-warmer branch and riser continuation fully inside the wall cavity.
- **⚠️ Transit-pipe rerouting where full wall-recessing isn't possible**: route the pipe up into a drop/stretch ceiling void instead, running it along the ceiling and back down behind the toilet — trading a wall chase for ceiling void space.
- **⚠️ Named cost breakdown**: ≈15,000 RUB (≈$200) welder/pipefitter labor + ≈15,000 RUB (≈$200) consumables/demolition = ≈30,000 RUB (≈$400) total.
- **Inferior fallback, explicitly discouraged**: a furniture-style box (мебельный короб) around exposed pipes is possible but typically looks poor compared to fully recessing the pipe.

## ⚠️ One-Point vs Two-Point Hygienic Showers — a Planning Decision (Татьяна Михайловская, added 2026-09-01)

**The hygienic shower replaces a bidet and is far more common in contemporary flats and houses.** [source: [[_Sources/YT_yqb7N1yA9hg_mikhailovskaya_wc_basin_mixers_storage|yqb7N1yA9hg]]]

- **Two configurations: ONE-POINT** (holder, water supply and mixer at a single point) **and TWO-POINT** (mixer and holder in separate places).
- **⚠️ Which one must be decided at PLANNING stage, because a two-point unit needs materially more wall space.**
  - **Her worked case: 850 mm available beside the WC where 900 would be preferable, with a hygienic shower needed to the right — "целесообразнее выбрать гигиенический душ на одну точку, так как для его монтажа требуется меньше места."**
- The mixer can be offset up or down from the holder, **but consider whether that is actually usable** — normally they sit in one line, and offsetting is *"чревато неудобными моментами и неправильным монтажом."*
- **⚠️ A brief-stage question almost nobody asks: the hose type. Some are corrugated metal tube, some rubber cord. Neither is better — "они просто разные, разная их мягкость и разная эксплуатация." Ask the client which they prefer.**
- Finishes: chrome, black, gold, coloured; round or square.
- **A basin mixer with an integrated hygienic shower exists as a combined, lighter, budget option.**

## Towel Rail — Sequence, and Why Electric Widens the Options

**Decide the key zones first — WC, basin, bath, washing machine, boiler — and add the towel rail where it fits.** *"Я советую сначала определиться с ключевыми зонами… а потом уже добавлять полотенцесушитель."* [source: [[_Sources/YT_Lqx2GSllqFQ_mikhailovskaya_bathroom_planning_bath_shower|Lqx2GSllqFQ]]]

**⚠️ And the consequence: if the rail is ELECTRIC rather than water-heated it can go almost anywhere, and the range of models, forms and sizes is far larger. So where the towel rail is the awkward item, switching to electric is the move.** *(Corroborates this page's existing electric-over-hydronic preference from the planning side rather than the plumbing side.)*
