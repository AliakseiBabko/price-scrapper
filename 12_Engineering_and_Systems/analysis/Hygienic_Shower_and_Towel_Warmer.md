# Plumbing — Hygienic Shower & Towel Warmer

Covers a specific, preventable hygienic-shower failure mode, and the case for electric over hydronic towel warmers. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]].

## Hygienic Shower / Bidet Spray — a Specific, Preventable Failure Mode

- **The failure mode, stated precisely**: a user opens the hygienic-shower/bidet supply valve, uses the spray, releases the trigger (which stops water at the nozzle) — but forgets to close the *upstream* shutoff valve. The hose and mixer then sit under full, sustained line pressure indefinitely, well beyond what they're rated to hold continuously; left this way for an extended period (the example given is a vacation), the source states it will "100%" eventually fail and flood the unit and likely the neighbors below.
- **Correct usage sequence, stated explicitly by the source**: open the shutoff valve → spray → release the trigger → close the shutoff valve → also bleed/release the residual pressure trapped in the hose.
- **A "vent-type" (anti-forgetfulness) hygienic-shower valve design is recommended as the practical fix for apartments**: it's physically impossible to hang the spray nozzle back on its wall holder unless the water-supply valve has been closed first, forcing the correct shutoff sequence through the fixture's own mechanical design rather than relying on the user remembering.

## Towel Warmer: Water (Hydronic) vs. Electric

**The case for electric as the default isn't stylistic — it's a specific, safety-driven cost/risk comparison:**

- **Availability**: electric works in any room and is largely independent of the building's own hot-water schedule — power outages are argued to be both rarer and shorter (roughly 1–2 hours/year outside genuine emergencies) than scheduled hot-water outages (commonly 10+ days/year, sometimes a full summer in some regions — see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Water_Heaters|Water Heaters]]).
- **A hydronic towel warmer typically sits downstream of the leak-protection system's own shutoff valve, so a leak there isn't caught by that system at all** — covering it properly requires installing a second, fully separate leak-protection branch (its own valve, sensor, and wiring) specifically for that one fixture, adding a cited ~45,000 RUB or more in hardware alone, plus roughly 6 additional pressurized joints to the overall system.
- **A hydronic towel warmer is permanently pressurized and circulating**, tied into a dedicated riser or tapped into the hot-water stack — it can lose heat output if line pressure drops or if it sits too far from the riser, and bleeding trapped air (a Maevsky valve) doesn't reliably fix this.
- **A specific, notable failure mode: stray/galvanic current corrosion from another unit's wiring fault.** If a neighbor anywhere on the shared stack incorrectly grounds an appliance (the example given is a washing machine) to the towel-warmer riser/stack, stray electrical current can flow through the water inside your own pipes, causing electrochemical corrosion that thins the pipe wall from the inside — eventually producing a pinhole leak (cited as growing to ~3mm) that floods the downstairs neighbor, caused entirely by a fault in someone else's apartment and undetectable/unpreventable from your own unit. The source states their company has witnessed this happen directly, and frames not installing a hydronic towel warmer at all as the only real defense against this specific risk.
- **A real-world corroboration of this stance, from a second source**: the presenter reports their own building's management company is now going door-to-door offering to disconnect residents' hydronic towel warmers *for free* and helping arrange electric replacements — described as a direct response to recurring leaks/incidents across the building, framed as cheaper for the management company than continuing to handle failures.
- **Cost comparison, concrete numbers**: installing a hydronic towel warmer costs roughly **10,000–15,000 RUB**, since it requires the management company's own plumbers to weld shutoff valves into the riser (a scheduled, supervised job) — versus roughly **~2,500 RUB** to install an electric unit. Electric running cost is cited at up to ~100W while actively heating, cycling on and off to maintain temperature, working out to roughly **10–15 RUB/month** in electricity — trivial next to the potential cost of a hydronic leak (a dedicated second leak-protection branch alone runs 30,000–50,000 RUB) or a flooded neighbor's repair bill.
- **A distinct, non-leak failure mode worth knowing if a hydronic unit is used anyway**: a hydronic towel warmer typically has a bypass; if central supply pressure drops (e.g., during heavy simultaneous building-wide demand), the bypass may stop "pushing through" properly and the unit simply stops heating — nothing leaks, nothing looks wrong, it just goes cold. **If installing a hydronic towel warmer despite the above, place it as close to the riser as possible with as few pipe angles/turns as possible** — this reduces exposure to the pressure-drop failure mode, though it doesn't address the leak-protection-coverage or stray-current risks above. An electric towel warmer has no equivalent placement constraint — it can go anywhere, including the ceiling.
- **The original case for a towel rail at all stands independently of type**: it genuinely helps post-shower humidity evacuate via ventilation, and skipping a bathroom heat source entirely does invite mold risk over time — this only argues for *which type*, not against having one.
- **Pre-order and dry-fit towel warmers before tiling, not after** — towel warmers have handedness (left/right water connection); without pre-ordering/dry-fitting the actual unit against the wall, installers cut the connection opening in the wrong spot, forcing a full tile tear-out and re-tile. The same applies to mixers, which have model-specific hole spacing. `single-account`, `confirmed`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_prefinish_stage_acceptance_d34c8e90.txt`]
- **Vertical radiators are an increasingly common alternative to standard horizontal ones** for three stated reasons: aesthetics, higher heat output for a given wall footprint, and fitting spaces too narrow for a standard horizontal radiator. `single-account`.
