# Plumbing — Pressure Sizing & Water Hammer

Covers riser pressure measurement, pressure reducer types, sizing for real flow, and the two distinct water-hammer mechanisms. Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Plumbing_and_Waterproofing|Plumbing & Waterproofing]].

## Pressure: Riser Figures, Reducer Types, and a Concrete Sizing Method

- LAB-REMONT, Знакомые сантехники, and Стройплощадка × Будни сантехника report: **Riser pressure figures vary meaningfully across sources and are not force-reconciled here**: ~5–6 atm (one source), 6–8 bar "possibly more" (a second source), and one specific measured example of 11 atm on a low floor of a 23-story building (a third source, tied to that building's own height). Treat these as illustrating a wide real-world range, not a single fixed number — **measure this specific apartment's own riser pressure directly** rather than assuming any of these figures.
- Добродушный сантехник explains: **A concrete measurement method, from a prefab-kit seller's own installation guide**: screw a gauge onto the line right after the shutoff valve (or after the coarse filter, described as the most reliable point) before finishing work — described as *mandatory*, not optional, before deciding whether a reducer is needed. If self-measurement isn't possible, ask the building engineer for the basement/riser pressure and calculate: pressure drops by roughly 1 atm (≈10 m of water column) per ~10 m of additional height climbed — worked example: 7 bar (=70 m) at the basement, minus ~20 m of total rise to reach the 3rd floor ≈ 50 m ≈ 5 bar at that floor. Treat this as an illustrative method, not a universal constant.
- LAB-REMONT and Знакомые сантехники recommend: **Target regulated pressure after a reducer**: consistently cited around 2–3 bar/atm across sources, with one source's own working practice capping at 3 bar (occasionally allowing 3.2–3.5 bar if no high-demand fixtures are present), and a "world norm" range of 2.8–4 bar mentioned by the same source. **A reducer often can't hit precisely 3 bar from a higher input** — some models only regulate with a 0.5–1 bar drop, so a ~3.2 bar input might land around 2.5 bar output rather than a precise 3.
- **Two reducer types, with a real mechanism difference — this is the actual decision that matters**: a **piston (поршневой)** reducer's output pressure sags progressively as more fixtures draw simultaneously — cheaper, but functionally degrades under multi-point demand. A **diaphragm/membrane (мембранный)** reducer holds pressure steady across simultaneous draws (a shower stays at set pressure even if a kitchen tap and a toilet flush happen at the same time) as long as total flow capacity isn't exceeded — costs more but needs less frequent servicing. **Two independent sources corroborate a durability gap**: one states diaphragm reducers in their career have never needed replacement, while piston reducers "seize/gum up" quickly in typical tap water and usually get replaced with a diaphragm unit eventually anyway — framing piston-reducer savings as false economy over the system's lifetime. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_2_07a629a6.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **Rated-pressure mismatches are a real risk worth checking component-by-component**: a typical water meter may be rated to 14 bar and standard pipe/fittings to 10 bar, but some components in the same node — filter housings/bowls, some meters, some mixers — may only be rated to 4–6 bar. Check each component's own rating, not just the pipe's. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_2_07a629a6.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **A manometer's placement determines what it actually tells you.** Placed downstream of the reducer, it shows the system's actual regulated pressure (a diagnostic for whether the reducer is still working). Placed downstream of a fine filter with no reducer present, a falling reading over time indirectly signals the filter is clogging, without needing to open and inspect the screen. On an economy build with only one gauge, pick whichever question matters more for that specific node. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_4_7f5e9a77.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **A dirty/undersized filter and an undersized pipe diameter both bottleneck real-world flow, independent of the meter's or reducer's own rating.** A worked example: a tropical rain showerhead alone can draw ≥35 L/min; running that alongside a kitchen tap, washing machine, and a toilet flush can momentarily exceed 3 m³/h total draw (>2 m³/h on the cold side alone under a typical 60/40 cold/hot split) — a standard 1/2" meter's nominal throughput is only ~1.5 m³/h, well short of that peak. **Fix**: either choose a rain showerhead rated ≤15 L/min, or build the node on 3/4" pipe instead of 1/2" if genuinely high simultaneous demand is expected. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_4_7f5e9a77.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]

## Check Valves — Why They're Mandatory, Mechanistically

- **The failure this prevents is driven by pressure *differential* between the hot and cold lines, not by absolute pressure** — worth stating explicitly since "my system pressure is well within the components' rating" can wrongly suggest a check valve is unnecessary. With a single-lever mixer left slightly open (including a hygienic-shower/bidet mixer accidentally left cracked), the higher-pressure side (commonly hot) can push backward through the mixer into the lower-pressure line, and from there into the shared riser. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **The consequence can affect an entire riser, not just one apartment**: hot water backfeeding into a cold riser overnight can fill the *entire* shared cold riser with hot water, so neighbors on that riser wake up to unexpectedly hot water from a cold tap — and it corrupts metering, since the affected apartments' cold meter and hot meter readings no longer reflect real consumption on either side. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- **This wasn't always a requirement** — older Soviet-era plumbing (two-handle "barashek" fittings, no meters, simple steel tee distribution) didn't have this cross-contamination risk, because two-handle mixers don't allow the same kind of hot/cold mixing a single-lever mixer does. Check valves became a hard requirement specifically as single-lever and thermostatic mixers became standard. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
- Cost: ~500 RUB per valve (one source, 2024).
- Konstantin Kruglov / Ontario generalizes the placement rule: **always install a check valve at the inlet (both hot and cold lines), and additionally at specific downstream devices with a reverse-flow risk** — named examples: pump equipment (reverse flow can damage a pump), a storage water heater/boiler, and a hygienic shower/bidet spray. [source: [[_Sources/YT_4jAQ526Zy2w_kruglov_perfect_manifold_unit|4jAQ526Zy2w_kruglov_perfect_manifold_unit]]]

## Water-Hammer Compensators — Two Distinct Mechanisms, Not One

LAB-REMONT, Знакомые сантехники, and Добродушный сантехник describe: **Two sources describe genuinely different physical triggers for what's sold under the same name ("гаситель/компенсатор гидроудара") — a third, independent source describes both mechanisms as functions of the same component, which is the framing adopted here rather than treating the first two as contradictory:**

1. **Thermal-expansion protection (cold line specifically)**: cold water sitting stagnant in the pipes overnight gradually warms toward room temperature and thermally expands, raising static pressure in the closed system. A compensator's internal membrane absorbs this slow pressure rise. Because hot water in idle pipes only cools (and loses pressure, never gains it), a compensator on the hot line serves no functional purpose under this mechanism — "aesthetic symmetry" only. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]
2. **Dynamic valve-slam shock protection (any line)**: a washing machine's fill valve slamming shut after filling, or a single-lever mixer closed abruptly, generates a genuine water-hammer pressure spike that stresses pipes, fittings, and fixtures over time — independent of the thermal-expansion mechanism above. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]

**A flexible braided-steel supply hose is repeatedly named, across multiple sources, as the single weakest and most commonly-failing point in a home's plumbing** — both mechanisms above are described as contributing causes of a hose bursting overnight (mechanism 1) or during use (mechanism 2). One source states the overnight-thermal-expansion failure mode is, in their experience, more common than the acute impact-shock failure mode. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]

**A pressure reducer does not substitute for a water-hammer arrestor — they answer two different viewer objections at once (added 2026-08-24, Round 5)**: Pavel Sidorik, answering a viewer comment questioning why arrestors are needed at all given a reducer is already installed, states directly that a **pressure reducer only addresses building-wide/central hammer events from the shared riser (cited threshold: roughly 10 bar)**, while a **water-hammer arrestor addresses local hammer generated inside the apartment itself by a mixer valve slamming shut** (mechanism 2 above) — the reducer has no effect on this local mechanism at all, so both components serve distinct, non-overlapping purposes rather than one making the other redundant. [source: [[_Sources/YT_N36scNqRwII_sidorik_water_heater_installation_errors|N36scNqRwII_sidorik_water_heater_installation_errors]]]

**No source — including the manufacturers, per one presenter's own honest disclosure — can quantify exactly how much longer a system lasts with a compensator installed.** It's presented consistently as comfort/risk-reduction, analogized by one source to blood-pressure management: you can't prove exactly how much it extends anything, but chronic excess stress on flexible hoses/gaskets/fittings is worse than a system operating in its designed comfortable range. Mechanically: a small canister ("flying saucer" or barrel-shaped) with an internal membrane, one side exposed to line pressure/water, the other charged with air (one source describes charging it via a standard car-tire valve). [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_plumbing_video_1_ce58ce96.txt|20260731_plumbing_video_]]]

## ⚠️ A Working Pressure Ceiling, and a Leak Caused by Raising It (Руслан via NSDSGN, 2023-10-11)

**A concrete figure, and a first-hand incident showing what happens when it is exceeded to solve a flow complaint.** [source: [[_Sources/YT_Z0brwxSe7gQ_nsdsgn_engineering_systems_site_review|YT_Z0brwxSe7gQ]]]

- **Normal working pressure: up to ~3.5 bar. «Выше 3,5 лучше не поднимать.»**
- **⚠️ And the reason it is a real ceiling rather than a preference: «некоторые сантехнические приборы — даже в их техничках написано, что [при превышении] это давление будет выходить из строя».** So the limit is set by the weakest specified appliance in the flat, and it is published in each one's datasheet.
- **⚠️ THE INCIDENT, from the designer's own flat and told against himself: he felt he lacked pressure, was advised by an acquaintance to raise it, and did. A washing-machine hose that had been hand-tightened without a spanner then loosened, seeped, and let go within a short time. Leak sensors shut the water off before it became a flood** — see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Leak_Protection_Systems|Leak Protection Systems]].
- **→ The rule the incident supports: raising system pressure to fix a flow complaint transfers the stress onto the weakest connection in the flat, and every poorly-made joint becomes a candidate.** Diagnose a flow problem at the fixture, the filter or the distribution before raising the pressure — this vault's collector-versus-tee distribution material is the more likely explanation for uneven flow. See [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Rough_Plumbing_Sequencing|Rough Plumbing Sequencing]].
