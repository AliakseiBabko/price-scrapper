# Heating — Where Underfloor Heating Is Needed

Part of [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Heating|Heating]]. Companion to [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Heating_Type_Selection|Heating: Type Selection]] — that page covers *which* heating-element type to use, this page covers *where* underfloor heating is needed at all.

## Rule 1 — Replace a Standard Heat Source With Underfloor Heating Wherever Legally/Technically Possible

In practice this rule mostly applies to a private house with individual heating — a standard apartment can't legally substitute water underfloor heating for central-heating radiators (see [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Heating_Type_Selection|Type Selection]]). Where it does apply, underfloor heating of the same "type" as the radiator it replaces is argued to be strictly better on every parameter: better energy efficiency for a given heated area; more even air-temperature distribution (a radiator only heats the air near itself); a cleaner room layout with no protruding fixture and easier cleaning; and — the central comfort argument — floor-level heating lets a room feel comfortable at a genuinely lower measured air temperature and lower humidity-drying effect than radiator heating, since a radiator overheats the air near itself while leaving the floor cold, forcing occupants to over-heat the whole room (and still often wear socks/slippers) to compensate for the cold floor underfoot.

## Rule 2 — Heat Every Tile-Covered Floor Area

Two independent reasons, not one:

1. **Thermal-conductivity comfort mechanism.** A material's thermal conductivity, not its actual temperature, determines how cold it *feels* underfoot — cork (low conductivity) feels warm at room temperature; tile and metal (high conductivity) feel cool at the identical room temperature, because a high-conductivity material draws heat from the body faster than the body can replace it. Sustained contact with an unheated high-conductivity floor isn't just uncomfortable — extended exposure (the source's own worked comparison: lying on room-temperature concrete for 30 minutes) is a real chill/illness risk, not merely a subjective preference. [source: [[_Sources/YT_C1L06upDI98_underfloor_heating_placement_rules_096|C1L06upDI98_underfloor_h]]]
2. **Moisture-drying mechanism.** Tile gets chosen precisely because it tolerates standing water (an entryway with wet shoes, a bathroom with splashed water) — but that water still needs to dry afterward. Underfloor heating is the most effective way to dry the room and prevent mold, not just a comfort add-on. [source: [[_Sources/YT_C1L06upDI98_underfloor_heating_placement_rules_096|C1L06upDI98_underfloor_h]]]

## Rule 3 — Every Insulated (Joined) Loggia Needs Underfloor Heating, No Exception

The floor is the coldest surface in any room. An insulated loggia's floor stays cold regardless of any other heat source added to the space — a relocated radiator or a space heater warms the air, not the slab. Even if the loggia below or adjacent has its own floor heating, that warmth doesn't transfer upward: the neighboring unit's own floor insulation blocks heat transfer through their ceiling into your loggia's slab. A loggia without floor heating stays uncomfortably cold to stand on even in wool socks, regardless of every other heating measure taken.

Zemstandart/Zemsproekt (Alexey Zemskov) says: **Independently corroborated** by this vault's existing loggia-heating content (`yt_aoKerAFdZwg`): "a well-glazed loggia still equalizes toward outdoor temperature without an active heat source... thin-film underfloor heating recommended over a convector/radiator, since the floor is the coldest surface" — same conclusion, independent source and date.

**⚠️ Practical caveat, in tension with (not a contradiction of) the "no exception" rule above — flagged explicitly rather than silently blended (added 2026-08-24, Round 3).** Konstantin Kruglov / Ontario: underfloor heating alone is far too slow for an occasionally-used loggia/balcony — by this source's own numbers, floor heating needs roughly **4 hours** to usefully warm the space (heat must pass through tile adhesive, then the tile itself, before the air warms), whereas a plug-in electric convector warms the same air in roughly **30 minutes**. This doesn't contradict the existing rule's core reasoning (the floor is still the coldest surface and still needs heating) — the caveat is that underfloor heating's slow response time means it shouldn't be the space's *only* heat source if the loggia is used occasionally rather than kept continuously warm. **Practical recommendation: install underfloor heating as usual, and also pre-wire an outlet (or directly install a wall-mounted electric convector) for fast on-demand air heating.** `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_Q1KSHFhLzJo_kruglov_heating_secrets|Q1KSHFhLzJo]]]

## Radiator-Niche Formula, Most Complete Version (added 2026-08-20, remainder-pool Round 2)

Zemstandart/Zemsproekt (Alexey Zemskov) says: **⚠️ The most complete radiator-niche formula found in this store to date**: lower return **15cm** (so the finished baseboard doesn't cover the decorative grille); niche height = the radiator's own **inter-axial (center-to-center) dimension + 20cm** (for adequate convection); upper return **minimum 5cm** — if this comes out under 5cm, choose a different radiator model with a smaller inter-axial dimension and recalculate the whole niche rather than accept an undersized upper return. **Black radiator-grille color caution, counterintuitive**: painting a concealment grille black makes accumulated dust *more* visible, not less — a lighter/neutral grille color hides dust better. `single-account`, `unverified`. [sources: [[_Sources/YT_hVFmcw1H2Rk_bedroom_design_sequence_masterclass_314|YT_hVFmcw1H2Rk]], [[_Sources/YT_GtOQ7h1p5qc_pure_design_madness_70sqm_303|YT_GtOQ7h1p5qc]]]

## Bidirectional (Double-Sided) Radiator Technique (added 2026-08-19, remainder-pool batch)

Zemstandart/Zemsproekt (Alexey Zemskov) says: **A radiator positioned within a dividing wall between two rooms/zones can be made double-sided** — remove the partition segment immediately around it and add a grille on both faces — so one radiator heats two adjacent spaces more efficiently instead of needing a separate unit for each. Deployed specifically to combat window-condensation risk on a large panoramic-glazing wall where in-floor convectors weren't an option on that project. Only applicable when an interior partition wall exists at the right location relative to the glazing needing heat. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_D8t1ADisUE8_odnushka_premium_replan_246|YT_D8t1ADisUE8]]]

## Underfloor-Heating Sensor Conduit (Петришин-Строй, added 2026-08-24, Round 2)

Route the heated-floor temperature sensor through a 16mm-diameter Rehau
(PE-Xa) or metal-plastic pipe, not the thin corrugated tube some sensor
manufacturers bundle with the sensor — the sensor must be able to slide
freely to the far end of the tube so it can be pulled and replaced later
if it fails. The manufacturer-supplied thin corrugated sleeve is described
as too flimsy for reliable future extraction; a rigid 16mm pipe keeps the
sensor serviceable without breaking into the screed. Region level 2
(channel-level Moscow association only, no city named in this source).
[source: [[_Sources/YT_Q6GKMOJuaPc_petrishin_electrical_quality_checklist|Q6GKMOJuaPc]]]

## Kitchen: Radiator Relocation When the Sink (Not the Prep Surface) Goes Under the Window (added 2026-08-25)

Архитектор Виталий Злобин says: **the modern kitchen-window trend of putting the sink (rather than the prep surface) under the window has a real infrastructure cost** — code/convention places a radiator under the window by default, which is no longer possible once the sink occupies that spot. The radiator must move to a side wall instead. Two consequences: (1) **a side-wall radiator needs a higher rated output** than a standard under-window unit, since it no longer directly counters the window's own cold-air draft the same way; (2) **never tuck a kitchen radiator inside the cabinet run itself** — its efficiency drops sharply, it doesn't work properly, and it risks the cabinetry. Underfloor heating or convectors are offered as alternatives, with the same whole-floor-consistency caveat this page already records for underfloor heating (mixing underfloor heat in the kitchen with radiators elsewhere on the same floor is impractical). `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_Ubl--73gmTs_zlobin_kitchen_theory_pt1|Ubl--73gmTs]]]

## Heated-Floor Scope, Stated as a Room-by-Room Rule (Игорь Краснов, added 2026-09-01, Round 4)

Consistent with — and a compact restatement of — this page's existing Rule 2 (heat every tile-covered floor area) and its two mechanisms above, from an unrelated turnkey-studio channel: heated floor is genuinely worth it mainly in the **bathroom** and the **kitchen's tiled work-zone specifically** (keeps a tiled floor comfortable underfoot, helps bathroom surfaces dry faster and resist mould — the same conductivity/moisture-drying mechanisms already recorded above) — **rarely worth it in bedrooms/living rooms** where wood flooring or laminate is typical and the comfort benefit is minimal. `single-account`, `unverified`. [source: [[_Sources/YT_NO6-2zAJgys_krasnov_19controversial_items|NO6-2zAJgys]]]

## The Cost of Deferring This Decision

Retrofitting floor heating after a renovation is finished (breaking out already-tiled/finished flooring to add it later) costs meaningfully more than including it in the original design-project scope. The source uses this specifically as a worked illustration of a broader point: hundreds of small planning details like this one compound in cost when deferred to "later," not just floor heating.
