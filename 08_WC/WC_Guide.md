# 🚽 WC (Guest Toilet) Guide

Covers minimum/comfortable room dimensions and fixture-selection notes specific to a standalone WC (separate from a combined bathroom — see [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Renovation Guide]] for combined-bathroom content, most of which also applies here). Quick-reference rules with sources live in [[08_WC/analysis/Dos_and_Donts|the companion Do's and Don'ts table]].

> [!NOTE]
> This page's content comes from the Земстандарт (Zemstandart) channel, a Moscow-area renovation company, processed 2026-07-31 as part of a 33-video playlist batch. Self-promotional by format; `single-account` tags reflect one practitioner's stated numbers/experience, not independently corroborated. Dimensions below are this source's own design-practice figures, not a cited building code — verify against local code/product specs before treating as a hard requirement.

## 1. Minimum and Comfortable Room Dimensions — a Concrete Worked Method

**The sewage stack (a 100mm pipe) is the anchor for everything** — if there's no stack near a proposed WC location, the location is wrong; find the stack first, then place the room around it.

**Length**, measured from the installation's front (finished) face:
- If the stack is *inside* the WC room, measure the setback from the room's back wall: leave **200mm** from the back wall — a typical in-wall toilet-frame installation depth is ~150mm, so 200mm covers the frame plus the corrugated connector hose even with some bend in it.
- If the stack/plumbing is in an *adjacent* room instead, measure from the plumbing's most-protruding point and leave **200–250mm** — the extra 50mm accounts for the toilet frame's center rarely lining up exactly with the stack's center, needing room for the connector hose to bend without kinking.
- From that setback (the installation's "front face," typically drywall-boxed and then tiled), leave a further **550mm** for the toilet bowl itself (standard bowl length is 52–56cm).
- **This is not enough on its own — don't stop at the seated-knee-clearance distance.** Measuring from the front face to your own knees while seated typically comes out to ~80cm, which can create a false impression that ~90cm total is plenty. It isn't: people also need to *stand* at the toilet, not just sit, and standing use needs a further **minimum 45cm** of clearance beyond the bowl's own length. One source recounts a real case where a client built to 90cm total and discovered the shortfall only after moving in. **Absolute minimum WC length: 1 meter** from the installation's front face. **Comfortable standing-use length: 1.10 meters.**

**Width**:
- 80cm alone is enough for even a large person to sit or stand comfortably at the toilet — but a WC this narrow can't actually be built, because of the door. A standard interior door leaf is at least 60cm, and the door casing/trim needs roughly 3–4cm of clearance from the side walls on each side (to avoid the casing being cut or crammed against the wall) — working through that arithmetic gives a **minimum finished width of 87cm**.
- **This 87cm figure is *finished* width — after tile.** Tile plus adhesive adds roughly 1.5cm per side (matching the general tile-buildup rule elsewhere on this vault), so the room needs to be at least **90cm wide after plastering, before tiling**, to net 87cm finished.
- **This minimum only holds if the WC has no hygienic shower/bidet spray.** If it does, a sink becomes mandatory too (you need to wash your hands after use) — which requires offsetting the door opening and pushes the minimum width to **1 meter**, with the sink itself as narrow as ~20cm (see §2) — the sink will slightly protrude into the door passage at this minimum, a cosmetic issue only, not a functional obstruction. **Comfortable width with a 25cm sink: 1.10 meters.**

## 2. Sink Sizing (also broadly applicable to a bathroom vanity — see [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Guide]] §21)

Sink **length** is the primary selection dimension — width is roughly proportional and fairly standardized across most models; thickness matters mainly for aesthetics or when mounting over a washing machine.

| Length | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| 50cm (~20cm wide) | Smallest reasonable size. WC-only, placed near the door, purely for handwashing after a hygienic shower — **too small to actually wash your face at**: leaning in as far as possible still leaves roughly half your face outside the basin, guaranteeing water on the floor. |
| 60cm | Still cramped, but at least workable for basic washing. Its real advantage: matches a standard washing machine's width, so it fits neatly above one — though being wedged between adjacent fixtures on both sides in that position makes it awkward to actually use. |
| 70cm | Enough room to brush teeth without hitting your elbows on the side walls. Used in small bathrooms, but rare in practice (~3% of this source's own designs) — there's rarely exactly 70cm free after placing the other fixtures. |
| **80cm** | **The best all-around size** — comfortable washing without pinning your elbows to your ribs. Also lines up neatly with a common room layout: a typical entry-facing wall of ~170cm, minus ~4cm plaster (2cm/side) minus ~3cm tile+adhesive (1.5cm/side) minus 80cm for a standard tub, leaves ~83cm — which an 80cm sink fits into cleanly. |
| 90cm | Same logic as 80cm, but for a ~180cm opposite-wall layout — less common simply because 180cm walls occur less often than 170cm ones. |
| 100cm+ | Enough length to fit two mixers/faucets — but a 100cm sink with two people using both faucets simultaneously effectively becomes two cramped 50cm sinks (see the top row). **Genuinely comfortable simultaneous two-person use needs 130cm+.** |

**Planning responsibility**: for a kitchen, accounting for plaster thickness before buying furniture is optional-but-recommended; **for a bathroom or WC specifically, accounting for both plaster thickness and tile+adhesive thickness is mandatory** — and it's the designer/planner's responsibility to hand the client exact, ready-to-shop finished dimensions, not the client's job to work it out themselves.

## 2a. Storage Cabinet Around a Toilet Installation Frame

**Build two separate boxes, not one uniform full-height box, above and around an in-wall toilet frame/cistern:**
- **Lower box**: height ~1250mm (the frame's own installation height), depth ~250mm — houses the installation itself.
- **Upper box**: height ~150–200mm, same ~250mm depth — genuinely usable storage (toiletries, cleaning supplies) that a single uniform box tied to the frame's own shallow depth would otherwise waste. Use ordinary cabinet doors here, not open shelving — a stack of toilet paper isn't something worth displaying.
- **Don't build the upper box's height flush with a stretch ceiling's own drop** (which typically eats ~70mm on its own) — doing so leaves the cabinet doors almost touching the ceiling, meaning any protruding light fixture can block them from opening at all, and it looks worse than leaving a small strip of visible tile above the cabinet. Build to the 150–200mm figure instead.
- **Don't shave the box depth below ~250mm to "win back" a few centimeters of room space** — the room-space gain from cutting depth to, say, 15cm is negligible (a few cm either way won't meaningfully affect knee clearance across a room), but a 15cm-deep cabinet is nearly useless (can barely hold a small bottle, awkward to close without crushing contents) versus a genuinely functional ~25cm depth.
- **A common construction mistake**: installers forget to build blocking/backing behind the tiled surface specifically for later mounting the upper cabinet's door hinges — without it, there's nothing to screw the hinges into once the client wants doors installed. Confirm this backing is planned before the box is tiled shut.
- **Keep a plumbing/utility cabinet (filters, meters, valves) and a general storage cabinet separate — don't try to merge them to save space.** A utility cabinet already takes real planning effort to keep serviceable; cramming toiletries or extra rolls of paper into the same space defeats that and makes future servicing harder for no real storage gain (use a second, dedicated cabinet for general storage instead).

## 3. A Space-Saving Alternative: Wall-Mounted Urinal

Presented primarily as a comfort/hygiene fixture, but with genuine space-planning implications worth recording independent of that framing:
- **Needs only ~50cm of width** (a corner-mounted model needs even less), versus a toilet's ~80cm minimum — a real option where a toilet simply won't fit (e.g., a small utility WC that already has a washing machine, sink, and toilet crammed in with no room for anything else).
- **Doesn't need to be placed near the stack** — unlike a toilet, whose drain needs a consistent gravity slope and therefore a short, direct run to the stack, a urinal is mounted at height and can reportedly be placed several meters from the stack while still draining correctly.
- Cheaper than a toilet and uses less water per use; most models include an auto-flush-on-departure sensor.
- **Practical implication**: in an apartment with only one full toilet, adding a urinal in a second, smaller space (even one too small for a second full toilet — e.g., a utility room) can function as a second relief point without needing a second full bathroom. `single-account`, and the framing is heavily aimed at a specific (male-focused) use case — recorded here for the concrete space/drainage facts, not the social framing.

## 4. Mirror-in-Shower-Tile and Recessed Fixtures

- **A mirror embedded flush into shower-wall tile (rather than mounted separately) is a genuine tradeoff, not a clear win or loss** — it requires wiping after every shower (water spots/splashes), and the tile-to-mirror junction is sealed with flexible sealant rather than matching grout, which is visually different from the surrounding grout, less durable, and needs periodic renewal. **The case for it**: for someone who uses an electric trimmer/razor daily, a regular vanity sink accumulates enough fine hair clippings on the sink and floor to require a genuinely time-consuming clean (cited around 30 minutes) every couple of days — a quick post-shower wipe of an in-shower mirror is argued to be meaningfully less hassle than that. Consider this specifically for a household member who grooms with an electric trimmer regularly, not as a general recommendation.
- **A recessed, spring-loaded toilet-paper holder (push-button pop-out mechanism) needs at least 8cm of chase depth into the wall** — not always available, particularly on a shared wall backing directly onto a neighboring unit. Confirm real wall depth before specifying this fixture type; it can't be retrofitted into a wall that's too thin.

## 2b. Bidet-Function Toilets and Similar Fixtures — Plumbing, Not Electrical

**A bidet-function toilet is purely mechanical, driven by water pressure — it needs no electrical supply**, despite the motorized-looking wash-arm mechanism sometimes creating that impression. **What it does need is both a hot and a cold water connection**, plumbed essentially like a standard hygienic shower/bidet spray — plan the rough-in accordingly regardless of whether an electrical run seems more obviously necessary. Pricing for these units has risen sharply since 2021 and shouldn't be assumed comparable to older cited figures.

## 3a. Sewage Stack Rerouting — a Top-Floor-Only Option

**On a top-floor apartment specifically (nothing flushes from above), the stack can sometimes be rerouted with a horizontal jog** — offset out from its default vertical line, then brought back to the same axis — to free up room for a properly laid-out plumbing utility area. **Never do this on any floor below the top one**: anything flushed from above (including, worst case, solid waste from a clog or backup) would fall onto a horizontally-offset section from height, a real risk that doesn't exist when there's nothing above to fall from. A narrow-use-case technique, but worth knowing if a top-floor unit's stack placement is otherwise blocking a good layout.

## 3b. Wide-Format Tile Complicates the Hidden-Access-Tile Technique — a Workaround

The hidden emergency-access technique in [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Guide]] §12 (unglued, film-backed tiles that pop out with a suction cup) assumes at least two smaller tiles can be left unglued near the access point. **A single large-format tile (e.g. 60×120cm or bigger) can't be handled the same way** — there's only one tile covering that whole span, not two to selectively leave unglued. Two workarounds: (1) cut an access flap/corner out of the single large tile and seal its edge with silicone, matching the same removable-panel logic; or (2) install a proper concealed hatch — but a hatch consumes some usable space around its own perimeter and costs meaningfully more for a quality unit, a real cost/space tradeoff against option (1). **A cheaper, durable middle option for a plumbing utility box specifically (not a bathtub apron)**: a small two-door furniture-style insert set flush with the surrounding tile plane — inexpensive, long-lasting, and only consumes ~2-3cm of depth at the opening while leaving the rest of the cabinet interior fully accessible.

## 4a. A Few More Corroborated Planning Constants

- **Minimum/comfortable bathtub width**: 700mm is workable but described as uncomfortable for most people; **800mm is the practical default**, sizing up to 900mm for larger-bodied users — corroborated consistently across multiple videos from this same channel.
- **Add a ~50mm safety margin beyond a sink or tub's nominal width** when setting the room dimension around it — manufacturers' actual product dimensions commonly run slightly over their nominal size (e.g. an "80cm" sink might really be 82cm); without the margin, the fixture can end up pressed unevenly against a side wall.
- **Heated-floor thermostat placement convention**: always mount it outside the bathroom/WC (in the corridor), at a comfortable eye-level height, on the same side of the doorway as the room's light switch — both aligned on a shared vertical axis roughly 15cm from the edge of the door opening. A specific, checkable convention worth matching for a consistent, predictable electrical layout across rooms.
- **Standard interior door opening height used throughout this source's own projects: 2070mm** — a reasonable default datum if no other constraint dictates otherwise.
- **A utility box's front wall facing a riser/stack should be built no thinner than ~50mm** — thinner risks piercing the pipe itself when later anchoring a sink or fixture to the box face. Don't build the box depth flush/tight against the actual pipe route either — leaving zero clearance creates unusable dead space and can still prevent a fixture from being properly anchored to the box.
- **A revision hatch for a toilet-installation utility box (water-treatment node, filters, meters) is commonly sized ~600mm wide × 900mm tall** — a reasonable default to plan around absent a more specific requirement.
- **Exhaust-fan switch placement, WC-specific detail (added 2026-08-04)**: locate the switch near the toilet itself, not elsewhere in the room — keeps it within immediate reach for prompt odor control right when it's needed. Extends [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/Electrical_and_Lighting|Electrical & Lighting]] §7's existing rule that a bathroom/WC fan switch belongs inside the room, not the hallway, with a specific in-room position. `single-account`.
