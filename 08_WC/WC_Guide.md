# 🚽 WC (Guest Toilet) Guide

Covers minimum/comfortable room dimensions and fixture-selection notes specific to a standalone WC (separate from a combined bathroom — see [[07_Bathroom/Bathroom_Guide|Bathroom Renovation Guide]] for combined-bathroom content, most of which also applies here). Quick-reference rules with sources live in [[08_WC/analysis/Dos_and_Donts|the companion Do's and Don'ts table]].

> [!NOTE]
> This page's content comes from the Земстандарт (Zemstandart) channel, a Moscow-area renovation company, processed 2026-07-31 as part of a 33-video playlist batch. Self-promotional by format; `single-account` tags reflect one practitioner's stated numbers/experience, not independently corroborated. Dimensions below are this source's own design-practice figures, not a cited building code — verify against local code/product specs before treating as a hard requirement.

## 1. Minimum and Comfortable Room Dimensions — a Concrete Worked Method

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov gives the following WC-dimension method (Source Notes: archived b120 extraction; channel mapping is explicit).

**The sewage stack (a 100mm pipe) is the anchor for everything** — if there's no stack near a proposed WC location, the location is wrong; find the stack first, then place the room around it. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toile]]]

**Length**, measured from the installation's front (finished) face: [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toile]]]
- If the stack is *inside* the WC room, measure the setback from the room's back wall: leave **200mm** from the back wall — a typical in-wall toilet-frame installation depth is ~150mm, so 200mm covers the frame plus the corrugated connector hose even with some bend in it. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- If the stack/plumbing is in an *adjacent* room instead, measure from the plumbing's most-protruding point and leave **200–250mm** — the extra 50mm accounts for the toilet frame's center rarely lining up exactly with the stack's center, needing room for the connector hose to bend without kinking. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- From that setback (the installation's "front face," typically drywall-boxed and then tiled), leave a further **550mm** for the toilet bowl itself (standard bowl length is 52–56cm). [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- **This is not enough on its own — don't stop at the seated-knee-clearance distance.** Measuring from the front face to your own knees while seated typically comes out to ~80cm, which can create a false impression that ~90cm total is plenty. It isn't: people also need to *stand* at the toilet, not just sit, and standing use needs a further **minimum 45cm** of clearance beyond the bowl's own length. One source recounts a real case where a client built to 90cm total and discovered the shortfall only after moving in. **Absolute minimum WC length: 1 meter** from the installation's front face. **Comfortable standing-use length: 1.10 meters.** [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toile]]]

**Width**:
- 80cm alone is enough for even a large person to sit or stand comfortably at the toilet — but a WC this narrow can't actually be built, because of the door. A standard interior door leaf is at least 60cm, and the door casing/trim needs roughly 3–4cm of clearance from the side walls on each side (to avoid the casing being cut or crammed against the wall) — working through that arithmetic gives a **minimum finished width of 87cm**. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- **This 87cm figure is *finished* width — after tile.** Tile plus adhesive adds roughly 1.5cm per side (matching the general tile-buildup rule elsewhere on this vault), so the room needs to be at least **90cm wide after plastering, before tiling**, to net 87cm finished. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- **This minimum only holds if the WC has no hygienic shower/bidet spray.** If it does, a sink becomes mandatory too (you need to wash your hands after use) — which requires offsetting the door opening and pushes the minimum width to **1 meter**, with the sink itself as narrow as ~20cm (see §2) — the sink will slightly protrude into the door passage at this minimum, a cosmetic issue only, not a functional obstruction. **Comfortable width with a 25cm sink: 1.10 meters.** [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toilet]]]
- **⚠️ Independent corroboration (added 2026-08-19, remainder-pool batch)**: a separate source independently arrives at the same **1.10m** figure, describing it as "the ideal minimum width for a comfortable WC" — an independent worked-example match for the comfortable-width figure above, not just the same author restating it. The same source also gives a fully worked too-narrow-WC example: a 65.9cm raw clear width (built from 60cm-thick aerated-concrete block), pre-finish — corroborating the "the finished/raw distinction matters, and a WC that looks adequate raw can be unusable once tiled" point already established above. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_8Xy-h8cS_-s_full_replan_worst_two_room_234|YT_8Xy-h8cS_-s]], [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_n7X10oIqugU_full_replan_252|YT_n7X10oIqugU]]]

## 2. Sink Sizing (also broadly applicable to a bathroom vanity — see [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Fixtures_Mixers_and_Sinks|Bathroom: Mixers, Toilet, Sinks & Hygienic Shower]])

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov gives the following sink-sizing guidance (Source Notes: archived b122 extraction; channel mapping is explicit).

Sink **length** is the primary selection dimension — width is roughly proportional and fairly standardized across most models; thickness matters mainly for aesthetics or when mounting over a washing machine.

| Length | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| 50cm (~20cm wide) | Smallest reasonable size. WC-only, placed near the door, purely for handwashing after a hygienic shower — **too small to actually wash your face at**: leaning in as far as possible still leaves roughly half your face outside the basin, guaranteeing water on the floor. |
| 60cm | Still cramped, but at least workable for basic washing. Its real advantage: matches a standard washing machine's width, so it fits neatly above one — though being wedged between adjacent fixtures on both sides in that position makes it awkward to actually use. |
| 70cm | Enough room to brush teeth without hitting your elbows on the side walls. Used in small bathrooms, but rare in practice (~3% of this source's own designs) — there's rarely exactly 70cm free after placing the other fixtures. |
| **80cm** | **The best all-around size** — comfortable washing without pinning your elbows to your ribs. Also lines up neatly with a common room layout: a typical entry-facing wall of ~170cm, minus ~4cm plaster (2cm/side) minus ~3cm tile+adhesive (1.5cm/side) minus 80cm for a standard tub, leaves ~83cm — which an 80cm sink fits into cleanly. |
| 90cm | Same logic as 80cm, but for a ~180cm opposite-wall layout — less common simply because 180cm walls occur less often than 170cm ones. |
| 100cm+ | Enough length to fit two mixers/faucets — but a 100cm sink with two people using both faucets simultaneously effectively becomes two cramped 50cm sinks (see the top row). **Genuinely comfortable simultaneous two-person use needs 130cm+.** |

**Planning responsibility**: for a kitchen, accounting for plaster thickness before buying furniture is optional-but-recommended; **for a bathroom or WC specifically, accounting for both plaster thickness and tile+adhesive thickness is mandatory** — and it's the designer/planner's responsibility to hand the client exact, ready-to-shop finished dimensions, not the client's job to work it out themselves. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b120_toilet_060b616c.txt|20260731_zems_b120_toile]]]

## 2a. Storage Cabinet Around a Toilet Installation Frame

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov recommends the following toilet-storage construction (Source Notes: archived b147 extraction; channel mapping is explicit).

**Build two separate boxes, not one uniform full-height box, above and around an in-wall toilet frame/cistern:** [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **Lower box**: height ~1250mm (the frame's own installation height), depth ~250mm — houses the installation itself. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **Upper box**: height ~150–200mm, same ~250mm depth — genuinely usable storage (toiletries, cleaning supplies) that a single uniform box tied to the frame's own shallow depth would otherwise waste. Use ordinary cabinet doors here, not open shelving — a stack of toilet paper isn't something worth displaying. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **Don't build the upper box's height flush with a stretch ceiling's own drop** (which typically eats ~70mm on its own) — doing so leaves the cabinet doors almost touching the ceiling, meaning any protruding light fixture can block them from opening at all, and it looks worse than leaving a small strip of visible tile above the cabinet. Build to the 150–200mm figure instead. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **Don't shave the box depth below ~250mm to "win back" a few centimeters of room space** — the room-space gain from cutting depth to, say, 15cm is negligible (a few cm either way won't meaningfully affect knee clearance across a room), but a 15cm-deep cabinet is nearly useless (can barely hold a small bottle, awkward to close without crushing contents) versus a genuinely functional ~25cm depth. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **A common construction mistake**: installers forget to build blocking/backing behind the tiled surface specifically for later mounting the upper cabinet's door hinges — without it, there's nothing to screw the hinges into once the client wants doors installed. Confirm this backing is planned before the box is tiled shut. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]
- **Keep a plumbing/utility cabinet (filters, meters, valves) and a general storage cabinet separate — don't try to merge them to save space.** A utility cabinet already takes real planning effort to keep serviceable; cramming toiletries or extra rolls of paper into the same space defeats that and makes future servicing harder for no real storage gain (use a second, dedicated cabinet for general storage instead). [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b147_toiletmistake_33c63821.txt|20260731_zems_b147_toile]]]

## 3. A Space-Saving Alternative: Wall-Mounted Urinal

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov gives the following urinal space/drainage guidance (Source Notes: archived b121 extraction; channel mapping is explicit).

Presented primarily as a comfort/hygiene fixture, but with genuine space-planning implications worth recording independent of that framing:
- **Needs only ~50cm of width** (a corner-mounted model needs even less), versus a toilet's ~80cm minimum — a real option where a toilet simply won't fit (e.g., a small utility WC that already has a washing machine, sink, and toilet crammed in with no room for anything else). [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b121_toilethack_60df4db3.txt|20260731_zems_b121_toilethack]]]
- **Doesn't need to be placed near the stack** — unlike a toilet, whose drain needs a consistent gravity slope and therefore a short, direct run to the stack, a urinal is mounted at height and can reportedly be placed several meters from the stack while still draining correctly. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b121_toilethack_60df4db3.txt|20260731_zems_b121_toilethack]]]
- Cheaper than a toilet and uses less water per use; most models include an auto-flush-on-departure sensor. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b121_toilethack_60df4db3.txt|20260731_zems_b121_toilethack]]]
- **Practical implication**: in an apartment with only one full toilet, adding a urinal in a second, smaller space (even one too small for a second full toilet — e.g., a utility room) can function as a second relief point without needing a second full bathroom. `single-account`, and the framing is heavily aimed at a specific (male-focused) use case — recorded here for the concrete space/drainage facts, not the social framing. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b121_toilethack_60df4db3.txt|20260731_zems_b121_toilethack]]]

## 4. Mirror-in-Shower-Tile and Recessed Fixtures

`channel not confirmed` — the following mirror/recessed-fixture claims cite archived b106 material without a directly linked extraction-note `channel:` field.

- **A mirror embedded flush into shower-wall tile (rather than mounted separately) is a genuine tradeoff, not a clear win or loss** — it requires wiping after every shower (water spots/splashes), and the tile-to-mirror junction is sealed with flexible sealant rather than matching grout, which is visually different from the surrounding grout, less durable, and needs periodic renewal. **The case for it**: for someone who uses an electric trimmer/razor daily, a regular vanity sink accumulates enough fine hair clippings on the sink and floor to require a genuinely time-consuming clean (cited around 30 minutes) every couple of days — a quick post-shower wipe of an in-shower mirror is argued to be meaningfully less hassle than that. Consider this specifically for a household member who grooms with an electric trimmer regularly, not as a general recommendation. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b106_mirror_d60b55af.txt|20260731_zems_b106_mirro]]]
- **A recessed, spring-loaded toilet-paper holder (push-button pop-out mechanism) needs at least 8cm of chase depth into the wall** — not always available, particularly on a shared wall backing directly onto a neighboring unit. Confirm real wall depth before specifying this fixture type; it can't be retrofitted into a wall that's too thin. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b106_mirror_d60b55af.txt|20260731_zems_b106_mirro]]]

## 2b. Bidet-Function Toilets and Similar Fixtures — Plumbing, Not Electrical

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov gives the following bidet-function plumbing guidance, via the b187/b197 source mapping in WC Source Notes.

**A bidet-function toilet is purely mechanical, driven by water pressure — it needs no electrical supply**, despite the motorized-looking wash-arm mechanism sometimes creating that impression. **What it does need is both a hot and a cold water connection**, plumbed essentially like a standard hygienic shower/bidet spray — plan the rough-in accordingly regardless of whether an electrical run seems more obviously necessary. Pricing for these units has risen sharply since 2021 and shouldn't be assumed comparable to older cited figures. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b187_uncomfortable_f947f90a.txt|20260731_zems_b187_uncom]]]

## 2c. Wall-Hung Toilet Installation Height (added 2026-08-17)

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov recommends the 40cm installation method, with the independent 40cm/42cm corroboration recorded in the linked notes.

**Target installation height for a wall-hung toilet: 40cm**, matching the long-standardized height of an ordinary floor-mounted toilet — per Zemskov/Zemstandart, `single-account`. [source: `11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_toilet_installation_height_035.md`]

**Independently corroborated, with an added seat-height detail (2026-08-19)**: a separate source states the same 40cm figure for the bowl rim itself (without a seat), and adds that the *seat* surface sits 42cm from the floor once fitted — the 2cm difference is the seat's own thickness. Real defect flagged in that source: an unusually tall installation left a shorter-statured occupant's feet not reaching the floor while seated. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_o7lGqaUuGm0_fake_renovation_scam_walkthrough_214|note]]]

- **Don't customize the height per household member, even though a hidden-frame system physically supports a wide adjustable range.** Stated reasoning: people use toilets outside their home constantly (work, restaurants, other homes) — nearly all standard-height, floor-mounted — so a home unit tuned to one person's comfort (or averaged across a multi-generational household with very different heights) ends up *less* familiar/comfortable than simply matching the universal standard. The toilet itself also only arrives on-site near the very end of the renovation (the frame is encased in drywall+tile early, with a disposable temporary toilet used by the crew meanwhile), making mid-project height-fitting to a specific person impractical anyway. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_toilet_installation_height_035|WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_to]]]
- **Two common causes push the installed height above the 40cm target, each with its own remedy — try both before resorting to a podium**: (1) toilet distance from the soil stack (farther away needs a higher outlet to maintain drain slope) → **remedy: position the toilet as close to the stack as the layout allows**; (2) the stack's own connection point set too high to begin with → **remedy: physically lower the stack's drain tie-in point**. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_toilet_installation_height_035|WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_to]]]
- **If height still can't be brought down to 40cm, build a podium**: recommended height **~47cm**, with **~45cm as the practical minimum** (45cm is workable but a bit uncomfortable; 47cm is comfortable). A too-high toilet is a real functional problem, not just cosmetic — it can prevent proper relaxation/full evacuation — but a podium itself is a daily-habituation cost (stepping up), so treat it as the fallback once the two remedies above are exhausted, not the default fix. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_toilet_installation_height_035|WjbLMKON_s8_wall_hung_to]]]

## 3a. Sewage Stack Rerouting — a Top-Floor-Only Option

`channel not confirmed` — the following stack-rerouting claim is mapped to archive-only b172 material without a directly cited extraction-note `channel:` field.

**On a top-floor apartment specifically (nothing flushes from above), the stack can sometimes be rerouted with a horizontal jog** — offset out from its default vertical line, then brought back to the same axis — to free up room for a properly laid-out plumbing utility area. **Never do this on any floor below the top one**: anything flushed from above (including, worst case, solid waste from a clog or backup) would fall onto a horizontally-offset section from height, a real risk that doesn't exist when there's nothing above to fall from. A narrow-use-case technique, but worth knowing if a top-floor unit's stack placement is otherwise blocking a good layout. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b172_toiletwild_5ab01d02.txt|20260731_zems_b172_toile]]]

## 3b. Wide-Format Tile Complicates the Hidden-Access-Tile Technique — a Workaround

`channel not confirmed` — the following wide-format-tile workaround is mapped to archive-only b172 material without a directly cited extraction-note `channel:` field.

The hidden emergency-access technique in [[07_Bathroom/analysis/Bathtub_and_Shower|Bathroom: Bathtub & Shower Selection]] (unglued, film-backed tiles that pop out with a suction cup) assumes at least two smaller tiles can be left unglued near the access point. **A single large-format tile (e.g. 60×120cm or bigger) can't be handled the same way** — there's only one tile covering that whole span, not two to selectively leave unglued. Two workarounds: (1) cut an access flap/corner out of the single large tile and seal its edge with silicone, matching the same removable-panel logic; or (2) install a proper concealed hatch — but a hatch consumes some usable space around its own perimeter and costs meaningfully more for a quality unit, a real cost/space tradeoff against option (1). **A cheaper, durable middle option for a plumbing utility box specifically (not a bathtub apron)**: a small two-door furniture-style insert set flush with the surrounding tile plane — inexpensive, long-lasting, and only consumes ~2-3cm of depth at the opening while leaving the rest of the cabinet interior fully accessible.

## 4a. A Few More Corroborated Planning Constants

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov supplies the planning constants mapped to b149/b159/b187/b197; the separately cited 20260804 fan-switch claim remains `channel not confirmed`.

- **Minimum/comfortable bathtub width**: 700mm is workable but described as uncomfortable for most people; **800mm is the practical default**, sizing up to 900mm for larger-bodied users — corroborated consistently across multiple videos from this same channel. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b122_sink_976a4002.txt|20260731_zems_b122_sink]]]
- **Add a ~50mm safety margin beyond a sink or tub's nominal width** when setting the room dimension around it — manufacturers' actual product dimensions commonly run slightly over their nominal size (e.g. an "80cm" sink might really be 82cm); without the margin, the fixture can end up pressed unevenly against a side wall. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b122_sink_976a4002.txt|20260731_zems_b122_sink]]]
- **Heated-floor thermostat placement convention**: always mount it outside the bathroom/WC (in the corridor), at a comfortable eye-level height, on the same side of the doorway as the room's light switch — both aligned on a shared vertical axis roughly 15cm from the edge of the door opening. A specific, checkable convention worth matching for a consistent, predictable electrical layout across rooms. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b122_sink_976a4002.txt|20260731_zems_b122_sink]]]
- **Standard interior door opening height used throughout this source's own projects: 2070mm** — a reasonable default datum if no other constraint dictates otherwise. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks_03d46838.txt|20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks]]]
- **A utility box's front wall facing a riser/stack should be built no thinner than ~50mm** — thinner risks piercing the pipe itself when later anchoring a sink or fixture to the box face. Don't build the box depth flush/tight against the actual pipe route either — leaving zero clearance creates unusable dead space and can still prevent a fixture from being properly anchored to the box. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks_03d46838.txt|20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks]]]
- **A revision hatch for a toilet-installation utility box (water-treatment node, filters, meters) is commonly sized ~600mm wide × 900mm tall** — a reasonable default to plan around absent a more specific requirement. [source: [[_Archive/processed_sources/20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks_03d46838.txt|20260731_zems_b149_bathnearstacks]]]
- **Exhaust-fan switch placement, WC-specific detail (added 2026-08-04)**: locate the switch near the toilet itself, not elsewhere in the room — keeps it within immediate reach for prompt odor control right when it's needed. Extends [[12_Engineering_and_Systems/analysis/Switches_and_Controls|Electrical: Switches & Controls]]'s existing rule that a bathroom/WC fan switch belongs inside the room, not the hallway, with a specific in-room position. `single-account`. [source: `_Archive/processed_sources/20260804_apartment_tour_tips_compilation_e2e466ad.txt`]

## 4b. Bathroom/WC Floor Level — a Leak-Containment Mechanism, Not Just a Preference (added 2026-08-19)

Zemstandart / Alexey Zemskov makes both floor-level claims, via `YT_EnSpVCSUiqg...` and `YT_ROv1BuBfECU...`.

> [!NOTE]
> Two corroborating Category 5 sources — a general critique establishing the requirement, and a dense on-site QC walkthrough giving the underlying mechanism. [sources: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_EnSpVCSUiqg_designer_lost_it_apartment_307|note 1]], [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_ROv1BuBfECU_designer_disaster_35m_281|note 2]]]

- **A bathroom/WC floor should be flush (no threshold) with the rest of the apartment's floor level, and this is only legally/technically permitted when the entire apartment has continuous (single-contour) waterproofing** — not just the wet room itself. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_EnSpVCSUiqg_designer_lost_it_apartment_307|EnSpVCSUiqg_designer_los]]]
- **⚠️ A bathroom floor must never be raised *above* the surrounding apartment floor level — a genuinely important leak-containment mechanism, not just an accessibility preference.** A documented defect found the bathroom floor 3cm *higher* than the rest of the apartment (extra screed poured specifically inside the bathroom) — the source calls this backwards: **if not building flush with full-apartment waterproofing, the correct direction is a shallow recessed basin (lower, not higher), so a leak stays contained and can be manually bailed toward the drain/toilet rather than flowing out into the rest of the unit.** [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_ROv1BuBfECU_designer_disaster_35m_281|ROv1BuBfECU_designer_dis]]]
- **Two valid methods only, never mixed at the same threshold**: (1) a raised-threshold system with an angled corner molding positioned directly under the door leaf, or (2) a flush, single-level transition (requiring whole-apartment waterproofing per the rule above) with a plain straight seam, also positioned under the door leaf. **Two corner moldings must never coexist at the same transition** — a documented defect had both types present together, explicitly flagged as a "never do this." [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_ROv1BuBfECU_designer_disaster_35m_281|ROv1BuBfECU_designer_dis]]]

## 4a. Toilet-Bowl Forced Ventilation Fan — a Distinct Mechanism from Room Ventilation (Петришин-Строй, added 2026-08-24, Round 10)

A ventilation fan built into the toilet bowl rim (a premium-brand upcoming
product at time of filming, price not yet set) captures odor **at the
source inside the bowl** before it disperses into the room air, rather
than relying on a room-level exhaust fan to remove already-dispersed odor
after the fact. Wired to share a switch with the room's light fixture for
simultaneous activation; can vent to either the building's existing
ventilation duct or a dedicated outside vent. `single-account`,
brand-specific, region unresolved. [source: [[11_Budget_and_Planning/_supporting/knowledge/sources/YT_zZ_1iOXL_wA_petrishin_installation_frame_shower_furniture_expo|zZ_1iOXL_wA]]]
